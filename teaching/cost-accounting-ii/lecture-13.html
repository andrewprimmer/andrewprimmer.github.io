Microteach 13.1 · 1:15Capital budgeting and the time value of money

Capital budgeting evaluates long-term investments; their cash flows must be discounted for the time value of money.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 22.

Read the script

A robotic welding cell costs $300,000 today and saves money for five years. Is it worth it? That's capital budgeting.

Capital budgeting has six stages: identify projects that fit the strategy, search for alternatives, gather information and predict cash flows, select projects, arrange financing, and implement, control and later audit the results.

Long-term decisions must recognize the time value of money: a dollar today can be invested to earn a return, so it's worth more than a dollar received in five years.

The required rate of return, also called the discount rate or hurdle rate, is the minimum return an investment must earn. Tallgrass uses its ten percent cost of capital.

The robotic frame-welding cell costs $300,000 today. It will save $90,000 of cash a year, mostly labor, for five years, and can be sold for $20,000 at the end.

Quick check. Which stage compares projects using net present value? Pause and pick.

It's C, the selection stage.

Microteach 13.2 · 1:25Net present value

NPV = present value of future cash flows − investment; accept projects with NPV ≥ 0.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 22.

Read the script

Net present value converts every future cash flow into today's dollars, then subtracts the investment.

Net present value is the present value of all expected cash flows, in and out, discounted at the required rate of return. If NPV is zero or positive, the project earns at least the required return.

Five years of $90,000 savings, discounted at ten percent using the annuity factor of three point seven nine one, are worth $341,190 today. The $20,000 disposal value, times point six two one, is worth $12,420. Less the $300,000 investment, NPV is $53,610. Accept.

NPV is measured in dollars: the project adds about $53,600 of value to Tallgrass in today's money. That makes it the best measure for comparing projects.

The higher the required rate, the lower the NPV. Riskier projects should be discounted at higher rates.

Quick check. The present value of inflows is four hundred eighty thousand, and the investment five hundred thousand. Decision? Pause and pick.

It's B. NPV is negative $20,000: reject.

Microteach 13.3 · 1:18Internal rate of return

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV zero; accept if IRR exceeds the required rate.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 22.

Read the script

Many managers prefer a percentage. The internal rate of return gives one.

The internal rate of return is the discount rate at which the present value of expected cash inflows equals the investment: the rate that makes NPV zero.

At ten percent, NPV is about $53,600. At sixteen percent, it's still slightly positive. At seventeen, slightly negative. So the IRR is about sixteen point six percent. Spreadsheets find it exactly.

The rule: accept if the IRR exceeds the required rate of return. Sixteen point six percent beats ten percent.

But IRR can mislead when ranking projects of different sizes or timing. A tiny project with a forty percent IRR may add less value than a big one at twenty percent. When they conflict, NPV wins.

Quick check. Project A has an IRR of twenty-five percent and NPV of ten thousand. Project B, eighteen percent and sixty thousand. Only one can go ahead. Which? Pause and pick.

It's B. NPV measures the value added.

Microteach 13.4 · 1:15Payback and accrual accounting rate of return

Payback and AARR are simple and popular, but ignore the time value of money.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 22.

Read the script

Two simpler measures are still widely used. They're useful, but limited.

The payback period is the time it takes to recover the investment from net cash inflows. For the welding cell: $300,000 divided by $90,000 a year, three point three years.

The accrual accounting rate of return uses accounting income. Annual savings of $90,000 less depreciation of $56,000 raise operating income by $34,000. Divided by the $300,000 investment, that's eleven point three percent.

Payback is simple and shows how quickly cash comes back, a rough measure of risk, but ignores everything after payback and the time value of money. AARR matches how managers are evaluated, on accounting income, but also ignores the time value of money.

Use payback and AARR as supplements to NPV, never as replacements.

Quick check. Investment a hundred twenty thousand; annual cash inflows thirty thousand. Payback period? Pause and pick.

It's B, four years.

Microteach 13.5 · 1:30Income taxes and relevant cash flows

Use after-tax cash flows; depreciation matters only through its tax shield.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 22.

Read the script

Taxes change the numbers. Depreciation isn't a cash flow, but it saves tax, and that saving is.

Relevant cash flows include the initial investment, plus any working capital needed; operating cash flows after tax; the tax saved by depreciation; and at the end, the after-tax disposal value and any working capital recovered.

At a twenty-five percent tax rate, the $90,000 of savings is worth $67,500 after tax. Straight-line tax depreciation of $60,000 a year saves $15,000 of tax. That's $82,500 a year. The disposal value, fully taxed, is worth $15,000. After tax, NPV falls to about $22,000: still positive.

Faster tax depreciation raises NPV by bringing tax savings forward. With one hundred percent bonus depreciation, covered in Federal Tax I, Tallgrass would save $75,000 of tax right away, raising NPV to about $40,000.

And as always, sunk costs, like the consultant's feasibility study last year, stay out of the analysis.

Quick check. Annual tax depreciation forty thousand dollars; tax rate twenty-five percent. Annual tax shield? Pause and pick.

It's B, $10,000.

Microteach 13.6 · 1:23After the decision

Post-investment audits check predictions; evaluation by early-year accounting income can block good projects.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 22.

Read the script

Approving the project isn't the end. Two things matter afterwards: checking the predictions, and how managers are judged.

A post-investment audit compares the project's actual cash flows and performance with the predictions used to approve it. It improves future forecasts and discourages managers from inflating them.

After a year, the welding cell saved $74,000, not $90,000. The audit finds that programming the robot took four months longer than planned. Savings are now on track, and future proposals will allow more start-up time.

The behavioral trap: a manager judged on year-one accounting income or return on investment may reject a positive-NPV project, because depreciation and start-up costs make early profits look poor. Align evaluation with the NPV decision, or judge over several years.

Finally, some benefits are hard to quantify: options to expand later, learning, better quality. They shouldn't be ignored just because they're hard to put in a spreadsheet.

Quick check. Why might a manager reject a positive-NPV project? Pause and pick.

It's B. Short-term evaluation can conflict with long-term value.

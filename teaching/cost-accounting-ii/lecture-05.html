Microteach 5.1 · 1:12Responsibility centers

Cost, revenue, profit and investment centers hold managers accountable for what they control.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 6 and 15.

Read the script

A budget only works if someone is responsible for each part of it. That's responsibility accounting.

A cost center manager is accountable for costs, like the assembly plant. A revenue center for revenues, like the dealer sales team. A profit center for both, like the parts and accessories business. An investment center for profit and the assets used to earn it.

The key is matching measures to authority. The assembly manager decides how bikes are built, not their price, so she's judged on cost, not profit.

Each type also creates temptations. A cost center manager might cut quality to save money. A revenue center might discount heavily to hit sales targets. Good systems add measures to balance them.

Measuring investment centers, with return on investment and residual income, comes in Lecture 14.

Quick check. The online store manager sets prices, buys stock and controls costs, but not the warehouse investment. What type of center is that? Pause and pick.

It's C, a profit center.

Microteach 5.2 · 1:07Controllability and performance reports

Performance reports should separate controllable items and be used to learn, not to blame.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 6 and 15.

Read the script

Should a manager be blamed for a cost she can't control? Usually not. But it's not that simple.

Controllability is the degree of influence a manager has over an item. Few costs are fully controllable by one person. Battery prices, for example, depend on suppliers and world markets.

The assembly plant's March report shows labor over budget and supplies under budget. It also shows a head-office IT allocation over budget, which the plant manager can't influence. Reports should separate it.

A better question than 'who controls it?' is 'who knows the most about it?' The purchasing manager can't set battery prices, but she should explain them and look for alternatives.

Performance reports work best as information for learning. Used only to blame, they encourage padding budgets and hiding problems.

Quick check. Which item is least controllable by the assembly manager? Pause and pick.

It's C. Head-office IT costs are set elsewhere and allocated in.

Microteach 5.3 · 1:32The sales-volume variance

Sales-volume variance = (actual units − budgeted units) × budgeted contribution margin per unit.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 6 and 15.

Read the script

Tallgrass sold fewer bikes than planned. How much did that cost? The sales-volume variance answers that.

The budget was nine hundred Trail bikes, with a contribution margin of $900 each, and three hundred Cargo bikes at $1,500. Actual sales were eight hundred eighty Trail bikes and two hundred twenty Cargo bikes.

The sales-volume variance is actual units minus budgeted units, times the budgeted contribution margin per unit. Using budgeted margins isolates the effect of volume alone.

Trail bikes: twenty short, times $900, $18,000 unfavorable. Cargo bikes: eighty short, times $1,500, $120,000 unfavorable. In total, selling fewer bikes cost $138,000 of contribution margin.

Recall from ACCT four thirteen: the static budget variance splits into a flexible-budget variance, about prices and costs, and this sales-volume variance. Now we dig into the volume part.

Quick check. Budget five hundred units, actual five forty, budgeted contribution margin eighty dollars. What's the sales-volume variance? Pause and pick.

It's B. Forty extra units times $80 is $3,200 favorable.

Microteach 5.4 · 1:27Sales-mix and sales-quantity variances

The sales-volume variance splits into a mix effect and a quantity effect.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 6 and 15.

Read the script

Tallgrass sold fewer bikes overall, and a less profitable mix. Two variances separate those effects.

The budgeted mix was seventy-five percent Trail and twenty-five percent Cargo. The actual mix was eighty and twenty. At the budgeted mix, the average contribution margin per bike is $1,050.

The sales-mix variance takes total actual units times the change in mix times budgeted margin. Selling more Trail bikes adds $49,500. Selling fewer Cargo bikes costs $82,500. The shift toward the cheaper bike cost $33,000.

The sales-quantity variance is the change in total units times the budgeted average margin: a hundred fewer bikes times $1,050, $105,000 unfavorable. Mix plus quantity gives the $138,000 sales-volume variance.

These point to different problems: the mix variance says customers chose the cheaper bike; the quantity variance says fewer bikes were sold overall.

Quick check. Total units hit budget, but the mix shifted toward the high-margin product. Which variance is favorable? Pause and pick.

It's B, sales mix. The quantity variance is zero.

Microteach 5.5 · 1:29Market-share and market-size variances

The sales-quantity variance splits into market-share and market-size effects.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 6 and 15.

Read the script

Did Tallgrass sell fewer bikes because the market shrank, or because competitors took share? Two more variances tell us.

Ana budgeted a regional market of twenty-four thousand e-bikes with a five percent share. The market actually grew to twenty-five thousand, but Tallgrass's share fell to four point four percent.

The market-share variance is actual market size times the change in share, times budgeted average margin: $157,500 unfavorable. The market-size variance is the change in market size times budgeted share and margin: $52,500 favorable. Together, the $105,000.

This changes the story. Sales didn't fall because of a weak market; the market grew. Tallgrass lost share to competitors, which is a problem for marketing and product strategy.

These variances are only as good as the market-size data, which comes from industry associations, registrations or surveys.

Quick check. The market fell from ten thousand to nine thousand units, budgeted share ten percent, budgeted margin two hundred dollars. What's the market-size variance? Pause and pick.

It's A. A thousand fewer units times ten percent times $200: $20,000 unfavorable.

Microteach 5.6 · 1:15Using sales variances

Layered variances point to causes, but managers must still investigate.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 6 and 15.

Read the script

We've broken one disappointing number into four. Now, what should Ana tell the CEO?

The sales-volume variance of $138,000 splits into a mix effect of $33,000 and a quantity effect of $105,000. Quantity splits into a share loss of $157,500, partly offset by $52,500 from a growing market.

The variances point to questions. Why did the Cargo bike underperform? Did dealers push the cheaper Trail bike? Did a competitor launch something new? The numbers don't answer these; people do.

Responsibility follows. The marketing and sales teams answer for share and mix. No one at Tallgrass controls the size of the market.

Then act: perhaps dealer incentives for Cargo bikes, a review of competitors' models, or a product update. And next year's budget should use better market forecasts.

Quick check. Which variance is least controllable by marketing? Pause and pick.

It's C. The size of the regional market depends on the economy and consumer trends.

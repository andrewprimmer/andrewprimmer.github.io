Microteach 7.1 · 1:08What is relevant?

Relevant costs and revenues are expected future amounts that differ among the alternatives.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 12.

Read the script

Every decision has a pile of numbers attached. Most of them don't matter. Here's how to find the ones that do.

Relevant costs and revenues are expected future amounts that differ among the alternatives being considered. Both tests must be met: future, and different.

The cost of batteries for extra bikes is relevant: it's future and it changes. Money already spent on tooling is a sunk cost: irrelevant. The factory lease, if it's paid either way, doesn't differ: also irrelevant.

A sunk cost is a past cost that nothing you decide can change. It's the most common trap in decision making.

Numbers aren't everything. Qualitative factors, like supplier reliability, quality, employee morale and customer relationships, must be weighed too.

Quick check. Tallgrass paid forty thousand dollars last year for a prototype. Is that relevant to deciding whether to launch? Pause and pick.

It's B. It's sunk: spent whatever Tallgrass decides now.

Microteach 7.2 · 1:28One-time special orders

With idle capacity, accept a special order if its price exceeds the incremental costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 12.

Read the script

A Wisconsin bike chain offers to buy a hundred fifty bikes, at a price below Tallgrass's full cost. Should Ana say yes?

The chain offers $1,800 a bike for a hundred fifty bikes. Tallgrass's full absorption cost is $1,600 a bike, but that includes $300 of fixed overhead. Variable manufacturing cost is $1,300, and shipping adds fifty dollars, with no dealer commission.

Tallgrass has idle capacity, so fixed costs won't change. Relevant revenue is $270,000 and relevant costs $202,500. Accepting adds $67,500 to operating income.

Using the $1,600 full cost would have made the order look barely worthwhile. But the fixed overhead is paid whether or not Tallgrass accepts.

Before accepting: make sure capacity really is idle; consider whether regular dealers will hear about the price and demand the same; and check that the pricing is lawful.

Quick check. With idle capacity and variable cost of thirteen fifty a bike, what's the lowest price that doesn't reduce income? Pause and pick.

It's B, $1,350, in the short run.

Microteach 7.3 · 1:29Make or buy, and opportunity cost

Compare avoidable costs of making with the purchase price, including the opportunity cost of the capacity.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 12.

Read the script

Tallgrass makes its own bike frames. A supplier offers them for less. Should Tallgrass outsource?

Each frame costs $210 in variable costs, plus $70 of allocated fixed overhead: $280 in total. A supplier offers $240.

Only some fixed overhead goes away if Tallgrass buys: the frame supervisor's $25,000 salary. Relevant cost to make is $281,200. Buying costs $292,800. Making is $11,600 cheaper.

But what else could the frame shop do? Opportunity cost is the contribution given up by not using a resource in its next-best alternative.

If the frame shop could instead make battery cases, saving $40,000 a year on purchases, making frames costs that $40,000 too. Now buying frames is $28,400 better.

Quick check. Relevant cost to make is fifty thousand, to buy fifty-six thousand, and the freed space could earn ten thousand in rent. What's the decision? Pause and pick.

It's B. Making really costs $60,000 including the forgone rent, so buying saves $4,000.

Microteach 7.4 · 1:24Product mix under a constraint

When a resource is scarce, rank products by contribution margin per unit of that resource.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 12.

Read the script

Assembly hours are Tallgrass's bottleneck. With limited hours, which bike should it build first?

A Cargo bike contributes $1,500, more than a Trail bike's $900. But it takes twelve assembly hours, twice as long. Per hour of the scarce resource, the Trail bike earns $150 and the Cargo $125.

When a resource is constrained, the rule is: maximize contribution margin per unit of the constrained resource, not per product.

With nine thousand assembly hours, Tallgrass first meets all Trail demand, a thousand bikes using six thousand hours. The remaining three thousand hours build two hundred fifty Cargo bikes. Total contribution: $1,275,000.

Longer term, managers should relieve the bottleneck: overtime, a second shift, or outsourcing some assembly, if the extra contribution exceeds the cost.

Quick check. Product X contributes sixty dollars in two machine hours; Y ninety dollars in four. Machine hours are scarce. Which first? Pause and pick.

It's A. X earns $30 per machine hour, Y only $22.50.

Microteach 7.5 · 1:30Keeping or dropping a product line

Drop a line only if the avoidable costs exceed the contribution margin lost.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 12.

Read the script

Tallgrass's kids' balance bikes show a loss every year. Should it drop the line? Not so fast.

Balance bikes bring in $120,000 of revenue and $36,000 of contribution margin. Fixed costs charged to the line are $50,000: $20,000 that would disappear if the line were dropped, and $30,000 of allocated plant and admin costs that wouldn't. The line reports a $14,000 loss.

If Tallgrass drops the line, it loses $36,000 of contribution and saves only $20,000 of avoidable costs. Operating income falls by $16,000. The $30,000 of allocated costs simply land on other products.

The lesson: allocated, unavoidable costs make segments look less profitable than they are. Decisions turn on avoidable costs.

And consider spillovers. Do parents who buy a balance bike come back for an e-bike? Losing the line could cost more than its own contribution.

Quick check. A line has fifty thousand of contribution, sixty thousand of avoidable fixed costs and twenty-five thousand allocated. Should it be dropped? Pause and pick.

It's A. Dropping saves $60,000 and loses $50,000: income rises $10,000.

Microteach 7.6 · 1:30Replacing equipment

Book value of old equipment is sunk; but performance measures can make managers act as if it isn't.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 12.

Read the script

Replace a three-year-old painting machine with a better one? The book value of the old machine shouldn't matter. But it often does.

The old machine has a book value of $60,000 but could be sold for only $10,000. It costs $50,000 a year to run. A new machine costs $80,000 and $20,000 a year to run. Both would last three more years.

Ignoring the time value of money, keeping costs $150,000. Replacing costs $80,000, less $10,000 from selling the old machine, plus $60,000 to run: $130,000. Replacing saves $20,000. The $60,000 book value is sunk and plays no part.

But if the plant manager replaces it, the books show a $50,000 loss on disposal this year, and year-one income falls. If her bonus depends on this year's income, she may keep the old machine.

This conflict between the decision model and the performance evaluation model is common. The fix is to evaluate managers over the life of their decisions, or on the decision itself, not just one year's accounting income.

Quick check. Which is relevant? Pause and pick.

It's C. The disposal value is future cash that differs between the options. The others are sunk.

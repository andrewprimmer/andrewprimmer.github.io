Microteach 10.1 · 1:09Choosing a strategy

Companies compete by differentiation or cost leadership; accounting should measure progress on the chosen strategy.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Before you can measure performance, you need to know what you're trying to achieve. That's strategy.

A company can compete through product differentiation, offering something customers value and will pay a premium for, or through cost leadership, delivering acceptable products at the lowest cost.

Michael Porter's five forces shape which strategy can work: rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, and the bargaining power of customers and suppliers. Cheap imported e-bikes and powerful battery suppliers both pressure Tallgrass.

Tallgrass chooses differentiation: rugged bikes built in Minnesota, backed by local dealer service and a long warranty, priced above imports.

The accounting system should then measure progress on that strategy. A differentiator should track quality, customer loyalty and price premiums, not just cost per bike.

Quick check. A company sells basic e-bikes at the lowest price through high volume. What's its strategy? Pause and pick.

It's B, cost leadership.

Microteach 10.2 · 1:14The balanced scorecard

The balanced scorecard links financial, customer, internal process and learning-and-growth measures to strategy.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Financial results tell you what happened, too late to change it. The balanced scorecard adds measures that lead to future results.

The balanced scorecard, developed by Robert Kaplan and David Norton in the 1990s, translates strategy into a set of performance measures across four perspectives.

Financial: did the strategy create value for shareholders? Customer: are we winning in our target market? Internal business process: what operations must we excel at? Learning and growth: do we have the people, systems and culture to improve?

The perspectives link by cause and effect. Better-trained assemblers lead to fewer defects and faster delivery, which lead to loyal customers, which lead to higher margins. Drawn out, this is a strategy map.

The balance is between financial and nonfinancial measures, internal and external, and leading indicators that predict results versus lagging indicators that report them.

Quick check. Hours of training per assembler belongs to which perspective? Pause and pick.

It's D, learning and growth.

Microteach 10.3 · 1:18Tallgrass's scorecard

Each measure needs a target and must connect to the strategy.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Here's the scorecard Ana built to track Tallgrass's differentiation strategy.

Financial: grow operating income by ten percent. Customer: hold a five percent share of the regional market. Internal process: fewer than three warranty claims per hundred bikes. Learning and growth: ninety percent of assemblers certified on the new motor system.

Results: operating income grew eight percent, below target. Market share slipped to four point four percent. But warranty claims fell to two point six, and ninety-two percent of assemblers are certified.

Read the scorecard as a story. Quality improved, yet share fell. Either the effect hasn't reached customers yet, or quality isn't what's driving their choice. Remember the Cargo bike problem from Lecture 5: range and price may matter more.

That's the value of the scorecard: it tests the strategy's cause-and-effect assumptions. If the links don't hold, managers should rethink the strategy or the measures.

Quick check. Which is a leading indicator of financial results? Pause and pick.

It's B. Dealer satisfaction should predict future sales and margins.

Microteach 10.4 · 1:25Growth, price recovery and productivity

Splitting the change in operating income shows whether it came from growth, pricing power or efficiency.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Operating income rose. But why? Strategic analysis splits the change into growth, price recovery and productivity.

In 2026, Tallgrass sold a thousand bikes at $2,300, with material kits at $1,000 and seven assembly hours a bike at twenty-eight dollars. In 2027, eleven hundred bikes at $2,400, kits at $1,040, and assembly took only six hours a bike, at thirty dollars an hour.

The growth component, a hundred more bikes at 2026 prices and productivity, added $110,400. The price-recovery component, the price rising faster than input prices, added $50,600. The productivity component, eleven hundred fewer assembly hours than 2026 efficiency would need, at thirty dollars, added $33,000.

For a differentiator, price recovery is the key test: Tallgrass raised prices by more than its input costs rose, a sign customers value the brand.

A cost leader would look for most of its gains in productivity: producing the same output with fewer inputs.

Quick check. Which component shows whether Tallgrass can pass higher costs on to customers? Pause and pick.

It's B, price recovery.

Microteach 10.5 · 1:18Engineered and discretionary costs

Engineered costs have a clear input–output link; discretionary costs don't, so they're managed by judgment.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Some costs can be precisely linked to output. Others depend on management judgment. They're managed differently.

Engineered costs have a clear cause-and-effect link to output: materials, assembly labor, machine time. Discretionary costs, like advertising, research and training, have no measurable link; managers decide how much to spend.

Take assembly. Twelve workers supply twenty-one thousand six hundred hours a year. Three thousand bikes at six hours need eighteen thousand. Thirty-six hundred hours, two workers' worth, are unused.

Unused engineered capacity can be filled by growing sales, redeployed to other work, or reduced. Downsizing should be done carefully, keeping the skills a differentiator needs.

Discretionary costs are the easiest to cut when profits are tight, because the harm is delayed. Cutting R&D or training to hit this year's target can damage a differentiator's future. The scorecard's learning and growth measures help guard against that.

Quick check. Which is a discretionary cost? Pause and pick.

It's C. There's no precise link between training spending and output.

Microteach 10.6 · 1:03Making scorecards work

Keep scorecards short, linked to strategy and to rewards; add sustainability where it matters.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Many scorecards fail. Here's how to make one work.

Common pitfalls: too many measures, so nobody can focus; measures with no cause-and-effect link to strategy; no targets or no link to rewards; and dropping the nonfinancial measures as soon as profits fall.

A rule of thumb: no more than fifteen to twenty-five measures, three to six per perspective, each tied to the strategy map.

Many companies now add environmental, social and governance measures. For Tallgrass: battery recycling rate, workplace injuries, and suppliers' compliance with its code of conduct, important given risks in battery supply chains.

And review the scorecard regularly. When strategy changes, the measures must change too.

Quick check. A scorecard has sixty measures. What's the main risk? Pause and pick.

It's B: with so many measures, managers can't focus on what drives the strategy.

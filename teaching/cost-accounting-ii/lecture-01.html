Microteach 1.1 · 1:26Cost functions and the relevant range

A linear cost function, y = a + bX, splits a cost into fixed and variable parts within a relevant range.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Planning starts with prediction. To predict a cost, you need to know how it behaves when activity changes.

This semester we'll follow Tallgrass Cycles, which builds electric bikes in Marshall, Minnesota. Its CFO, Ana Vang, uses cost accounting to plan, to evaluate performance and to make decisions.

A linear cost function is written y equals a plus b X. Y is total cost. A is the fixed part, which doesn't change with activity. B is the variable cost per unit of the cost driver, X.

A variable cost, like battery packs at $310 a bike, rises in proportion to the driver. A fixed cost, like the $18,000 monthly factory lease, doesn't. A mixed cost, like power, has both: a $2,000 base plus four dollars per machine hour.

These relationships hold only within the relevant range. If Tallgrass doubled output, it would need a second lease and the fixed cost would jump. Predictions outside the range are risky.

Quick check. Power cost is two thousand dollars plus four dollars per machine hour. What's the cost for thirty-five hundred hours? Pause and pick.

It's B. $2,000 plus $14,000 is $16,000.

Microteach 1.2 · 1:11Four ways to estimate a cost

Costs can be estimated by engineering studies, conferences, account analysis or quantitative analysis of past data.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

How does Ana find the a and the b? There are four approaches, from expert judgment to statistics.

The industrial engineering method studies inputs physically, like timing assembly steps. The conference method pools experts' views. Account analysis classifies each ledger account as fixed, variable or mixed. Quantitative analysis fits a line to past cost and activity data.

Quantitative analysis follows six steps: choose the cost to explain; pick a likely driver; collect data on both; plot them; estimate the cost function; and evaluate whether the driver works.

Always plot the data first. A scatter plot shows outliers, like a month with a one-off repair, and whether the relationship really looks linear.

Watch the data. A repair recorded in the month after the work, or overtime paid in a later period, will weaken the relationship.

Quick check. Ana labels each overhead account in the ledger fixed or variable. Which method is this? Pause and pick.

It's C, account analysis.

Microteach 1.3 · 1:17The high-low method

The high-low method draws a line through the highest and lowest activity points.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

The quickest way to split a mixed cost is the high-low method. It uses just two data points.

Ana has twelve months of maintenance costs. The month with the most machine hours, five thousand, cost $30,000. The month with the fewest, two thousand hours, cost $18,000. The difference is three thousand hours and $12,000.

The variable cost per hour is the change in cost divided by the change in hours: four dollars. Plug back into either point: $30,000 minus four times five thousand gives a fixed cost of $10,000.

Note: you choose the points with the highest and lowest activity, not the highest and lowest cost.

The weakness is obvious: the line depends on two points. If either was an unusual month, the estimate is distorted. Regression uses every point.

Quick check. Using ten thousand dollars plus four dollars an hour, predict maintenance for forty-two hundred hours. Pause and pick.

It's B. $10,000 plus $16,800 is $26,800.

Microteach 1.4 · 1:16Regression analysis

Regression fits the line that minimizes squared errors; r-squared measures how much variation it explains.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Regression uses all the data, not two points. Spreadsheets do the math. You need to read the output.

Simple regression finds the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances between the data points and the line.

Ana's spreadsheet gives an intercept of $9,200 and a slope of $4.25 per machine hour. R-squared is point eight six. And the t-value on the slope is seven point eight.

R-squared means machine hours explain eighty-six percent of the month-to-month variation in maintenance. A t-value above about two means the slope is significantly different from zero. Machine hours are a good driver.

But a strong fit isn't enough. The driver must plausibly cause the cost. Ice cream sales might correlate with maintenance in summer, but they don't cause it.

Quick check. Using nine thousand two hundred plus four twenty-five per hour, predict maintenance at four thousand hours. Pause and pick.

It's B. $9,200 plus $17,000 is $26,200.

Microteach 1.5 · 1:16Choosing a cost driver

Choose drivers by economic plausibility, goodness of fit and the slope of the regression line.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Machine hours or number of setups? Ana must pick the driver that best explains overhead. Here are the criteria.

Three criteria. Economic plausibility: is there a sensible cause-and-effect link? Goodness of fit: how much variation does it explain? And the slope: a relatively steep, significant slope means the driver matters.

Ana compares three drivers for maintenance. Machine hours have an r-squared of point eight six and a clear causal link. Labor hours fit poorly, and units shipped hardly at all. Machine hours win.

Some costs aren't driven by volume at all. A cost hierarchy groups them: unit-level costs vary with each bike, batch-level with each production run, product-sustaining costs with each model, and facility-sustaining costs with the plant itself.

Getting drivers right matters. Use the wrong driver and some bikes look more profitable than they are.

Quick check. Setup costs for each production run are which level in the hierarchy? Pause and pick.

It's B, batch-level. They vary with the number of runs, not the number of bikes.

Microteach 1.6 · 1:22Nonlinear costs and learning curves

With an 80% learning curve, cumulative average time falls 20% each time output doubles.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Not every cost is a straight line. Step costs jump, quantity discounts bend, and workers get faster with practice.

A step cost jumps at intervals, like adding a supervisor for every forty workers. Quantity discounts bend the line downward. And learning curves make labor time per unit fall as workers gain experience.

In the cumulative average-time learning model, the average time per unit falls by a fixed percentage each time cumulative output doubles. An eighty percent curve means it falls to eighty percent.

The first cargo e-bike takes a hundred hours. With an eighty percent curve, the average for two is eighty hours, a hundred sixty total. For four, sixty-four hours average, two fifty-six total. For eight, about fifty-one hours average, four hundred ten total.

Learning matters for pricing and budgeting. If Ana prices the first units at their full cost, Tallgrass will overprice the line. Bids should reflect the expected average.

Quick check. On a ninety percent curve, the first unit takes fifty hours. What's the cumulative average for two units? Pause and pick.

It's B. Ninety percent of fifty is forty-five hours.

Microteach 8.1 · 1:12What sets a price?

Customers, competitors and costs all shape prices; their weight depends on the time horizon and market.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

How should Tallgrass price a new commuter e-bike? Three forces push and pull on every price.

Customers set the ceiling: what they'll pay. Competitors shape what's possible: if a rival sells a similar bike for less, customers will notice. And costs set the floor: in the long run, price must cover them.

Short-run pricing, like a one-off special order, can be set on incremental costs if capacity is idle. Long-run pricing must cover all costs, fixed included, and earn a return on investment.

In crowded markets, companies have little say over price: they're price-takers, and the job is to get costs below the market price. In less competitive markets, with unique products, companies have more freedom.

Even when the market sets the price, costs decide whether you can sell profitably at that price. That's the idea behind target costing.

Quick check. For a long-term price on a new model, which costs matter? Pause and pick.

It's C. In the long run, prices must cover all costs and earn a return.

Microteach 8.2 · 1:16Target price and target cost

Target cost = target price − target operating income per unit.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Instead of 'what does it cost, add a markup', target costing starts with the market price and works backwards.

Four steps: develop a product customers want; choose a target price from customers and competitors; subtract the profit you need to get a target cost; then design and engineer the product to meet that cost.

Rival commuter e-bikes sell for about $2,000. Tallgrass wants fifteen percent of price as operating income, $300, so the target full cost is $1,700. Engineering's current design would cost $1,950. There's a $250 gap to close.

The target cost covers the whole value chain, not just manufacturing: design, marketing, distribution and warranty too.

The mindset shift: the market decides the price, and costs must adjust to it, not the other way round.

Quick check. Target price eight hundred dollars; target operating income twenty percent of price. Target cost? Pause and pick.

It's A. Eight hundred less a hundred sixty is $640.

Microteach 8.3 · 1:15Value engineering

Value engineering cuts costs across the value chain while keeping what customers value.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Closing a $250 cost gap without making a worse bike takes value engineering.

Value engineering is a systematic evaluation of every part of the value chain, aiming to cut costs while still satisfying customers.

Tallgrass's cross-functional team finds savings: dual-sourcing batteries, $60; a frame with fewer welds, $80; snap-fit wiring, $50; tougher motor seals that cut warranty claims, $40; and flat-pack shipping, $30. That's $260, enough to close the gap.

The test for each feature: do customers value it enough to pay for it? Range and good brakes stay. Twelve paint colors, rarely chosen, become three.

Value engineering works best with cross-functional teams, because the biggest savings come from design changes that affect purchasing, production and service at once.

Quick check. Which change is most likely to cut cost without cutting customer value? Pause and pick.

It's B. Customers don't notice fastener types, but they do notice range, brakes and warranties.

Microteach 8.4 · 1:16Cost-plus pricing

Cost-plus pricing adds a markup to a cost base; it's simple but must be checked against the market.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Many companies still start with cost and add a markup. Here's how it works, and where it goes wrong.

Cost-plus pricing takes a cost base and adds a markup. The cost base might be variable cost, manufacturing cost or full cost; the markup is set to cover the costs not in the base and earn a return.

The Cargo bike's full cost is $3,000. A twenty percent markup gives a price of $3,600.

One way to set the markup: a target rate of return on investment. If the Cargo line uses $1,500,000 of capital and Tallgrass wants an eighteen percent return, it needs $270,000 of operating income.

Cost-plus is simple and easy to defend, and it ensures costs are covered at the expected volume. But it ignores what customers will pay and what competitors charge, and it relies on a volume estimate. Use it as a starting point, then check against the market.

Quick check. Full cost two fifty, markup thirty percent. Price? Pause and pick.

It's B, $325.

Microteach 8.5 · 1:26Life-cycle budgeting and costing

Life-cycle budgeting estimates all costs from R&D to support, so prices cover them over the product's life.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

A bike model's costs start years before the first sale and continue after the last. Life-cycle budgeting captures them all.

The product life cycle runs from initial research and development to the end of customer service and support for the last unit sold.

Tallgrass budgets the Commuter's whole life: $400,000 of R&D and design, $5,400,000 of manufacturing for four thousand bikes, $900,000 of marketing and distribution, and $300,000 of service and warranty. Seven million dollars, or $1,750 a bike.

Annual accounting spreads these costs across years and expenses R&D as incurred, so a product can look profitable each year while never earning back its development cost. Life-cycle reporting shows the whole picture.

Customers think in life-cycle terms too. A buyer compares total cost of ownership: price, charging, repairs and battery replacement. A longer-lasting battery can justify a higher price.

Quick check. Life-cycle costs are three point two million for two thousand units. What's the minimum average price over the life cycle? Pause and pick.

It's A, $1,600 a unit.

Microteach 8.6 · 1:23Price discrimination and the law

Prices can differ by customer or time when costs differ; predatory pricing, dumping and collusion are illegal.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Charging different prices to different customers can be smart, or illegal. Here's the line.

Price discrimination charges different customers different prices for the same product, based on what they'll pay, like student or fleet discounts. Peak-load pricing charges more when demand presses on capacity, like a premium for spring delivery.

But some practices are illegal. Predatory pricing sets prices below cost to drive rivals out, then raises them. Dumping sells abroad below cost or below the home price. And collusive pricing, agreeing prices with competitors, is a crime.

In the US, the Robinson-Patman Act of 1936 limits price differences between competing business buyers unless they can be justified, for example by differences in the cost of serving them. That's another reason to have good customer cost data.

If Tallgrass gives a big chain a lower price than small dealers, it should be able to show the chain costs less to serve: bigger orders, fewer deliveries, no co-op advertising.

Quick check. Two e-bike makers agree to keep prices above twenty-two hundred dollars. What is this? Pause and pick.

It's C, collusion, which is illegal.

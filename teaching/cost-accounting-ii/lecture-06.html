Microteach 6.1 · 1:21Variable vs absorption costing

Absorption costing puts fixed manufacturing overhead in inventory; variable costing expenses it.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

There are two ways to cost a bike for inventory. The only difference is fixed manufacturing overhead, but it can change profit a lot.

Both methods inventory direct materials, direct labor and variable manufacturing overhead. Absorption costing also inventories fixed manufacturing overhead. Variable costing expenses it in the period, as a period cost.

Each Trail bike has $1,300 of variable manufacturing cost. Fixed manufacturing overhead is $366,000 a year, and Tallgrass produces twelve hundred twenty bikes, so absorption costing adds $300 of fixed overhead to each bike.

So a bike in inventory is worth $1,300 under variable costing and $1,600 under absorption costing.

GAAP and tax rules require absorption costing for external reports. Many companies use variable costing internally, because it fits the contribution margin thinking of CVP.

Quick check. Under variable costing, fixed manufacturing overhead is what? Pause and pick.

It's B: expensed in the period, like a period cost.

Microteach 6.2 · 1:32Why incomes differ

Absorption income minus variable income equals the change in fixed overhead held in inventory.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Same company, same year, two different profits. The difference comes down to inventory.

Under variable costing, twelve hundred bikes give a contribution margin of $1,080,000. All $540,000 of fixed costs, including the $366,000 of fixed manufacturing overhead, are expensed. Operating income is $540,000.

Absorption costing income differs by the change in fixed manufacturing overhead held in inventory: fixed overhead in ending inventory minus fixed overhead in beginning inventory.

Tallgrass ends the year with sixty bikes, holding $18,000 of fixed overhead, and began with forty, holding $12,000. So absorption income is $6,000 higher: $546,000.

When production exceeds sales, inventory grows and absorption income is higher. When sales exceed production, it's lower. When they're equal, the two match.

Quick check. Fixed overhead is fifty dollars a unit, and inventory falls from eight hundred to five hundred units. Which income is higher, and by how much? Pause and pick.

It's B. Three hundred units times $50 of fixed overhead released from inventory: absorption income is $15,000 lower.

Microteach 6.3 · 1:19The incentive to overproduce

Under absorption costing, building inventory raises reported income, which can tempt managers.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Under absorption costing, a manager can raise profit without selling a single extra bike. Here's how.

Suppose the plant manager builds fifteen hundred bikes but sales stay at twelve hundred. Fixed overhead per bike falls to $244. Three hundred forty bikes now sit in inventory, carrying $82,960 of fixed overhead. Absorption income jumps to about $611,000, with no extra sales.

The company is worse off: it has spent cash building bikes nobody ordered, which must be stored and may go out of date. But a bonus tied to absorption income rewards it.

Ways to guard against it: evaluate managers on variable costing income; charge them for the cost of carrying inventory; set nonfinancial targets like inventory turnover; and judge performance over several years.

Under variable costing, income depends only on sales, so producing for inventory doesn't help.

Quick check. Under which method can a manager raise income simply by producing more? Pause and pick.

It's B, absorption costing, because fixed overhead is deferred in inventory.

Microteach 6.4 · 1:14Throughput costing

Throughput costing inventories only direct materials, removing almost all incentive to build inventory.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Some companies go further than variable costing. Throughput costing treats almost everything as a period cost.

Throughput costing, sometimes called super-variable costing, inventories only direct materials. Direct labor and all overhead are treated as period costs.

A Tallgrass bike in inventory is worth $1,100 under throughput costing, its direct materials. $1,300 under variable costing. And $1,600 under absorption costing.

The income statement shows throughput margin: revenue minus direct materials of the units sold. Each bike's throughput margin is $2,400 minus $1,100, or $1,300.

It reflects the view that in the short run, labor and overhead are fixed and only materials vary. It's for internal use only; GAAP doesn't allow it.

Quick check. Rank the inventory values, lowest to highest. Pause and pick.

It's B: throughput, then variable, then absorption.

Microteach 6.5 · 1:33Four capacity levels

The fixed overhead rate depends on the denominator: theoretical, practical, normal or master-budget capacity.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Fixed overhead per bike depends on the number you divide by. There are four choices, and they give very different costs.

Theoretical capacity assumes perfect, nonstop operation: eighteen thirty bikes. Practical capacity allows for maintenance and holidays: fifteen hundred. Normal capacity utilization is average demand over several years: thirteen hundred. Master-budget utilization is this year's plan: twelve twenty.

Dividing $366,000 by each gives fixed overhead per bike of $200, $244, about $282, or $300.

Many experts favor practical capacity. Products are charged only for the capacity they use, and the cost of unused capacity, here $366,000 minus twelve twenty times $244, shows up separately for managers to act on.

That's two hundred eighty bikes of spare capacity, or $68,320 of fixed cost Tallgrass is paying for but not using: a prompt to find more sales or shrink the plant.

Quick check. Fixed overhead five hundred thousand, practical capacity twenty-five thousand units, budget twenty thousand. What's the rate using practical capacity? Pause and pick.

It's B, twenty dollars a unit.

Microteach 6.6 · 1:17The downward demand spiral

Spreading fixed costs over shrinking budgeted volume raises unit costs and prices, driving demand lower.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Using budgeted volume as the denominator can trap a company in a vicious circle. It's called the downward demand spiral.

Demand falls. The same fixed costs are spread over fewer units, so reported unit cost rises. Managers raise prices to cover the higher cost. Customers buy even less, and the cycle repeats.

If Tallgrass budgets twelve twenty bikes, fixed overhead is $300 a bike. If demand drops to a thousand, it's $366. At eight hundred, $458. Cost-plus pricing would push prices up just as customers are leaving.

With practical capacity as the denominator, unit cost stays at $244 whatever demand does. The cost of idle capacity is reported separately, not loaded onto customers.

And prices should reflect what customers will pay and what competitors charge, not just a cost figure. That's Lecture 8.

Quick check. Which denominator avoids the downward demand spiral? Pause and pick.

It's B. Practical capacity doesn't change when demand falls.

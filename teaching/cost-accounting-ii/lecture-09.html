Microteach 9.1 · 1:22Why allocate costs?

Allocation serves four purposes; cause-and-effect and benefits received are the best criteria.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Allocation can feel arbitrary. But every allocation should serve a purpose and follow a defensible criterion.

Allocations serve four purposes: to inform economic decisions like pricing; to motivate managers, for example to use a support service carefully; to justify costs or compute reimbursements, as in government contracts; and to measure income and assets for external reporting.

The best criterion is cause and effect: allocate by what drives the cost. When that's not possible, benefits received: who gains most. Fairness is vague, and ability to bear, charging the most profitable units more, distorts decisions.

The purpose decides the method. For decisions, use causal allocations, or leave costs unallocated. For a government contract, follow the contract's rules.

And some costs, like the CEO's salary, have no causal link to any product or customer. For decisions, they're better left unallocated.

Quick check. Tallgrass charges its most profitable division the most head-office cost, because it can afford it. Which criterion is that? Pause and pick.

It's C, ability to bear, the weakest criterion.

Microteach 9.2 · 1:23Allocating corporate costs

Allocate corporate cost pools with causal bases, and only as far as the purpose requires.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Tallgrass has two divisions and a head office. How should head-office costs be shared?

Tallgrass's head office costs $600,000: $200,000 for human resources, $150,000 for I.T., and $250,000 for executives and corporate administration.

Ana allocates human resources by headcount: sixty employees in Bikes, fifteen in Parts. I.T. by help tickets: twelve hundred and eight hundred. Executive and admin costs have no good causal base, so for decisions she leaves them unallocated.

For other purposes, like long-run pricing or external segment reporting, she might allocate everything, for instance in proportion to revenue. Just be clear which purpose each number serves.

Allocations change behavior. Charging I.T. by help tickets encourages divisions to use the help desk sensibly, but too high a charge might stop them asking for help they need.

Quick check. HR costs two hundred thousand; Bikes has sixty employees and Parts fifteen. What's Bikes' share? Pause and pick.

It's C. Sixty of seventy-five is eighty percent: $160,000.

Microteach 9.3 · 1:07Customer revenues and price discounts

Track discounts by customer; list prices hide large differences in what customers actually pay.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Customer profitability starts with revenue. And customers rarely pay the list price.

Three Tallgrass dealers. Prairie Pedals in Sioux Falls buys in volume and gets ten percent off: $2,160 a bike. Lakes Cycle in Brainerd gets five percent. Big Sky Bikes in Bismarck pays full list price.

A price discount is a reduction from list price. Discounts may be earned through volume, negotiated by powerful buyers, or given away by salespeople to close a deal.

If a company records only net revenue, it can't see which customers get which discounts, or whether salespeople are discounting too freely. Tallgrass records list price and discount separately.

But revenue is only half the story. The other half is what each dealer costs to serve.

Quick check. List price twenty-four hundred, discount seven and a half percent. Net price? Pause and pick.

It's A, $2,220.

Microteach 9.4 · 1:09The customer cost hierarchy

Customer costs occur at output-unit, batch, customer-sustaining, channel and corporate levels.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Just as products have a cost hierarchy, so do customers.

Output unit-level costs vary with each bike sold. Batch-level costs vary with orders and deliveries. Customer-sustaining costs support a customer regardless of volume, like sales visits and co-op advertising. Channel costs support a whole channel, like dealers versus online. And corporate-sustaining costs support the whole company.

Ana's activity rates: forty dollars per order, three hundred dollars per truck delivery in Minnesota or four-fifty to the Dakotas, two-fifty per sales visit and a hundred fifty for a rush delivery.

Customer-level analysis assigns costs down to the customer-sustaining level. Channel and corporate costs stay at their own levels.

It's the activity-based logic from Cost Accounting I, applied to customers instead of products.

Quick check. Co-op advertising paid for one dealer, regardless of what it buys, is which level? Pause and pick.

It's C, customer-sustaining.

Microteach 9.5 · 1:22Customer-profitability profiles

Ranking customers by profit often shows a few earn most of it and some lose money.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Put revenue and costs together for each dealer, and the results are often surprising.

Prairie Pedals buys two hundred bikes. Despite its discount, it earns $112,000 of gross margin and costs only $10,300 to serve: $101,700 of profit. Lakes Cycle earns $24,100. Big Sky pays full price but orders one bike at a time, far away, with lots of returns. It loses $750.

Plot cumulative profit with customers ranked from most to least profitable, and you often get a whale curve: the best twenty percent of customers may generate more than a hundred percent of profit, with the tail of loss-makers dragging it back down.

Notice the lesson: the discounted customer is the most profitable, and the full-price customer loses money. The cost to serve matters as much as the price.

Managers grasp this best visually: a bar chart of profit by customer, or a cumulative curve, makes the loss-makers obvious.

Quick check. A dealer has thirty thousand of gross margin and thirty-four thousand of customer-level costs. Customer-level income? Pause and pick.

It's B, a $4,000 loss.

Microteach 9.6 · 1:11Acting on customer profitability

Change prices, service levels or ordering behavior before dropping a customer; consider lifetime value.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Big Sky Bikes loses money. Should Tallgrass drop it? There are better first moves.

Ana could set a minimum order of four bikes, charge for rush deliveries, combine Big Sky's shipments with other Dakota dealers, and replace some sales visits with video calls.

With seven orders instead of twenty-five, seven deliveries, four visits, and the same returns and co-op costs, Big Sky earns $9,570 instead of losing $750.

Before dropping anyone, ask which costs would really disappear, what the customer could become over its lifetime, whether it opens a new territory, and how other dealers would react.

The same analysis works by channel. Tallgrass might find that selling online directly costs less per bike than dealers in some regions, or more once returns and support are counted.

Quick check. What's usually the best first step with an unprofitable customer? Pause and pick.

It's B. Change the cost to serve or the price before dropping anyone.

Microteach 14.1 · 1:25Management control and transfer prices

Transfer prices should promote goal congruence, effort and autonomy; methods are market, cost or negotiated.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 23 and 24.

Read the script

Tallgrass has grown into two divisions. Motors makes the electric drive units. Assembly builds the bikes. What should Assembly pay Motors?

A transfer price is the price one subunit of an organization charges another for a product or service. It's revenue for Motors and a cost for Assembly, so it drives both divisions' profits.

A good management control system, including transfer prices, promotes goal congruence, so managers acting in their own interest also act in the company's; management effort; subunit autonomy; and fair performance evaluation.

There are three methods. Market-based prices use the outside price, best when there's a competitive market. Cost-based prices use variable or full cost, sometimes plus a markup, when no market price exists. Negotiated prices come from bargaining between the divisions.

Motors' variable cost is $180 per drive unit, full cost $260. Outside buyers pay $320. Selling internally saves $20 of selling cost.

Quick check. When managers acting in their own interest also act in the company's interest, what's that called? Pause and pick.

It's B, goal congruence.

Microteach 14.2 · 1:26The minimum transfer price

Minimum transfer price = incremental cost per unit + opportunity cost per unit.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 23 and 24.

Read the script

One rule tells you the lowest price the selling division should accept.

The minimum transfer price equals the incremental cost per unit up to the point of transfer, plus the opportunity cost per unit to the selling division.

With idle capacity, Motors gives up nothing, so the minimum is the $180 variable cost. At full capacity, every internal unit displaces an outside sale. Incremental cost is $160 after the $20 saving, plus $140 of lost margin, so the minimum is $300: the market price less the selling cost saved.

Assembly won't pay more than the $320 it would pay outside. With idle capacity, any price from $180 to $320 benefits the company. Where in that range it lands just divides the profit.

Full-cost transfer prices can mislead. Fixed costs make units look more costly than they are, and divisions may make choices that hurt the company. Dual pricing, where the seller is credited at one price and the buyer charged another, is one fix.

Quick check. Motors is at full capacity. Market price three twenty; twenty dollars saved internally. Minimum transfer price? Pause and pick.

It's C, $300.

Microteach 14.3 · 1:25Multinational transfer pricing and taxes

Across borders, transfer prices shift taxable income; the arm's-length standard limits them.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 23 and 24.

Read the script

When divisions sit in different countries, the transfer price also decides where profit is taxed.

Suppose Motors were in a country with a fifteen percent tax rate, and Assembly stayed in the US at twenty-one percent. Raising the transfer price by forty dollars on a thousand units shifts $40,000 of profit abroad. That saves $8,400 of US tax but costs $6,000 abroad. Net saving: $2,400.

Tax authorities don't let companies pick any price. Under the arm's-length standard, in the US Internal Revenue Code Section 482 and in OECD guidelines, related parties must price as unrelated parties would.

Other factors matter too: customs duties based on the import value, restrictions on moving cash out of some countries, global minimum tax rules for large groups, and how the local managers are evaluated and motivated.

One transfer price can't serve every purpose perfectly, so many multinationals keep one price for tax and a different measure internally for evaluating managers.

Quick check. The rule that related parties must price as unrelated parties would is called what? Pause and pick.

It's B, the arm's-length standard.

Microteach 14.4 · 1:36ROI, residual income and EVA

ROI = income ÷ investment; RI = income − required return × investment; EVA uses after-tax income and WACC.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 23 and 24.

Read the script

How do you compare a division's profit with the money tied up in it? Three measures.

Return on investment is operating income divided by investment. Motors earns $600,000 on $4 million of assets: fifteen percent. Assembly earns $900,000 on $7.5 million: twelve percent.

The DuPont method splits ROI into return on sales times investment turnover. Motors has $5 million of revenue: a twelve percent margin times a turnover of one point two five gives fifteen percent. Improve either to raise ROI.

Residual income is operating income minus the required rate of return times investment. Motors: $200,000. Assembly: $150,000. Both earn more than the ten percent required.

Economic value added is a version of residual income: after-tax operating income minus the weighted-average cost of capital times total assets less current liabilities. For Motors, $450,000 after tax, less nine percent of $3.5 million, gives $135,000.

Quick check. Operating income three hundred thousand; investment two million; required return ten percent. Residual income? Pause and pick.

It's A, $100,000.

Microteach 14.5 · 1:25Choosing measures and the ROI trap

ROI can make managers reject projects that beat the required return; RI and EVA don't.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 23 and 24.

Read the script

The measure you choose changes the decisions managers make.

Motors can invest $1 million in a project earning $130,000: thirteen percent, well above the ten percent required. But it would pull Motors' ROI down from fifteen to fourteen point six percent. A manager judged on ROI says no. Residual income rises by $30,000, so a manager judged on RI says yes.

That's why many firms prefer residual income or EVA: any project that beats the required return raises them.

Other choices matter too: the time horizon; whether investment is total assets or net of current liabilities; whether assets are at net book value, which makes old plants look good, gross book value, or current cost; and the targets and timing of feedback.

Finally, reward managers for what they can control, balance incentive pay against the risk they bear, and use nonfinancial measures, like the balanced scorecard from Lecture 10, alongside the financial ones.

Quick check. A division's ROI is eighteen percent; required return twelve. A project offers fifteen percent. Who accepts it? Pause and pick.

It's B. ROI would fall, but RI rises.

Microteach 14.6 · 1:17Course recap: Cost Accounting II

Predict costs, plan, decide, then measure and control: the cost accountant's toolkit for management.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 23 and 24.

Read the script

Fourteen lectures in two minutes: how cost information helps managers plan, decide and control.

First, cost behavior and planning: estimating cost functions with regression and learning curves, using data analytics to predict, cost-volume-profit analysis, and the master budget with responsibility accounting.

Then decisions: variable versus absorption costing and capacity, relevant costs for make-or-buy and special orders, pricing and target costing, and capital budgeting with NPV and IRR.

And performance and control: cost allocation and customer profitability, strategy and the balanced scorecard, quality, time, inventory management and just-in-time, and transfer prices with ROI, residual income and EVA.

The one idea tying it all together: different costs for different purposes. Before using any cost number, ask what decision it's for.

Last quick check. Which measure is best for ranking long-term investments? Pause and pick.

It's C, net present value. Good luck on the final, and thanks for riding with Tallgrass.

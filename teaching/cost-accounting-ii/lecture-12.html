Microteach 12.1 · 1:20What inventory really costs

Inventory costs include purchasing, ordering, carrying, stockout, quality and shrinkage costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 21.

Read the script

Inventory looks like an asset on the balance sheet. But holding it costs money every day.

Six costs. Purchasing costs, the price. Ordering costs: preparing orders, freight and receiving. Carrying costs: storage, insurance and the return on the capital tied up. Stockout costs: lost sales and rush orders. Quality costs: defects hidden in big batches. And shrinkage: theft, damage and obsolescence.

The biggest carrying cost is often invisible in the accounts: the opportunity cost of the capital tied up. Cash sitting in battery packs could be earning a return elsewhere.

A battery pack costs $310. At Tallgrass's eight percent cost of capital, holding one for a year costs $24.80, plus $2.20 of climate-controlled storage and insurance: twenty-seven dollars a year.

The core trade-off: ordering often keeps carrying costs low but multiplies ordering costs. Ordering rarely does the reverse. The economic order quantity balances them.

Quick check. Which is a carrying cost? Pause and pick.

It's B, the opportunity cost of capital tied up in inventory.

Microteach 12.2 · 1:18The economic order quantity

EOQ = √(2DP ÷ C) balances ordering and carrying costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 21.

Read the script

How many battery packs should Tallgrass order at a time? The economic order quantity gives the answer.

The economic order quantity is the square root of two times annual demand times the cost per order, divided by the annual carrying cost per unit.

Tallgrass uses twelve hundred packs a year. Each order costs a hundred eighty dollars to place and receive. Carrying a pack costs twenty-seven dollars a year. The EOQ is the square root of sixteen thousand: about a hundred twenty-six packs.

At that quantity, Tallgrass places about nine and a half orders a year, costing about $1,700, and carries an average of sixty-three packs, also about $1,700. At the EOQ, ordering and carrying costs are equal.

The purchase price doesn't affect the EOQ, unless bigger orders earn quantity discounts, which must then be weighed against extra carrying costs.

Quick check. Annual demand two thousand, cost per order a hundred dollars, carrying cost ten dollars. What's the EOQ? Pause and pick.

It's A. The square root of forty thousand is two hundred.

Microteach 12.3 · 1:05Reorder point and safety stock

Reorder point = usage during lead time + safety stock.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 21.

Read the script

EOQ says how much to order. The reorder point says when.

The reorder point is the inventory level at which to place a new order: expected usage during the lead time, plus any safety stock.

Tallgrass uses about twenty-four packs a week, and overseas orders take eight weeks to arrive. That's a hundred ninety-two packs used while waiting. Add two weeks of safety stock, forty-eight packs, and the reorder point is two hundred forty.

Safety stock is a buffer against uncertainty: demand spikes in spring, or shipping delays at the ports.

How much safety stock? Enough that the expected cost of stockouts, lost sales and idle assembly lines, balances the extra carrying cost. For a key part like batteries, Tallgrass holds more.

Quick check. Usage thirty a week, lead time four weeks, safety stock forty. Reorder point? Pause and pick.

It's B. A hundred twenty plus forty is a hundred sixty.

Microteach 12.4 · 1:14The cost of a prediction error

EOQ is robust to estimation errors; evaluation systems must reward managers for the right inventory decisions.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 21.

Read the script

Ana's estimates of ordering and carrying costs could be wrong. How much would a mistake cost?

Suppose the true cost per order is $240, not $180. The true EOQ is a hundred forty-six packs, with relevant costs of $3,944. Ordering a hundred twenty-six, as Ana did, costs $3,987. The prediction error costs only forty-three dollars.

The EOQ model is robust: total cost is flat near the optimum, so moderate errors in the inputs cost little.

The bigger risk is behavioral. If the purchasing manager's performance ignores the cost of capital tied up in inventory, she has every reason to over-order and never run out.

The fix: charge managers for the capital their inventory ties up, so the decision model and the evaluation model agree.

Quick check. A purchasing manager is judged only on price variances and stockouts. What's the likely behavior? Pause and pick.

It's B: big batches, ignoring the carrying costs she isn't charged for.

Microteach 12.5 · 1:23Just-in-time purchasing and production

JIT buys and makes only what's needed, when needed; it cuts carrying costs but demands reliable suppliers and quality.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 21.

Read the script

Just-in-time aims to hold almost no inventory: buy and make only what's needed, when it's needed.

JIT production is pulled by actual demand rather than pushed by forecasts. Work is organized in cells that build a product from start to finish, with multi-skilled workers. Defects stop the line, because there's no inventory to hide behind. And fast setups make small batches economical.

Tallgrass is considering daily frame deliveries from a supplier in nearby Worthington. Lower inventory saves $4,500 of carrying cost. Extra deliveries cost $3,200, and the expected cost of occasional stockouts is $600. The net benefit is $700, small but positive, plus floor space freed.

JIT suits local, reliable suppliers. Battery packs shipped from overseas with eight-week lead times aren't candidates, as supply-chain disruptions in recent years showed.

With little inventory, there's less need to track costs through work in process. That opens the door to simpler costing: backflush costing.

Quick check. Which supplier is the best JIT candidate? Pause and pick.

It's B: local, frequent and reliable.

Microteach 12.6 · 1:24Backflush costing

Backflush costing skips recording work in process and assigns standard costs at a later trigger point.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 21.

Read the script

In a JIT plant, tracking every cost through work in process isn't worth the effort. Backflush costing takes a shortcut.

Backflush costing omits some or all of the entries for the flow of costs through production. Costs are assigned to output using standard costs, at chosen trigger points, such as when materials are bought or when bikes are finished.

At Tallgrass's JIT assembly cell, the first trigger is buying materials: $112,200 for a hundred two bikes' worth of kits, debited to a combined materials and in-process inventory account.

Conversion costs incurred are collected in a control account. When a hundred bikes are finished, the second trigger, their standard costs are backflushed: $1,100 of materials and $200 of conversion each. Debit finished goods $130,000.

Backflush is simple and cheap, and accurate when inventories are small and stable. If work in process is large or changes a lot, it misstates inventory and income, and it leaves less of an audit trail.

Quick check. When is backflush costing least appropriate? Pause and pick.

It's B: large, fluctuating work in process.

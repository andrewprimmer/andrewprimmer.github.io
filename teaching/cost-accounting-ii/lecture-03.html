Microteach 3.1 · 1:37Contribution margin

Contribution margin is revenue minus variable costs; it covers fixed costs, then produces profit.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

How many e-bikes must Tallgrass sell to make a profit? Cost–volume–profit analysis starts with one idea: contribution margin.

The Trail e-bike sells for $2,400. Variable costs, frame, motor, battery, assembly labor and dealer commission, are $1,500. Each bike contributes $900 toward fixed costs and profit. That's a contribution margin ratio of thirty-seven and a half percent.

At nine hundred bikes, revenue is $2,160,000, variable costs $1,350,000, and contribution margin $810,000. After $540,000 of fixed costs, operating income is $270,000.

CVP assumes costs split cleanly into fixed and variable parts, behave linearly within the relevant range, that selling price, unit costs and the mix of products are known, and that production equals sales.

The power of contribution margin: every extra bike sold adds $900 to operating income, as long as fixed costs don't change.

Quick check. A cargo bike sells for thirty-six hundred with variable costs of twenty-one hundred. What's the contribution margin ratio? Pause and pick.

It's A. Fifteen hundred divided by thirty-six hundred is about forty-one point seven percent.

Microteach 3.2 · 1:24The breakeven point

Breakeven units = fixed costs ÷ unit contribution margin; breakeven revenue = fixed costs ÷ CM ratio.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Breakeven is where operating income is exactly zero. Below it Tallgrass loses money; above it, it makes money.

Breakeven in units is fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit. Breakeven in revenue is fixed costs divided by the contribution margin ratio.

Fixed costs of $540,000 divided by $900 per bike: six hundred bikes to break even. In revenue, $540,000 divided by thirty-seven and a half percent: $1,440,000. Six hundred bikes at $2,400 confirms it.

The equation method gives the same answer: set price times quantity, minus variable cost times quantity, minus fixed costs, equal to zero. Twenty-four hundred Q minus fifteen hundred Q minus five hundred forty thousand equals zero, so Q is six hundred.

Breakeven isn't a target. It tells managers how risky a plan is: how far sales can fall before losses start.

Quick check. If fixed costs rise to six hundred thirty thousand, what's breakeven? Pause and pick.

It's B. 630,000 divided by 900 is seven hundred bikes.

Microteach 3.3 · 1:13Target income and taxes

Units for a target = (fixed costs + target operating income) ÷ unit CM; gross up net income for taxes.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Breaking even isn't the goal. How many bikes for a target profit, before or after tax?

To earn $180,000 of operating income, Tallgrass must cover fixed costs plus the target: $720,000. Divided by $900 a bike, that's eight hundred bikes.

If the target is net income after taxes, first convert it to operating income: divide by one minus the tax rate.

Ana's board wants $150,000 of net income, with a twenty-five percent tax rate. That needs $200,000 of operating income. Fixed costs plus $200,000, divided by $900, is eight hundred twenty-two point two, so Tallgrass must sell eight hundred twenty-three bikes.

Notice that taxes don't change the breakeven point. With zero operating income there's no tax to pay.

Quick check. Target operating income ninety thousand. How many bikes? Pause and pick.

It's B. 630,000 divided by 900 is seven hundred.

Microteach 3.4 · 1:33Decisions and sensitivity

Use CVP to test decisions; margin of safety shows how far sales can fall before losses.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

CVP really earns its keep when managers ask 'what if'. Here's how Ana tests decisions and risk.

Marketing wants $30,000 more for advertising and expects forty more sales. Forty bikes times $900 is $36,000 of extra contribution. Less the $30,000, operating income rises by $6,000. Worth it, if the forty sales are realistic.

Sales also suggests cutting the price to $2,250 to sell a thousand bikes. Contribution margin falls to $750 a bike, and total contribution to $750,000, $60,000 less than now. Reject it.

The margin of safety is how far sales can fall below budget before Tallgrass loses money: budgeted revenue minus breakeven revenue.

With nine hundred bikes budgeted, the margin of safety is $720,000, or a third of budgeted sales.

Quick check. Raising the price to twenty-five hundred cuts volume from nine hundred to eight hundred twenty. What's the change in operating income? Pause and pick.

It's A. Contribution becomes 820 times $1,000, or $820,000, against $810,000 now: up $10,000.

Microteach 3.5 · 1:28Cost structure and operating leverage

More fixed costs mean higher operating leverage: bigger swings in income when sales change.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Two companies with the same profit can carry very different risk. The difference is cost structure.

The degree of operating leverage is contribution margin divided by operating income. It tells you how much operating income changes for each percent change in sales.

At nine hundred bikes, Tallgrass's contribution margin is $810,000 and operating income $270,000: a degree of operating leverage of three. A ten percent rise in sales raises operating income by thirty percent.

Ana is considering a robotic assembly line. Variable cost would fall to $1,300 a bike, but fixed costs would rise to $720,000. At nine hundred bikes, income is the same, $270,000. But breakeven rises to about six hundred fifty-five bikes.

With more fixed costs, the robotic line earns more when sales beat nine hundred and loses more when they fall short. The choice depends on how confident Ana is about volume.

Quick check. With a degree of operating leverage of four, sales fall five percent. What happens to operating income? Pause and pick.

It's B. Four times five percent: a twenty percent fall.

Microteach 3.6 · 1:20Multiple products and sales mix

With several products, compute breakeven for a bundle in the expected sales mix.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Tallgrass sells two bikes, not one. With several products, breakeven depends on the sales mix.

Tallgrass sells three Trail bikes for every Cargo bike. A bundle of three Trails and one Cargo contributes three times $900 plus $1,500: $4,200.

Fixed costs of $540,000 divided by $4,200 gives about a hundred twenty-nine bundles. That's about three hundred eighty-six Trail bikes and a hundred twenty-nine Cargo bikes.

If the mix shifts toward Cargo bikes, which contribute more, breakeven falls. If customers switch to Trail bikes, it rises, even if total units don't change.

And if a resource is scarce, like assembly hours, the best product is the one with the highest contribution per hour of that resource, not per bike. That's Lecture 7.

Quick check. If the mix were one Trail to one Cargo, what's the contribution per bundle? Pause and pick.

It's B. $900 plus $1,500 is $2,400 per two-bike bundle, so breakeven would be 225 of each.

Microteach 4.1 · 1:13Why budget?

Budgets turn strategy into plans, coordinate departments and set benchmarks for performance.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

A budget is a plan expressed in numbers. Done well, it's the most useful document a company produces each year.

Budgets turn strategy into concrete plans. They force sales, production and purchasing to coordinate. They communicate goals. They set benchmarks to judge performance against. And, set at the right level, they motivate.

The master budget pulls everything together: operating budgets, which lead to a budgeted income statement, and financial budgets, like the cash budget and budgeted balance sheet.

Most companies budget annually. A rolling budget adds a new month as each month ends, so it always looks twelve months ahead. Kaizen budgeting builds steady cost reductions into each period.

A budget isn't just a forecast of what will happen. It's a plan for what managers intend to make happen, and the actions they'll take.

Quick check. Tallgrass adds January 2028 to its budget as soon as January 2027 ends. What kind of budget is that? Pause and pick.

It's B, a rolling budget.

Microteach 4.2 · 1:10The budget sequence

Budgeting starts with the revenue budget and flows through production and costs to the income statement and cash.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

The master budget is built in a fixed order. Almost everything starts with one number: expected sales.

Start with the revenue budget. That sets the production budget. Production drives the direct materials, direct labor and overhead budgets. Those give ending inventory and cost of goods sold. Add nonmanufacturing costs and you have the budgeted income statement.

The financial budgets follow: the capital expenditures budget, the cash budget, the budgeted balance sheet and the budgeted statement of cash flows.

The sales forecast is the hardest and most important part. Ana combines past sales, dealer surveys, the economic outlook and planned prices and promotions. If it's wrong, everything else is wrong too.

Budgets are built in spreadsheets or planning software, which lets managers run scenarios: what if sales are ten percent lower, or battery prices rise?

Quick check. Which budget comes first? Pause and pick.

It's C. Expected sales drive everything else.

Microteach 4.3 · 1:08Revenue and production budgets

Production = budgeted sales + target ending finished goods − beginning finished goods.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

With the sales forecast in hand, Ana budgets revenue, then works out how many bikes to build.

Tallgrass expects to sell twelve hundred Trail bikes at $2,400: budgeted revenue of $2,880,000.

Production equals budgeted sales plus the target ending inventory of finished bikes, minus what's already on hand. Twelve hundred plus sixty is twelve sixty. Less the forty in stock, Tallgrass must build twelve hundred twenty bikes.

Why plan to end with more bikes than it started with? E-bike demand peaks in spring. Building stock over the winter keeps production steady.

But inventory ties up cash and space, and bike models go out of date. Lecture 12 looks at how much inventory is too much.

Quick check. Sales nine hundred, target ending finished goods fifty, beginning seventy. How many to produce? Pause and pick.

It's B. 900 plus 50 minus 70 is 880.

Microteach 4.4 · 1:14Materials usage and purchases

Purchases = usage + target ending materials − beginning materials.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Production tells purchasing how many parts to use. Inventory targets tell it how many to buy.

Each bike needs one battery pack costing $310. Building twelve hundred twenty bikes uses $378,200 of batteries.

To end the year with eighty packs, after starting with fifty, Tallgrass must buy twelve hundred fifty packs: $387,500. The same calculation is done for frames, motors and every other material.

Labor follows the same logic. Each bike takes six assembly hours at twenty-eight dollars: $204,960 of direct labor.

In practice, lead times shape the purchases budget. If battery packs take three months to arrive, orders must be placed a quarter ahead of production.

Quick check. Usage two thousand units, target ending three hundred, beginning two fifty. How many to buy? Pause and pick.

It's A. 2,000 plus 300 minus 250 is 2,050.

Microteach 4.5 · 1:29The cash budget

The cash budget shows when cash comes in and goes out, and when the company must borrow.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Profitable companies still go bust when they run out of cash. The cash budget is the early warning.

The cash budget starts with the beginning balance, adds collections from customers, subtracts disbursements for materials, wages, overhead, taxes and equipment, then shows any financing needed to keep a minimum balance.

Tallgrass starts the first quarter with $40,000. It expects to collect $620,000 from dealers but pay out $690,000 while building spring stock. That leaves minus $30,000. With a $30,000 minimum balance, it must borrow $60,000.

Collections lag sales, because dealers pay thirty to sixty days after delivery. A collection schedule spreads each month's sales over the months when cash actually arrives.

Knowing in the fall that Tallgrass will need $60,000 in the first quarter lets Ana arrange a line of credit calmly, instead of scrambling in March.

Quick check. Beginning cash twenty-five thousand, collections three hundred thousand, disbursements three ten, minimum balance twenty thousand. How much to borrow? Pause and pick.

It's B. Cash before financing is $15,000, so $5,000 is needed to reach $20,000.

Microteach 4.6 · 1:09The human side of budgeting

Participative budgeting improves buy-in but invites slack; stretch targets motivate if they're achievable.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Budgets are made by people, and people respond to how they're judged. That creates problems, and solutions.

Budgetary slack is building in a cushion: underestimating revenues or overestimating costs so the budget is easier to beat. It's common when bonuses depend on beating the budget.

Top-down budgets are fast and ambitious, but managers may not commit to them. Participative budgets use the knowledge of the people who must meet them and earn buy-in, but invite slack.

Ways to reduce slack: compare budgets with benchmarks and past results; reward accurate forecasts, not just beating the number; and set stretch targets that are challenging but achievable.

Watch for year-end games too, like spending leftover budget in December so it isn't cut next year.

Quick check. The sales manager forecasts a thousand bikes, though she expects eleven fifty, because her bonus rewards beating the budget. What's this? Pause and pick.

It's B, budgetary slack.

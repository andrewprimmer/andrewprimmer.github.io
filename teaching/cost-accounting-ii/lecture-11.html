Microteach 11.1 · 1:14Two kinds of quality

Quality of design fits the product to customer needs; conformance quality makes it to specification.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 20.

Read the script

For a differentiator like Tallgrass, quality isn't optional. But quality means two different things.

Quality of design is how well a product's features match what customers need: a fifty-mile range when commuters need seventy is a design failure. Conformance quality is how well the product meets its own specifications: a motor seal that leaks is a conformance failure.

Both cost money. Design failures lose sales to better-designed rivals. Conformance failures create rework, warranty claims and angry customers.

Many companies certify their processes under the ISO nine thousand standards, which require documented, consistent quality procedures. Some large buyers insist on it.

Quality measures sit in the internal-process perspective of the balanced scorecard from Lecture 10, and drive customer satisfaction and financial results.

Quick check. Tallgrass's bikes meet every specification, but buyers want more range than the design offers. What's failing? Pause and pick.

It's B, quality of design.

Microteach 11.2 · 1:26The costs of quality

Costs of quality fall into prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 20.

Read the script

Poor quality is expensive, but the costs are scattered across the company. A cost-of-quality report pulls them together.

Prevention costs stop defects from happening: design reviews, supplier evaluation, training. Appraisal costs find defects before shipping: inspection and testing. Internal failure costs arise from defects caught in-house: rework and scrap. External failure costs arise after the sale: warranty repairs, returns and lost future sales.

Tallgrass's 2027 report: $60,000 of prevention, $70,000 of appraisal, $60,000 of internal failure and $180,000 of external failure, including an estimated $70,000 of contribution from lost sales. Total: $370,000, almost thirteen percent of revenue.

The pattern is typical: most of the cost is failure, especially external failure. Shifting spending toward prevention usually lowers the total.

Notice that lost sales are an opportunity cost, estimated, not recorded in the accounts. Ignoring them understates how much poor quality really costs.

Quick check. Road-testing every finished bike is which category? Pause and pick.

It's B, appraisal: finding defects before shipping.

Microteach 11.3 · 1:15Finding quality problems

Control charts spot problems, Pareto diagrams rank them, and fishbone diagrams trace their causes.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 20.

Read the script

To fix quality problems you have to find them, rank them, and trace their causes. Three tools help.

A control chart plots a process measure, like defects per day, against limits set from its normal variation, often two or three standard deviations from the mean. Points outside the limits signal a problem to investigate.

A Pareto diagram ranks problems by frequency. Of eighty-two warranty claims, forty-one were motor seal leaks and twenty-two battery connector faults. Two causes account for three-quarters of claims. Fix those first.

A cause-and-effect, or fishbone, diagram lists possible causes under headings like methods, machines, materials and people. For the leaking seals: a seal pressed at an angle, a worn press fixture, a supplier's new rubber compound, or untrained new assemblers.

Then the team tests the candidate causes with data. At Tallgrass, leaks clustered in bikes built after the supplier changed its rubber compound.

Quick check. Which tool ranks problems by frequency? Pause and pick.

It's B, the Pareto diagram.

Microteach 11.4 · 1:27Is a quality improvement worth it?

Compare the relevant costs of a quality program with the failure costs it saves, including lost contribution.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 20.

Read the script

A better motor seal costs more. Is it worth it? Relevant cost analysis answers.

The upgraded seal costs an extra twenty-five dollars a bike, $30,000 for twelve hundred bikes, plus $15,000 of one-time redesign and testing: $45,000.

It's expected to avoid $40,000 of warranty repairs and $10,000 of rework, and to recover twenty lost sales worth $900 each in contribution, $18,000. Savings total $68,000, a net benefit of $23,000.

Include only costs that actually change. If Tallgrass keeps its inspectors anyway, their salaries aren't savings, even if they inspect fewer seals.

And count the nonfinancial benefits: brand reputation, dealer confidence and fewer riders stranded with a dead motor, which matter a great deal for a differentiator.

Quick check. A fifty thousand dollar program saves thirty thousand of warranty costs and recovers twenty-five sales at nine hundred dollars contribution. Net benefit? Pause and pick.

It's A. Savings of $52,500 less $50,000: $2,500.

Microteach 11.5 · 1:27Customer-response time and queues

Response time includes waiting; as capacity fills, waiting time grows sharply.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 20.

Read the script

Customers care how fast they get their bike. Most of the delay isn't work; it's waiting.

Customer-response time runs from the order to delivery. It includes receipt time, until the order reaches production; waiting time in the queue; manufacturing time; and delivery time.

For a single bottleneck with random order arrivals, average waiting time can be estimated as orders times processing time squared, divided by two times the capacity left over.

Tallgrass's custom paint booth has four thousand hours a year. A hundred orders at thirty hours each means an average wait of forty-five hours. Add twenty-five new orders at twenty hours, and the booth is eighty-seven and a half percent busy: the average wait more than doubles to a hundred hours.

The lesson: waiting time rises steeply as a resource approaches full use. A new product can slow every existing order. The cost of those delays, lost sales and price discounts, belongs in the decision.

Quick check. Fifty orders at forty hours each; capacity three thousand hours. Average waiting time? Pause and pick.

It's A. 50 times 1,600 is 80,000, divided by 2 times 1,000 spare hours: forty hours.

Microteach 11.6 · 1:12The theory of constraints

Manage the bottleneck: maximize throughput margin through it, then relieve it.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 20.

Read the script

The theory of constraints says one resource limits the whole system. Manage that, and the rest follows.

The theory of constraints focuses on throughput margin: revenue minus direct materials. In the short run, other costs are treated as fixed.

Five steps: identify the bottleneck; get the most throughput margin out of it; pace everything else to it, avoiding inventory piling up in front; relieve the bottleneck by adding capacity; then find the next one.

For the paint booth: never let it sit idle, covering breaks; inspect frames before painting so the booth never paints a defective frame; send simple jobs to an outside painter; and add a second booth if the extra throughput margin justifies the cost.

The key insight: an hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole plant, while an hour saved elsewhere is often worth nothing.

Quick check. If painting is the bottleneck, where should inspection happen? Pause and pick.

It's B, before painting, so the bottleneck never wastes time on defective frames.

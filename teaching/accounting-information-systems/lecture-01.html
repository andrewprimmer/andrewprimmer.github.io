Microteach 1.1 · 1:54What is an AIS?

An accounting information system, or AIS, is the people, procedures and technology that collect, store and process financial data, and turn it into information…

Read the script

Every time you tap your card for a coffee, at least three accounting systems wake up. So what actually is an accounting system?

An accounting information system, or AIS, is the people, procedures and technology that collect, store and process financial data, and turn it into information people can use to make decisions.

Think of it as a pipeline. Raw data comes in: a sale, a delivery, a timesheet. The system processes it by recording, classifying and summarizing. Out comes information: an invoice, a balance, a report. And that information drives decisions.

Here's the key distinction. Data is just facts. Ten thousand rows of coffee sales is data. Information is data organized so it's useful. Sales are down twelve percent at the downtown store on Mondays: that's information.

Not all information is good information. To be valuable it must be relevant, reliable, complete, timely, understandable, verifiable and accessible. A perfect report that arrives two weeks after the decision is worthless.

Back to that coffee. The point-of-sale terminal records the sale. Inventory drops by one cup and one shot of espresso. The general ledger picks up cash and revenue. And by tomorrow morning, a manager's dashboard shows how the store did.

So an AIS doesn't just keep the books. It's the system that turns everyday transactions into decisions. That's what this whole course is about.

Quick check. Which of these is information rather than data? Pause and pick.

The answer is C. It's been processed into something a manager can act on.

Microteach 1.2 · 1:52The six components of an AIS

Most textbooks break an AIS into six components.

Read the script

What do a cashier, a password and a spreadsheet have in common? They're all parts of the same accounting system.

Most textbooks break an AIS into six components. Learn these six and you can take apart any system you'll ever meet.

First, people: the employees who run the system, and the managers, customers and auditors who use what it produces.

Second, procedures and instructions, both manual and automated, for collecting, processing and storing data. For example, who approves a refund, and how.

Third, data about the organization and its business activities: customers, vendors, products, sales and payments.

Fourth, the software that processes that data. Anything from QuickBooks to a full enterprise resource planning system like SAP.

Fifth, IT infrastructure: the computers, networks, cloud servers and devices everything runs on, right down to the card reader at the till.

And sixth, internal controls and security measures that safeguard the data and the assets. Passwords, approvals, backups and segregation of duties.

Back to our three items. The cashier is people. The password is an internal control. The spreadsheet is software holding data. One system, many parts.

If any one component fails, the whole system fails. Great software run by untrained people still produces bad information.

Quick check. A rule that refunds over a hundred dollars need a manager's approval. Which component is that? Pause and pick.

It's an internal control. It protects assets by requiring authorization.

Microteach 1.3 · 1:45AIS and the value chain

Michael Porter's value chain splits a business into the activities that add value for the customer.

Read the script

Why do companies spend millions of dollars on accounting systems? Because good information makes money. Here's how.

Michael Porter's value chain splits a business into the activities that add value for the customer. There are two kinds.

Primary activities deliver the product: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and after-sales service.

Support activities make the primary ones possible: firm infrastructure, which includes accounting and the AIS itself, human resources, technology, and purchasing.

An AIS adds value in six ways. It improves quality and cuts costs, improves efficiency, supports better decisions, helps people share knowledge, smooths the supply chain, and strengthens internal control.

For example, a retailer's AIS shows which products sell in which week. Purchasing uses that forecast to order less slow-moving stock. Less cash tied up, less waste. Information turned straight into profit.

And it doesn't stop at the company wall. Your value chain links to your suppliers' chains and your customers' chains. That's the value system, and shared data such as electronic orders ties it together.

So an AIS isn't just a cost of doing business. Used well, it's a source of competitive advantage.

Quick check. In Porter's value chain, is accounting a primary activity or a support activity? Pause and pick.

It's a support activity. It sits inside firm infrastructure.

Microteach 1.4 · 1:29The five transaction cycles

A transaction cycle is a group of related business activities that repeat.

Read the script

Every business, from a lemonade stand to Apple, runs on the same five cycles. Learn them once and use them forever.

A transaction cycle is a group of related business activities that repeat. Each one is basically an exchange: give something, get something.

The revenue cycle: give goods or services, get cash from customers. We call it order to cash.

The expenditure cycle: give cash, get goods and services from suppliers. Procure to pay.

The production cycle: give labor and raw materials, get finished products.

The human resources and payroll cycle: give cash, get employees' time and skills. Hire to retire.

And at the center sits the general ledger and reporting system. Every other cycle feeds data into it, and it produces the financial statements and management reports.

A bakery buys flour: expenditure. It bakes bread: production. It pays its bakers: payroll. It sells loaves: revenue. And the ledger pulls it all together into a profit figure.

The cycles are linked. Cash from the revenue cycle pays for the expenditure and payroll cycles. We'll spend six weeks on these, one at a time.

Quick check. Paying a supplier's invoice: which cycle? Pause and pick.

Expenditure. Procure to pay.

Microteach 1.5 · 1:28Who relies on the AIS?

Inside the company, executives want KPIs and strategy reports.

Read the script

One accounting system, a dozen audiences, and every one of them wants something different.

Inside the company, executives want KPIs and strategy reports. Managers want budgets and variance analysis. Operations staff need real-time orders, stock levels and schedules. Internal auditors want evidence that controls work.

Outside, investors and analysts read the financial statements. Lenders check debt covenants and cash flow. Regulators like the SEC and the IRS need filings and tax returns. External auditors need evidence. And customers and suppliers need invoices and orders.

The trick is that they all draw on the same underlying data. A modern AIS stores each transaction once, then presents it in different views for different users.

Take a single sale. It appears on a sales manager's dashboard, on the customer's invoice, in the sales tax return, and eventually inside revenue on the 10-K.

So designing an AIS starts with three questions: who needs this information, when do they need it, and in what form?

Quick check. Who cares most about debt covenant compliance? Pause and pick.

Lenders. Breaking a covenant can let them call in the loan.

Microteach 1.6 · 1:30Why accountants need systems skills

Accountants relate to the AIS in four ways.

Read the script

Spoiler: the robots aren't taking accounting jobs. But accountants who understand systems are taking the best ones.

Accountants relate to the AIS in four ways. You use it every day. You help design it, because you know what the reports need to show. You evaluate it, as an auditor testing its controls. And you analyze its data to answer business questions.

Look at accounting job postings and the same skills come up again and again: ERP experience, advanced Excel and data analytics, SQL and databases, visualization tools like Power BI and Tableau, internal controls and IT audit, and process documentation.

The profession agrees. Since 2024, the CPA exam has included Information Systems and Controls as one of its three discipline sections.

Systems skills open doors: IT auditor, ERP consultant, systems accountant, financial data analyst, and in time, controller or CFO.

This course gives you that toolkit: business processes, data, ERP systems, controls and audit. Let's get started.

Quick check. Which CPA exam discipline section focuses on information systems? Pause and pick.

ISC: Information Systems and Controls.

Microteach 8.1 · 1:04Plan to produce in 2 minutes

The production cycle has four activities: product design, planning and scheduling, production operations, and cost accounting.

Read the script

How does a factory turn raw materials into a product with a known cost?

The production cycle has four activities: product design, planning and scheduling, production operations, and cost accounting.

The key documents: the bill of materials, the routing or operations list, the production order, the materials requisition, and the move ticket that tracks work between stations.

Production connects to every other cycle. Sales forecasts drive the plan. The expenditure cycle buys materials. Payroll pays the workers. And the ledger records the cost of what's made.

The accounting goal is accurate cost information: what did each product really cost? That's how we price it, value inventory, and control waste.

So: design, plan, make, and cost.

Quick check. Which document lists the materials that go into a product? Pause and pick.

The bill of materials.

Microteach 8.2 · 1:15Bills of materials and routings

A bill of materials, or BOM, lists every component in a product, and how many of each are needed.

Read the script

Every product has a recipe. In manufacturing, it's called a bill of materials.

A bill of materials, or BOM, lists every component in a product, and how many of each are needed.

Example: a bicycle needs one frame, two wheels, one chain and one seat. And each wheel has its own sub-assembly: a rim, spokes and a tire. That's a multi-level BOM.

A routing, or operations list, describes the steps needed to make the product, which work center does each step, and how long each should take.

Together they drive standard costing. Materials from the BOM, plus labor and machine time from the routing, give the standard cost of the product.

Because so much depends on them, changes to BOMs and routings need approval and a change log. One wrong quantity can misprice every unit.

So the BOM says what's in it, and the routing says how it's made.

Quick check. Which document describes the steps and work centers used to make a product? Pause and pick.

The routing, or operations list.

Microteach 8.3 · 1:20MRP II versus lean and JIT

Manufacturing resource planning, or MRP two, is a push approach.

Read the script

Should a factory build to a forecast, or only when a customer orders? Two philosophies.

Manufacturing resource planning, or MRP two, is a push approach. It plans production from sales forecasts, then pushes products through the factory.

Lean manufacturing, with just-in-time inventory, is a pull approach. Products are made only in response to actual demand, and materials arrive just as they're needed.

The trade-offs. Push means longer production runs and more inventory, with the risk the forecast is wrong. Pull means small batches and little inventory, with the risk that one late supplier stops the line.

Just-in-time often uses kanban: a card or electronic signal telling the previous step to make or move more.

Lean's roots are in the Toyota Production System. Most modern ERPs support both push and pull planning.

So: push to a forecast, or pull to demand. Many companies blend the two.

Quick check. Producing only in response to actual customer demand describes which approach? Pause and pick.

Lean, or just-in-time: a pull approach.

Microteach 8.4 · 1:23Cost accounting systems

There are three main approaches: job-order costing, process costing, and activity-based costing.

Read the script

Two factories can make the same product and report different costs. It depends on the costing system.

There are three main approaches: job-order costing, process costing, and activity-based costing.

Job-order costing tracks costs to individual jobs or batches. It suits custom work: a print shop, a construction project, a custom machine.

Process costing averages costs across many identical units. It suits continuous production: soft drinks, chemicals, gasoline.

Activity-based costing assigns overhead using the activities that actually cause it, like machine setups or inspections, instead of one broad rate such as labor hours. It gives more accurate product costs.

Each system needs data from the AIS: materials used, labor time from time tickets, machine hours, and for ABC, counts of activities like setups. Barcode and IoT data make this easier.

So match the costing system to how the product is made.

Quick check. Which costing system would a soft drink bottler typically use? Pause and pick.

Process costing: many identical units in continuous production.

Microteach 8.5 · 1:14Inventory controls

Inventory faces four big threats: theft, damage or obsolescence, errors in the records, and holding too much or too little.

Read the script

Inventory is cash sitting on a shelf. And cash on a shelf can walk away.

Inventory faces four big threats: theft, damage or obsolescence, errors in the records, and holding too much or too little.

Physical controls: restrict access to storerooms, release materials only against an approved requisition, use cameras, and document every transfer.

The key detective control is a physical count: compare what's on the shelf to what the system says. Differences are investigated, then adjusted.

Companies may do one annual wall to wall count, or cycle count a few items every day, focusing on high-value items. RFID tags and barcodes make counting faster and more accurate.

Segregation applies here too: the people who count shouldn't be the people who look after the stock.

So the core question is always: does the shelf match the system?

Quick check. Counting a few inventory items every day, instead of everything once a year, is called what? Pause and pick.

Cycle counting.

Microteach 8.6 · 1:04Smart factories and IoT data

The Internet of Things means sensors on machines, parts and products, constantly sending data over the network.

Read the script

In a smart factory, the machines report their own costs.

The Internet of Things means sensors on machines, parts and products, constantly sending data over the network.

That data shows machine run time and downtime, units produced in real time, scrap and defect rates, and energy used per unit.

For accountants, that means real-time costing: actual costs as production happens, instead of waiting for month end.

It also enables new measures: overall equipment effectiveness, first pass yield, cycle time, and predictive maintenance that fixes machines before they fail.

But every sensor is a network device, so cybersecurity becomes part of production control.

So in Industry four point oh, the factory floor feeds the AIS directly.

Quick check. What's a key accounting benefit of IoT data? Pause and pick.

Real-time costing.

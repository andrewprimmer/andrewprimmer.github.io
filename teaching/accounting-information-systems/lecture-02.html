Microteach 2.1 · 1:35Data flow diagrams

A data flow diagram, or DFD, shows how data moves through a system: where it comes from, what happens to it, and where it ends up.

Read the script

One picture can explain a whole accounting process. Meet the data flow diagram.

A data flow diagram, or DFD, shows how data moves through a system: where it comes from, what happens to it, and where it ends up. It shows data, not physical documents or who does the work.

There are just four symbols. A square for a data source or destination, like a customer. An arrow for a data flow. A circle for a process that transforms data. And an open-ended rectangle for a data store, like a sales file.

Example. A customer sends a payment. The payment data flows into a process called process payment, which updates the accounts receivable data store.

DFDs come in levels. The context diagram shows the whole system as one process, with its outside sources and destinations. Level zero breaks that into the main processes. Lower levels zoom into each one.

One golden rule is balancing. The data flowing in and out at one level must match the level above it. No data should appear or vanish.

So a DFD answers one question: what happens to the data? Flowcharts, which come next, answer how.

Quick check. In a data flow diagram, what does an open-ended rectangle represent? Pause and pick.

A data store: somewhere data is held, like a customer file.

Microteach 2.2 · 1:34Flowchart symbols you must know

A flowchart shows how a process actually works: the documents, the steps, the order they happen in, and who or what does them.

Read the script

Auditors read flowcharts like maps. Here are the symbols you need to read them too.

A flowchart shows how a process actually works: the documents, the steps, the order they happen in, and who or what does them. Unlike a DFD, it shows physical documents and devices.

Learn these seven. A document has a wavy bottom. A trapezoid is a manual operation. A rectangle is a computer process. A diamond is a decision. A cylinder is a database. A small circle connects to another part of the page, and an oval marks the start or end.

There are three main types. Document flowcharts follow documents between departments. System flowcharts show inputs, processing and outputs in an information system. Program flowcharts show the logic inside a single program.

Document flowcharts are laid out in columns, one for each department or person, so you can see at a glance who touches each document.

Auditors and accountants use flowcharts to understand a process and spot weaknesses, such as a missing approval, or one person handling too many steps.

Learn seven symbols and you can read any business process on a single page.

Quick check. Which flowchart symbol represents a decision? Pause and pick.

The diamond. Each path out of it is one answer, such as yes or no.

Microteach 2.3 · 1:28Reading a document flowchart

Start at the oval: a customer order arrives.

Read the script

Let's read a real flowchart together: a sales order, from customer to invoice.

Start at the oval: a customer order arrives. In the sales column, a clerk enters the order. It flows to credit, where a diamond asks: is credit approved? If yes, the warehouse picks and ships. Finally, billing sends the invoice.

Tip one: follow the arrows, not your reading habit. Flow usually runs from top to bottom and left to right, and connectors can jump you across the page.

Tip two: track every copy. A multi-part document might send copy one to the customer, copy two to the warehouse, and copy three to a file. Where each copy ends up tells you something.

Tip three: look for the controls. Where's the approval? Who reconciles the totals? Does the person who ships the goods also bill for them? That's what auditors look for.

In our example, the key control is credit approval before shipping. Without it, goods could leave the building for customers who will never pay.

So read a flowchart like a story: who does what, with which document, in what order, and where it could go wrong.

Quick check. In our sales flowchart, what must happen before the goods are shipped? Pause and pick.

Credit approval. That's the control that protects the company from bad debts.

Microteach 2.4 · 1:36BPMN basics

BPMN stands for Business Process Model and Notation.

Read the script

Business analysts don't draw DFDs. They use BPMN. Here's the two-minute version.

BPMN stands for Business Process Model and Notation. It's an international standard for drawing business processes, and it's what most ERP and process improvement teams use.

There are four building blocks. Events are circles: a start event, like an order arriving, and an end event. Activities are rounded rectangles: the work being done. Gateways are diamonds: decisions or splits. And arrows show the sequence.

Pools and lanes show who does the work. A pool is an organization, like your company or your customer. Lanes inside it are roles or departments. Dashed arrows show messages passing between pools.

Example. A purchase request starts the process. A manager reviews it. A gateway asks: is it over five thousand dollars? If so, the CFO approves too. The process ends when a purchase order is issued.

BPMN's strength is that managers can read it, but it's precise enough for analysts to configure an ERP or automate a workflow from it.

So remember: events, activities, gateways, and lanes for who does what.

Quick check. In BPMN, what does a lane represent? Pause and pick.

A role or department. It shows who performs each activity.

Microteach 2.5 · 1:42Source documents and turnaround documents

A source document captures transaction data at its origin: a sales order, a purchase requisition, a time card, a receiving report.

Read the script

Every number in the ledger started life somewhere. Usually on a source document.

A source document captures transaction data at its origin: a sales order, a purchase requisition, a time card, a receiving report. If it isn't captured correctly here, every report downstream is wrong.

Each cycle has its own. Revenue starts with sales orders. Expenditure starts with purchase requisitions. Production uses job time tickets. Payroll uses time cards. And the general ledger uses journal vouchers.

A turnaround document is output from the system that comes back later as input. The classic example is the remittance slip on a utility bill: you send it back with your payment, already coded with your account number.

Good design reduces errors. Use prenumbered forms so missing ones stand out. Prefill what the system already knows. Scan barcodes or RFID tags instead of typing. And use online forms with edit checks.

The trend is source data automation: capturing data electronically the moment the transaction happens, like a scanner at the checkout. Paper is disappearing, but the logic is the same.

Remember: garbage in, garbage out. Controls at the point of capture matter most.

Quick check. The slip you return with a utility bill payment: what type of document is it? Pause and pick.

A turnaround document. The system sent it out, and it comes back as input.

Microteach 2.6 · 1:21Five common documentation mistakes

Here are the five most common documentation mistakes, and how to avoid them.

Read the script

Most student flowcharts make the same five mistakes. Let's fix them.

Here are the five most common documentation mistakes, and how to avoid them.

One: unlabeled arrows. Every data flow should say what is flowing, such as customer order, not just point somewhere.

Two: mixing notations. Don't put flowchart document symbols inside a DFD. Pick one technique and stick to its symbols.

Three: black holes and miracles. A process with inputs but no outputs is a black hole. One with outputs but no inputs is a miracle. Both are errors.

Four: the wrong level of detail. A context diagram shouldn't show every keystroke, and a detailed diagram shouldn't skip key steps.

Five: documents that disappear. In a flowchart, every document copy must end somewhere: sent out, filed, or destroyed.

Before you submit, check all five: labels, one notation, every process balanced, the right level of detail, and every document accounted for.

Quick check. What do we call a process that has outputs but no inputs? Pause and pick.

A miracle. Data can't come from nowhere.

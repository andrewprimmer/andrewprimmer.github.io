Microteach 3.1 · 1:29Files versus databases

In old file-based systems, each department kept its own files.

Read the script

Why did companies stop keeping separate files for every department? One word: redundancy.

In old file-based systems, each department kept its own files. Sales had a customer file. Billing had another. Shipping had a third.

That caused problems. The same data was stored many times. Copies got out of sync, so a customer's address might differ by department. Combining data for a report was hard. And every program depended on its own file format.

A database solves this by storing data once, in a shared, integrated pool, managed by a database management system, or DBMS.

The benefits: data is integrated across functions, shared by many users, stored with minimal redundancy, and independent of the programs that use it. That makes cross-functional analysis possible.

Example. A customer moves. In a database, you update the address once, and sales, billing and shipping all see the new one immediately.

Store once, use everywhere. That idea is the foundation of every ERP system in this course.

Quick check. What's the main problem with separate department files? Pause and pick.

Data redundancy: the same data stored many times, which leads to inconsistencies.

Microteach 3.2 · 1:31Tables, keys and relationships

A relational database stores data in two-dimensional tables.

Read the script

Every ERP system is built from tables. Here's how they link together.

A relational database stores data in two-dimensional tables. Each row is one record, like one customer. Each column is an attribute, like the customer's name or credit limit.

Every table needs a primary key: an attribute that uniquely identifies each row. For customers, it's a customer number, never a name, because names aren't unique.

A foreign key is an attribute that's the primary key of another table. It's how tables link.

Example. The customers table has customer number as its primary key. The sales table has invoice number as its primary key, plus customer number as a foreign key. That foreign key tells you which customer made each sale.

Two rules keep the data clean. Entity integrity: a primary key can never be blank. Referential integrity: a foreign key must match a real primary key. You can't record a sale to a customer who doesn't exist.

So: rows are records, columns are attributes, primary keys identify, and foreign keys link.

Quick check. Recording a sale to a customer number that doesn't exist breaks which rule? Pause and pick.

Referential integrity. Every foreign key must match an existing record.

Microteach 3.3 · 1:29The REA model

The REA data model, developed by William McCarthy, designs accounting databases around three things: resources, events and agents.

Read the script

Accountants have their own way to design databases. It's called REA.

The REA data model, developed by William McCarthy, designs accounting databases around three things: resources, events and agents.

Resources are things of economic value, like cash and inventory. Events are business activities management wants to track, like a sale or a payment. Agents are the people and organizations involved: customers, employees and suppliers.

Take a sale. The sale event decreases inventory, a resource. It involves two agents: the customer and the salesperson. It's paired with another event, receiving cash, which increases the cash resource.

That's the give-to-get duality. Every event where you give something up is paired with one where you get something back. Give inventory, get cash.

REA captures what actually happened economically, not just debits and credits. You can still produce financial statements from it, plus much richer management reports.

So remember: resources, events, agents, and every give has a get.

Quick check. In the REA model, is a customer a resource, an event or an agent? Pause and pick.

An agent: a person or organization involved in an event.

Microteach 3.4 · 1:23Normalization

Cram everything into one big table and you get three anomalies.

Read the script

Badly designed tables cause strange errors. Normalization prevents them.

Cram everything into one big table and you get three anomalies. Update: change an address and you must change it in many rows. Insert: you can't add a new customer until they buy something. Delete: delete their only sale and you lose the customer entirely.

Normalization fixes this by splitting data into smaller, well-structured tables, each about one thing.

The first three normal forms matter most. First normal form: no repeating groups in a row. Second: every attribute depends on the whole primary key. Third: attributes depend on nothing but the key.

A handy way to remember it: every attribute should depend on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key.

Example. Instead of one table holding every sale with the customer's full address repeated, split it into a customers table and a sales table, linked by customer number. Now the address lives in one place.

Normalization means one fact, in one place.

Quick check. You can't add a new customer until they make a purchase. Which anomaly is that? Pause and pick.

An insert anomaly.

Microteach 3.5 · 1:22SQL for accountants

SQL, or Structured Query Language, is the standard language for querying relational databases.

Read the script

You don't need to be a programmer to ask a database a question. You need SQL.

SQL, or Structured Query Language, is the standard language for querying relational databases. Almost every ERP system sits on a SQL database.

Four clauses cover most of what accountants need. SELECT picks the columns. FROM names the tables. WHERE filters the rows. GROUP BY creates totals by category.

Here's an example. Select each customer and the sum of their sales, from the sales table, where the year is twenty twenty-six, grouped by customer. That's a sales by customer report in four lines.

JOIN combines tables on a shared key, such as linking sales to customers by customer number, so you can report sales by region.

Auditors love SQL. You can find duplicate invoices, list payments over an approval limit, match employee addresses to vendor addresses, and test the entire population instead of a sample.

With SQL, you ask the data directly, instead of waiting for someone to build you a report.

Quick check. Which SQL clause filters the rows you get back? Pause and pick.

WHERE.

Microteach 3.6 · 1:31Data warehouses and analytics

There are two kinds of system.

Read the script

Your ERP is great at recording today's sales. It isn't built to analyze ten years of them.

There are two kinds of system. Online transaction processing, or OLTP, runs the business: fast, small, constant transactions. Online analytical processing, or OLAP, analyzes the business: big queries across lots of history.

A data warehouse is a separate database holding years of historical data copied from operational systems, organized for analysis, so queries don't slow down the ERP.

Data gets there through ETL: extract it from the ERP and other systems, transform it by cleaning and standardizing it, and load it into the warehouse. Then analysts query it.

Some related terms. A data mart is a smaller warehouse for one department. A data lake stores raw data in any format, including text and images. And business intelligence tools sit on top, turning data into dashboards.

Controls still matter. If the ETL process introduces errors, every analysis built on the warehouse is wrong.

So: record in the ERP, and analyze in the warehouse.

Quick check. In ETL, what does the T stand for? Pause and pick.

Transform: cleaning and standardizing data before it's loaded.

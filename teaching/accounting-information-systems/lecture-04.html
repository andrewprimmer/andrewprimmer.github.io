Microteach 4.1 · 1:23What is ERP?

Enterprise resource planning, or ERP, is a system that integrates all of a company's business processes, from sales to manufacturing to accounting, around a single…

Read the script

Before ERP, a single customer order could be typed into five different systems.

Enterprise resource planning, or ERP, is a system that integrates all of a company's business processes, from sales to manufacturing to accounting, around a single shared database.

Every module, whether sales, purchasing, production or HR, reads and writes the same database. Enter data once, and every department sees it.

Enter a sales order once, and the ERP reserves the inventory, schedules production if needed, creates the invoice after shipping, and posts to the general ledger. No retyping.

The benefits: a single source of truth, real-time information, standardized best-practice processes, and stronger built-in controls.

But ERP has costs. It's expensive and slow to implement, complex to configure, and people often resist the changes it forces on how they work.

So ERP means one database, many modules: enter once, use everywhere.

Quick check. What is the defining feature of an ERP system? Pause and pick.

A single integrated database shared by every module.

Microteach 4.2 · 1:22From MRP to cloud ERP

Here's the timeline.

Read the script

ERP didn't appear overnight. It grew out of factory planning in the nineteen sixties.

Here's the timeline. In the nineteen sixties, material requirements planning, or MRP, calculated which materials a factory needed. In the eighties, MRP two added capacity, labor and finance. In the nineties, ERP covered the whole enterprise. In the two thousands, ERP connected to customers and suppliers online. Today, cloud ERP leads new projects.

MRP answered one question: given our production plan, which materials do we need, how many, and when?

By the nineteen nineties, companies wanted every function on one system, and vendors like SAP, Oracle and PeopleSoft grew fast. Fears about Y2K pushed many companies to replace their old systems.

Cloud ERP is hosted by the vendor, paid for by subscription, upgraded automatically several times a year, and accessible from anywhere.

So the story runs from factory planning, to enterprise-wide integration, to the cloud.

Quick check. ERP grew out of which earlier type of system? Pause and pick.

MRP: material requirements planning.

Microteach 4.3 · 1:21ERP modules and the cycles

Here's the map.

Read the script

Every ERP is sold in modules. Each one maps to a cycle you already know.

Here's the map. Sales and distribution handles the revenue cycle. Materials management handles purchasing, the expenditure cycle. Production planning handles production. Human capital management handles HR and payroll. And financial accounting handles the general ledger.

Many ERPs add more: controlling, for management and cost accounting; customer relationship management; supply chain management; and business intelligence.

ERP teams talk in end-to-end processes, not departments: order to cash, procure to pay, plan to produce, hire to retire, and record to report.

The key is integration. When a warehouse worker records a goods receipt in materials management, the ERP posts the accounting entry in financial accounting automatically.

So ERP modules mirror the transaction cycles, and that's how we'll study them for the rest of the course.

Quick check. Which module supports the expenditure cycle? Pause and pick.

Materials management, which handles purchasing and receiving.

Microteach 4.4 · 1:21Master data versus transaction data

Master data is stable reference data used over and over: customers, vendors, materials, employees, and the chart of accounts.

Read the script

Most ERP errors don't start with transactions. They start with bad master data.

Master data is stable reference data used over and over: customers, vendors, materials, employees, and the chart of accounts.

Transaction data records individual business events: a sales order, a goods receipt, an invoice, a payment.

When you create a sales order, you pick the customer and materials from master data, then enter the quantity. The ERP fills in prices, payment terms and the shipping address automatically.

That's powerful, but risky. One wrong bank account in a vendor record means every payment to that vendor goes to the wrong place.

So companies tightly control master data. They restrict who can change it, require approval for changes, review the change logs, and verify any bank detail change by phoning the vendor.

So: master data is reused, transaction data is recorded once, and master data deserves the strongest controls.

Quick check. Which of these is master data? Pause and pick.

The vendor's bank details. It's stable data reused for every payment.

Microteach 4.5 · 1:17On-premise, cloud and SaaS

There are four main options.

Read the script

Where does your ERP actually live? On your servers, or on someone else's?

There are four main options. On-premise: you run the software on your own servers. Private cloud: a provider hosts it just for you. Public cloud, or software as a service: the vendor runs one version for many customers. And hybrid: a mix.

On-premise gives you full control and deep customization, but you pay a big upfront cost, and you're responsible for upgrades, security and backups.

SaaS means a subscription, a lower upfront cost, and upgrades done for you. The trade-off: less customization, and dependence on the vendor and your internet connection.

There's a control angle too. If your ERP runs in the vendor's cloud, your auditors will want a SOC one report showing the vendor's controls work.

So the choice is a trade-off between control and convenience, and the market is moving fast toward the cloud.

Quick check. Which option usually has the lowest upfront cost? Pause and pick.

SaaS. You pay a subscription instead of buying licenses and servers.

Microteach 4.6 · 1:16Why ERP projects fail

Hershey switched on a new ERP and supply chain system just before its busiest season.

Read the script

In nineteen ninety-nine, a new ERP left Hershey struggling to ship Halloween candy.

Hershey switched on a new ERP and supply chain system just before its busiest season. Orders got stuck, deliveries were missed, and sales that quarter fell sharply.

The causes repeat across failures: unrealistic timelines, too much customization, poor data migration, too little testing, not enough training, and weak support from senior leaders.

Successful projects do the opposite. They have an executive sponsor, clear scope, clean data, thorough testing, real training and change management, and they don't go live in peak season.

There's also the go-live choice. Big bang switches everything at once: fast, but risky. Phased rolls out one module or site at a time: slower, but safer.

The lesson: ERP failures are rarely about the software. They're about people, planning and process.

Quick check. Which go-live approach is usually lower risk? Pause and pick.

Phased: rolling out one module or site at a time.

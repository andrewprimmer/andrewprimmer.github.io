Microteach 7.1 · 0:59Procure to pay in 2 minutes

The expenditure cycle, or procure to pay, mirrors the revenue cycle.

Read the script

The revenue cycle brings money in. The expenditure cycle sends it out, carefully.

The expenditure cycle, or procure to pay, mirrors the revenue cycle. Order goods and services, receive them, approve the vendor's invoice, and pay.

The documents: a purchase requisition, the purchase order, the receiving report, the vendor invoice, and finally the check or electronic payment.

Notice the mirror. Your purchase order is your supplier's sales order. Your payment is their cash collection.

The goals: buy only what's needed, at the right price, receive exactly what was ordered, and pay only valid invoices, on time, so you capture any early payment discounts.

So remember: order, receive, approve, pay.

The purchase requisition.

Microteach 7.2 · 1:12Requisitions and purchase orders

An employee raises a purchase requisition.

Read the script

Before anyone spends a dollar of company money, someone has to say yes.

An employee raises a purchase requisition. Their manager approves it. Purchasing selects an approved vendor and issues a purchase order.

The purchase order, or PO, is a legal offer to buy specific items at an agreed price. Once the vendor accepts it, it's a contract.

Controls: approval limits based on amount, an approved vendor list, competitive bids for large purchases, and an automatic budget check before approval.

Segregation matters. The person who needs the item, the person who approves it, and the person who places the order should be different people.

Modern options include e-procurement catalogs, blanket purchase orders for routine supplies, vendor-managed inventory, and purchasing cards for small items.

So: approve the need before you commit the company's money.

Quick check. What does a purchase order represent? Pause and pick.

A legal offer to buy, which becomes a contract once accepted.

Microteach 7.3 · 1:05Receiving

When a delivery arrives, the receiving clerk counts and inspects it, records a goods receipt in the ERP, and moves the goods to storage.

Read the script

Here's a sneaky control: sometimes the receiving clerk isn't told how many items to expect.

When a delivery arrives, the receiving clerk counts and inspects it, records a goods receipt in the ERP, and moves the goods to storage.

The receiving report, or goods receipt, is independent evidence of what actually arrived. It's essential for deciding whether to pay.

A blind count hides the ordered quantity from the clerk, so they must actually count, rather than just tick a box.

Threats: accepting goods that weren't ordered, wrong quantities or poor quality, theft at the dock, and receipts that are never recorded.

Receiving should be separate from purchasing, so no one can order fake goods and then confirm they arrived.

So: count it, don't assume it, and record it right away.

Quick check. In a blind count, what doesn't the receiving clerk know? Pause and pick.

The quantity that was ordered.

Microteach 7.4 · 1:17The three-way match

The three-way match compares the purchase order, showing what we ordered and at what price; the receiving report, showing what arrived; and the vendor invoice,…

Read the script

Before paying any invoice, a good system asks three documents to agree.

The three-way match compares the purchase order, showing what we ordered and at what price; the receiving report, showing what arrived; and the vendor invoice, showing what we're being billed.

If all three agree on items, quantities and prices, the invoice is approved for payment. If not, it's held.

Example. We ordered a hundred units at five dollars. Ninety arrived. The vendor bills for a hundred. The match fails, so we pay for ninety, or hold the invoice until it's resolved.

ERPs let you set tolerances, so tiny differences, like a few cents of rounding, pass automatically, while bigger ones are flagged.

Some companies go further with evaluated receipt settlement. There's no invoice at all: trusted vendors are paid automatically from the PO and the receipt.

So: purchase order, receiving report, invoice. Three documents, one decision.

Quick check. Which of these is not part of the three-way match? Pause and pick.

The sales order. That belongs to the revenue cycle.

Microteach 7.5 · 1:21Cash disbursements and positive pay

When approved invoices fall due, the ERP proposes a payment run.

Read the script

Check fraud is still a real problem. Banks offer a simple fix called positive pay.

When approved invoices fall due, the ERP proposes a payment run. The treasurer reviews and releases it, and payments go out by ACH, wire or check.

With positive pay, the company sends its bank a list of every check it issued. The bank only pays checks that match the number, date and amount. Anything else is rejected.

Other controls: pay only approved invoices, mark supporting documents as paid so they can't be reused, require dual approval for large payments, and separate invoice approval from releasing payment.

A modern threat is business email compromise. Fraudsters pose as a vendor and email new bank details. Always verify changes by calling a known contact.

Timing matters too. Terms of two ten, net thirty mean a two percent discount if you pay within ten days. Good systems capture those discounts.

So: verify, approve, and only then release the money.

Quick check. What does positive pay protect against? Pause and pick.

Altered or fake checks.

Microteach 7.6 · 1:16Expenditure fraud

Common expenditure frauds: fake shell vendors that bill for nothing, kickbacks where a buyer favors a vendor in return for cash, duplicate payments, and personal…

Read the script

The easiest way to steal from a company? Become one of its vendors.

Common expenditure frauds: fake shell vendors that bill for nothing, kickbacks where a buyer favors a vendor in return for cash, duplicate payments, and personal purchases on company accounts.

In a shell vendor scheme, an employee sets up a fake company in the vendor master file, submits invoices from it, gets them approved, and collects the payments.

ERP controls catch these. Restrict who can add vendors, match vendor addresses and bank accounts against employee records, flag duplicate invoice numbers and amounts, and review vendors paid without purchase orders.

Kickbacks are harder to spot. Use competitive bidding, rotate buyers between vendors, require conflict of interest declarations, and compare prices against the market.

Most of these frauds leave patterns in the data. That's why auditors run analytics over the entire vendor and payment files.

Quick check. A vendor sharing a bank account with an employee suggests what? Pause and pick.

A shell vendor scheme.

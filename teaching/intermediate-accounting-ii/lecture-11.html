Microteach 11.1 · 1:16The core principle

Recognize revenue for the transfer of goods or services in the amount the company expects to be entitled to.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Revenue is the biggest number on Bluestem's income statement, and the one most often misstated. One standard governs it.

The core principle of Topic 606: recognize revenue to depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers, in an amount that reflects the consideration the company expects to be entitled to in exchange.

Five steps apply it: identify the contract with the customer; identify the separate performance obligations; determine the transaction price; allocate that price to the performance obligations; and recognize revenue when, or as, each obligation is satisfied.

The standard uses the asset-liability approach from the conceptual framework: revenue arises as contract rights and obligations change, when control passes to the customer.

It was developed jointly with the IASB, so US GAAP and IFRS 15 are nearly identical, and it replaced hundreds of industry-specific rules.

Quick check. Which step comes third? Pause and pick.

It's B, determine the transaction price.

Microteach 11.2 · 1:18Contracts and performance obligations

A contract creates enforceable rights; each distinct good or service is a separate performance obligation.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Bluestem launches the Paddle Package: a kayak, two days of lessons, and a year of winter storage, for $1,800.

Step one. A contract exists if it's approved and the parties are committed, each party's rights and the payment terms are identifiable, it has commercial substance, and collection is probable. It can be written, oral or implied by business practice.

Step two: identify performance obligations. A promise is a separate performance obligation if it's distinct: the customer can benefit from it on its own or with readily available resources, and it's separately identifiable from the other promises.

All three are distinct. Customers can use the kayak without the lessons, lessons are sold by others, and storage is a separate service. So the package has three performance obligations.

If Bluestem promised to custom-fit a seat into the hull as part of building it, that wouldn't be distinct from the kayak. Highly interdependent promises are combined into one obligation.

Quick check. A contract exists only if collection is what? Pause and pick.

It's B, probable.

Microteach 11.3 · 1:29The transaction price

Include variable consideration at its expected value or most likely amount, constrained to avoid reversal.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Step three: how much will Bluestem get? It's not always the sticker price.

The transaction price is adjusted for variable consideration like rebates, bonuses and returns; significant financing components, using present value; noncash consideration, at fair value; and amounts paid to the customer, which reduce revenue.

Prairie Pedals pays $500 a kayak, but if it buys more than 1,000 this year, the price drops to $450 for all of them, retroactively. Bluestem expects it to buy 1,200, so the most likely price is $450. First-quarter revenue on 300 kayaks is $135,000, with a $15,000 refund liability.

Variable consideration is estimated using expected value, for many possible outcomes, or the most likely amount, when there are just two, like rebate or no rebate.

Then the constraint: include variable consideration only to the extent it's probable that a significant revenue reversal won't occur later. When in doubt, recognize less.

Quick check. A performance bonus: sixty percent chance of ten thousand, forty percent chance of zero. Using the most likely amount, how much is included? Pause and pick.

It's B, $10,000, if the constraint is met.

Microteach 11.4 · 1:26Allocating the price

Allocate the transaction price in proportion to standalone selling prices.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Step four: split the $1,800 Paddle Package price among the kayak, the lessons and the storage.

Allocate by relative standalone selling prices: what Bluestem would charge for each item on its own. If there's no observable price, estimate it: adjusted market assessment, expected cost plus a margin, or, in limited cases, the residual approach.

Separately, the kayak sells for $1,500, the lessons for $300 and storage for $200: $2,000 in total. The package price is ninety percent of that, so each gets ninety percent of its standalone price: $1,350, $270 and $180.

The $200 package discount is spread across all three obligations, unless there's clear evidence it relates to only some of them.

If the transaction price changes later, say a rebate estimate changes, the change is allocated on the same basis as the original price.

Quick check. Bundle price nine hundred; standalone prices: product eight hundred, service two hundred. Revenue allocated to the service? Pause and pick.

It's B, $180: ninety percent of $200.

Microteach 11.5 · 1:20Recognizing revenue

Recognize revenue when control transfers: at a point in time, or over time if criteria are met.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Step five: when is each part of the package earned?

Revenue is recognized when control transfers to the customer. Indicators include: Bluestem has a right to payment, the customer has legal title and physical possession, the customer bears the risks and rewards of ownership, and the customer has accepted the asset.

Revenue is recognized over time if the customer consumes the benefits as Bluestem performs, like storage; or controls the asset as it's created; or the asset has no alternative use and Bluestem has an enforceable right to payment for work to date.

For a package sold March 1: kayak revenue of $1,350 on delivery that day; lesson revenue of $270 when the lessons are taught in May; and storage revenue of $15 a month over the year of storage.

On March 1, Bluestem records $1,350 of sales revenue and $450 of unearned revenue, a contract liability, for the lessons and storage it still owes.

Quick check. Which is usually recognized over time? Pause and pick.

It's B, the storage service.

Microteach 11.6 · 1:23Contract balances and costs

Contract liabilities, contract assets and receivables; incremental costs of obtaining a contract may be capitalized.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Revenue timing and cash timing differ. The balance sheet shows the gap.

A contract liability arises when the customer pays before Bluestem performs. A contract asset arises when Bluestem has performed but its right to payment depends on something other than time, like finishing the rest of the order. A receivable is an unconditional right to payment.

Incremental costs of obtaining a contract, like a sales commission paid only if the contract is signed, are capitalized and amortized over the period of benefit. If that period is a year or less, a practical expedient allows expensing them.

Costs to fulfill a contract that aren't covered by another standard, like inventory, are capitalized if they relate directly to the contract, create resources for future performance, and are expected to be recovered.

Disclosures include revenue disaggregated by category, contract balances and their changes, the amount of remaining performance obligations, and significant judgments, like how variable consideration was estimated.

Quick check. A customer pays ten thousand in advance for services next year. At year-end, what is it? Pause and pick.

It's B, a contract liability.

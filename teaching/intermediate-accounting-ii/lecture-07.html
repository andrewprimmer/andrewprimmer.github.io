Microteach 7.1 · 1:21Convertible bonds

Convertible debt is recorded as debt; on conversion, the carrying amount moves to equity with no gain or loss.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

Bluestem wants to borrow cheaply. Investors will accept a lower rate if they can swap their bonds for shares later.

A convertible bond lets the holder exchange it for a fixed number of common shares. The conversion feature is worth something, so the interest rate is lower: Bluestem pays four percent instead of six.

Under current US GAAP, most convertible debt is recorded entirely as a liability. The conversion option isn't separated, unless special features require it.

When all bonds convert, holders receive 50,000 shares. Under the book value method, the bonds' carrying amount moves to equity: $50,000 at par and $950,000 to paid-in capital. No gain or loss, whatever the share price.

If Bluestem offers holders extra cash or shares to convert early, that sweetener is an expense, measured at the fair value of the extra consideration.

Quick check. Convertible bonds carried at five hundred thousand convert into twenty thousand one-dollar par shares worth thirty dollars. Gain or loss? Pause and pick.

It's B, none. The book value method records no gain or loss.

Microteach 7.2 · 1:34Convertible preferred and stock warrants

Warrants issued with bonds are allocated by relative fair value; the warrant portion is equity.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

Two more ways to attract investors: convertible preferred stock, and warrants.

Convertible preferred stock is equity. On conversion, the preferred stock and its paid-in capital are moved to common stock and paid-in capital. Any excess of par issued over the preferred balance reduces retained earnings.

A warrant gives the holder the right to buy shares at a set price for a period of time. Bluestem attaches detachable warrants to a bond issue as a sweetener.

Bluestem receives $500,000. Without warrants, the bonds would be worth $480,000; the warrants trade for $20,000. The proceeds are allocated by relative fair value: $480,000 to the bonds, $20,000 to paid-in capital, stock warrants.

The bonds start at $480,000, a $20,000 discount amortized as interest. If the warrants are exercised, their $20,000 joins the cash paid as paid-in capital. Nondetachable warrants aren't separated.

Quick check. Bonds with detachable warrants sell for one million twenty thousand. Fair value of the bonds alone nine hundred thousand; warrants one twenty thousand. Amount allocated to warrants? Pause and pick.

It's A, $120,000: total fair value equals the price here.

Microteach 7.3 · 1:16Stock options: measurement

Measure options at grant-date fair value and expense it over the service period.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

To keep its designers after the IPO, Bluestem grants them stock options.

An employee stock option gives the right to buy shares at a fixed exercise price, usually after a vesting period of continued service.

On January 1, 2029, Bluestem grants 20,000 options at an exercise price of $16, equal to the market price. An option-pricing model values each option at $5. Total compensation cost is $100,000, recognized over the two-year service period.

Even with no intrinsic value at grant, an option is worth something: the chance the price rises. GAAP requires measuring that fair value at the grant date. It isn't remeasured later.

Companies either estimate expected forfeitures from employees who'll leave before vesting, or account for forfeitures as they occur. It's an accounting policy choice.

Quick check. Thirty thousand options, fair value four dollars each, three-year service period. Annual compensation expense? Pause and pick.

It's B, $40,000.

Microteach 7.4 · 1:20Stock options: recording

Expense builds paid-in capital, stock options; at exercise it moves to common stock with the cash.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

Here's how the designers' options move through the books.

Each year of the service period, Bluestem records $50,000 of compensation expense and credits Paid-in Capital, Stock Options. No cash changes hands: employees are paid in equity.

When the designers exercise, they pay $16 a share, $320,000. That cash, plus the $100,000 already in Paid-in Capital, Stock Options, is credited to common stock at par and paid-in capital: $420,000 in total.

If the share price stays below $16 and the vested options expire, the expense isn't reversed. The balance moves to Paid-in Capital from Expired Stock Options.

Companies long fought expensing options. But options are pay, and leaving them out made profits look higher. Expensing them gives a faithful representation of the cost of employees.

Quick check. Options with sixty thousand in paid-in capital, stock options, are exercised for two forty thousand cash. Total credited to common stock and paid-in capital? Pause and pick.

It's B, $300,000.

Microteach 7.5 · 1:18Restricted stock and purchase plans

Restricted stock is expensed at grant-date fair value over vesting; discounted employee purchase plans may be compensatory.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

Bluestem's new CFO, Maria Okafor, prefers restricted stock to options.

Restricted stock, or restricted stock units, transfer shares to employees that vest over time and are forfeited if the employee leaves early. Unlike options, they keep value even if the price falls.

Maria receives 4,000 restricted stock units when the share price is $15: $60,000 of compensation, recognized over four years of vesting, $15,000 a year.

Employee stock purchase plans let all employees buy shares, often at a discount. If the discount is small, around five percent or less, and the plan is broad-based, it's not compensatory. Bigger discounts are compensation expense.

Stock appreciation rights pay the increase in share price. If they're settled in cash, they're a liability remeasured to fair value every period, so expense moves with the share price.

Quick check. Six thousand restricted shares, fair value twenty dollars at grant, vesting over three years. Annual expense? Pause and pick.

It's B, $40,000.

Microteach 7.6 · 1:08Midterm review: Lectures 1 to 7

Intangibles, liabilities and equity: what Bluestem owns, owes, and owes its owners.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

The midterm is next week. Here's the first half of Intermediate Accounting Two in two minutes.

We covered intangibles, R&D and goodwill impairment; current liabilities, contingencies, warranties and asset retirement obligations; bond pricing and the effective-interest method; and notes, early extinguishment and the fair value option.

Then equity: issuing shares and treasury stock; preferred stock and dividend preferences; stock dividends and splits; and dilutive securities and stock-based pay.

One principle ties the equity lectures together: transactions with owners, like buying back shares or converting bonds, never create gains or losses. Paying employees in shares does create an expense.

For the exam, practice bond amortization tables and treasury stock entries. They're perennial favorites.

Last quick check. Which of these creates an expense? Pause and pick.

It's C, employee stock options. Good luck on the midterm.

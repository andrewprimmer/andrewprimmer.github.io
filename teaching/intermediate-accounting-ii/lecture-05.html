Microteach 5.1 · 1:21The corporate form and equity

Equity separates contributed capital from earned capital; par value is a legal amount, not a market value.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

In 2028, Bluestem Outdoor goes public. Its equity section is about to get a lot more interesting.

Common shareholders have the right to share proportionately in profits and losses, to vote, including for directors, to share in assets if the company liquidates, and sometimes a preemptive right to buy new shares to keep their percentage.

Equity has two main sources. Contributed capital is what shareholders paid in: capital stock and additional paid-in capital. Earned capital is retained earnings. Accumulated OCI and treasury stock also appear.

Par value is an arbitrary legal amount per share, often a dollar or less. It has nothing to do with market value. Amounts received above par go to additional paid-in capital.

Bluestem's new charter authorizes two million shares. Shares issued are those ever sold; outstanding shares are issued shares less any bought back and held in treasury. After the IPO, 500,000 are issued and outstanding.

Quick check. Issued eight hundred thousand; treasury fifty thousand. Outstanding shares? Pause and pick.

It's B, 750,000.

Microteach 5.2 · 1:15Issuing shares for cash

Credit common stock at par and paid-in capital for the excess; issuance costs reduce paid-in capital.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Bluestem sells 250,000 new shares, $1 par, to the public at $16 each.

Gross proceeds are $4 million. Underwriting fees, legal and accounting costs total $280,000, so Bluestem receives $3,720,000.

Common stock is credited at par, $250,000. Paid-in capital in excess of par is the $3.75 million above par, less the $280,000 of issuance costs: $3,470,000. Issuance costs reduce paid-in capital; they're not an expense.

With no-par stock, the whole amount goes to common stock, unless the board sets a stated value, which works just like par.

A key principle: transactions in a company's own shares never create income or expense. They're transactions with owners.

Quick check. Ten thousand shares, five dollar par, issued at twelve dollars. Paid-in capital in excess of par? Pause and pick.

It's B, $70,000.

Microteach 5.3 · 1:25Noncash and lump-sum issuances

Use the fair value of the stock or of what's received, whichever is more clearly determinable.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Not every share is sold for cash.

Bluestem issues 10,000 shares to an inventor for a paddle-blade patent. The shares trade at $16, so the patent is recorded at $160,000, the more clearly determinable fair value.

Shares issued for services, like legal work, are recorded at the fair value of the services or the shares, whichever is clearer. Watering stock, by overvaluing what's received, inflates assets and equity.

If common and preferred shares are sold together for one price, allocate it by relative fair values. A $220,000 package of shares worth $240,000 in total: a third to common, $73,333, and two thirds to preferred, $146,667.

If only one class has a known market value, it gets that amount and the other class gets the remainder: the incremental method.

Quick check. Five thousand shares, one dollar par, trading at twenty dollars, are issued for land appraised at ninety-five thousand. Land is recorded at what? Pause and pick.

It's B, $100,000. The active market price is usually more reliable than an appraisal.

Microteach 5.4 · 1:20Treasury stock

Cost method: debit treasury stock at cost; reissue differences go to paid-in capital, never income.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

A year after the IPO, Bluestem's shares slump. The board buys some back.

Companies buy back shares to return cash to shareholders, to have shares for employee plans, to signal that they think the stock is cheap, and, less admirably, to boost EPS.

Under the cost method, Bluestem debits Treasury Stock for the $360,000 cost. Treasury stock is a contra equity account, not an asset. Those shares get no dividends and no votes.

Later, Bluestem reissues 8,000 shares at $21. Treasury stock is credited at cost, $144,000, and the $24,000 excess goes to Paid-in Capital from Treasury Stock. Not a gain.

Then it reissues 5,000 at $15, below cost. The $15,000 shortfall is debited to Paid-in Capital from Treasury Stock, which has a $24,000 balance. If that account ran out, the rest would reduce retained earnings.

Quick check. Treasury shares bought at thirty dollars are reissued at twenty-six. Where does the four dollar difference go? Pause and pick.

It's B. Never the income statement.

Microteach 5.5 · 1:14Retiring treasury shares

Retiring shares removes their par and original paid-in capital; any excess cost reduces retained earnings.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Bluestem decides to retire the 7,000 treasury shares it still holds.

They cost $18 each, $126,000. They were originally issued at $16: $1 of par and $15 of paid-in capital. Retiring them removes $7,000 of common stock and $105,000 of paid-in capital. The remaining $14,000 reduces retained earnings.

The entry debits common stock, paid-in capital and retained earnings, and credits Treasury Stock at cost. Issued shares fall by 7,000.

If shares are retired for less than their original issue price, the difference is credited to paid-in capital from retirement. Again, no income statement effect.

State laws often limit buybacks and dividends to protect creditors, sometimes by restricting retained earnings equal to the cost of treasury stock held.

Quick check. Retiring shares that cost more than their original issue price reduces what? Pause and pick.

It's B, retained earnings, after paid-in capital from those shares.

Microteach 5.6 · 1:09Presenting and analyzing equity

The statement of stockholders' equity reconciles every component; ROE and book value per share summarize it.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Here's Bluestem's equity section after a busy year.

Common stock at par, paid-in capital in excess of par, paid-in capital from treasury stock, retained earnings and accumulated OCI total $7.43 million.

Public companies present a statement of stockholders' equity, reconciling each component: shares issued and repurchased, net income, dividends and other comprehensive income.

Return on common stockholders' equity is net income over average common equity: fifteen percent. Book value per share is equity over shares outstanding, $14.77, below the share price, as is usual.

Investors price shares on expected future earnings and cash flows. Book value per share is a floor-ish reference, not a valuation.

Quick check. Net income six hundred thousand; average common equity four million. Return on common equity? Pause and pick.

It's B, fifteen percent.

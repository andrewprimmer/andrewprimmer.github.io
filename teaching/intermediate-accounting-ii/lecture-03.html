Microteach 3.1 · 1:20Bonds: the basics

A bond is a promise to pay principal at maturity plus periodic interest; terms are set in the indenture.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

To pay for Northwind and a new plant, Bluestem needs $2 million for five years. A bank loan is one option. Bonds are another.

A bond is a long-term debt arising from a promise to pay a sum at maturity, plus periodic interest at a stated rate on the face value. The terms are set out in a bond indenture.

Bonds can be secured by assets or unsecured debentures; term bonds mature on one date, serial bonds in installments; convertible bonds can become shares; callable bonds can be redeemed early; zero-coupon bonds pay no interest until maturity.

Bluestem issues $2 million of five-year bonds on January 1, 2028, paying six percent interest semiannually, on June 30 and December 31. Investors want eight percent for bonds of this risk.

The stated, or coupon, rate fixes the cash interest: three percent of $2 million, $60,000, every six months. The market, or effective, rate determines the price investors will pay.

Quick check. What are unsecured bonds called? Pause and pick.

It's B, debentures.

Microteach 3.2 · 1:25Pricing the bonds

Price = PV of principal + PV of interest at the market rate; market above stated means a discount.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

You priced a bond as an investor in Intermediate Accounting One. Now Bluestem is the issuer. Same math.

Semiannual: ten periods at four percent. The principal is worth $1,351,120 today and the ten interest payments $486,654. Investors pay $1,837,774, a discount of $162,226.

Bluestem records the bonds at their issue price: debit Cash and credit Bonds Payable for $1,837,774. Many companies use a separate Discount on Bonds Payable account; the carrying amount is the same.

The discount isn't a loss. It's additional interest: Bluestem borrows $1,837,774 and repays $2 million, on top of the $60,000 coupons. It's spread over the five years.

Bond prices are quoted as a percentage of face: these bonds sold at about ninety-one point eight nine.

Quick check. Stated rate seven percent; market rate seven percent. The bonds sell at what? Pause and pick.

It's C, par.

Microteach 3.3 · 1:14Issuing at par and between interest dates

Bonds sold between interest dates include accrued interest, returned at the next payment.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Suppose instead Bluestem sold $1 million of six percent bonds at par, but two months late, on March 1.

On June 30, every bondholder receives a full six months' interest, $30,000, even though they've held the bonds only four months. So buyers pay the accrued interest up front.

Two months of interest is $10,000. Bluestem receives $1,010,000 and credits Bonds Payable for $1 million and Interest Expense, or Interest Payable, for $10,000.

On June 30, Bluestem pays $30,000. Interest expense nets to $20,000: exactly four months of interest, the time the bonds were outstanding.

When bonds sell at par, interest expense equals cash interest each period. There's nothing to amortize.

Quick check. Five hundred thousand of eight percent bonds, interest January 1 and July 1, are sold at par on April 1. Accrued interest received? Pause and pick.

It's B, $10,000: three months.

Microteach 3.4 · 1:30Amortizing a discount

Interest expense = carrying amount × market rate; the excess over cash increases the carrying amount.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Back to Bluestem's discounted bonds. What's the interest expense on June 30?

Interest expense is the carrying amount times the market rate: $1,837,774 times four percent, $73,511. Cash paid is $60,000. The $13,511 difference is discount amortization, which raises the carrying amount to $1,851,285.

Debit Interest Expense $73,511; credit Cash $60,000 and Bonds Payable $13,511.

Six months later, interest is four percent of the higher carrying amount, $74,051. Amortization grows to $14,051. Each period, expense rises, and after ten periods the carrying amount reaches $2 million.

Straight-line would amortize $16,223 every period. It's allowed only when results aren't materially different from the effective-interest method.

Quick check. Carrying amount nine fifty thousand; market rate five percent per period; cash interest forty thousand. Discount amortized? Pause and pick.

It's B, $7,500.

Microteach 3.5 · 1:27Bonds issued at a premium

When the market rate is below the stated rate, the premium reduces interest expense over the term.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

What if rates had fallen, and investors wanted only five percent?

At a five percent market rate, ten periods at two and a half percent: the principal is worth $1,562,400 and the interest $525,124. The bonds sell for $2,087,524, a premium of $87,524.

On June 30, interest expense is two and a half percent of $2,087,524, $52,188. Cash paid is $60,000. The $7,812 of premium amortized reduces the carrying amount to $2,079,712.

Debit Interest Expense $52,188 and Bonds Payable $7,812, and credit Cash $60,000. Interest expense is less than the cash paid, because investors paid extra up front.

Either way, the carrying amount moves to face value by maturity: up from a discount, down from a premium.

Quick check. With a premium, interest expense each period is what compared with cash paid? Pause and pick.

It's B, less than cash paid.

Microteach 3.6 · 1:25Issuance costs and year-end accruals

Debt issuance costs reduce the carrying amount; accrue interest when year-end falls between payment dates.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Two practical wrinkles: the bankers' fees, and a fiscal year that doesn't match the interest dates.

Underwriting, legal and printing costs of $40,000 are deducted from the carrying amount of the debt. Bluestem receives $1,797,774 net, and that's the bonds' initial carrying amount. The costs are effectively amortized as extra interest.

Debt issuance costs aren't an asset. They're presented as a reduction of the liability, like a discount, which raises the effective interest rate slightly.

If Bluestem's fiscal year ended March 31, it would accrue three months of interest at year-end: half of the first period's $73,511, or $36,756, with half the amortization too.

Bonds are reported as long-term liabilities at their carrying amount, and moved to current in the year they mature, unless they'll be refinanced. Notes disclose rates, maturities and covenants.

Quick check. One million of bonds issued at ninety-eight, with fifteen thousand of issuance costs. Initial carrying amount? Pause and pick.

It's B, $965,000.

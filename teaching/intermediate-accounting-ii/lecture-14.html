Microteach 14.1 · 1:17Purpose and classification

Classify cash flows as operating, investing or financing; disclose significant noncash transactions.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 23.

Read the script

Bluestem earned $1.3 million in 2031. Its cash rose only $140,000. The statement of cash flows explains why.

The statement helps users assess the company's ability to generate cash, to pay dividends and debts, the reasons income and cash differ, and the investing and financing transactions of the period.

Under US GAAP, interest and dividends received and interest paid are operating. Buying and selling long-term assets and investments, and making loans, is investing. Issuing and buying back stock, borrowing and repaying principal, and paying dividends is financing.

IFRS gives more choice for interest and dividends. That's a common source of differences when comparing companies.

Significant noncash investing and financing activities, like converting bonds to stock or buying land with a note, are disclosed separately, not in the body of the statement.

Quick check. Under US GAAP, interest paid on bonds is classified how? Pause and pick.

It's A, operating. Repaying the principal is financing.

Microteach 14.2 · 1:22Direct and indirect methods

Both methods give the same operating cash flow; the indirect method starts from net income.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 23.

Read the script

There are two ways to present operating cash flows. They always give the same total.

The direct method shows major classes of operating cash receipts and payments, like cash from customers. The indirect method starts with net income and adjusts it to cash. Most companies use the indirect method, and direct-method users must also provide the reconciliation.

Under the direct method, cash from customers is sales of $9 million less the $120,000 increase in receivables: $8,880,000. You did the same conversion, in reverse, in Intermediate One.

Each direct-method line converts an income statement item to cash. Payments to suppliers, for example, adjust cost of goods sold for changes in inventory and accounts payable.

The indirect method is cheaper to prepare from balance sheet changes, and it highlights why income and cash differ. Users often prefer the direct method's clarity.

Quick check. Sales six hundred thousand; accounts receivable decreased twenty-five thousand. Cash collected from customers? Pause and pick.

It's B, $625,000.

Microteach 14.3 · 1:26Indirect method: noncash items and gains

Add back depreciation and amortization; remove gains and losses on investing and financing transactions.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 23.

Read the script

The indirect method starts with Bluestem's net income of $1.3 million.

Add back depreciation of $420,000 and amortization of $60,000: they reduced income but used no cash. Subtract the $30,000 gain on selling equipment: the whole sale proceeds belong in investing activities.

A common misunderstanding: depreciation doesn't generate cash. It's added back only because it was subtracted to get net income without any cash leaving.

Other adjustments: add back losses, like a loss on extinguishing debt; add back bond discount amortization, interest expense not paid in cash; subtract premium amortization; subtract equity-method income in excess of dividends received; and add back deferred tax expense.

Stock-based compensation is another big add-back for public companies. Bluestem's $50,000 of option expense from Lecture 7 would be added back too.

Quick check. Net income five hundred thousand; depreciation eighty thousand; loss on sale of land fifteen thousand. Subtotal before working capital changes? Pause and pick.

It's B, $595,000.

Microteach 14.4 · 1:21Indirect method: working capital

Increases in current operating assets reduce cash; increases in current operating liabilities increase it.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 23.

Read the script

Step two: changes in current operating assets and liabilities.

Receivables rose $120,000: sales not yet collected, so subtract. Inventory rose $200,000: cash spent on stock, subtract. Prepaids fell $10,000: add. Payables rose $90,000: costs not yet paid, add. Accrued liabilities fell $20,000: subtract. Net cash from operations: $1,510,000.

The rule: an increase in a current operating asset is subtracted; a decrease is added. An increase in a current operating liability is added; a decrease is subtracted.

Watch out: not every current account is operating. Short-term notes payable to banks and dividends payable relate to financing; short-term investments may be investing.

Bluestem's operating cash flow, $1.51 million, exceeds its net income of $1.3 million. That's a healthy sign: earnings are backed by cash.

Quick check. Inventory increased forty thousand; accounts payable increased twenty-five thousand. Net adjustment to net income? Pause and pick.

It's B, minus $15,000.

Microteach 14.5 · 1:31Investing, financing and noncash activities

Analyze changes in noncurrent accounts; report sale proceeds, not gains; disclose noncash transactions.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 23.

Read the script

Now the long-term accounts. Every change tells a story.

Bluestem bought $1.6 million of equipment and sold old equipment for $110,000: its $80,000 book value plus the $30,000 gain. It bought $200,000 of debt securities. Net cash used in investing: $1.69 million.

It issued $1 million of bonds, repaid $300,000 of notes, paid $230,000 of dividends, and bought $150,000 of treasury stock: net cash from financing of $320,000.

Operating $1,510,000, investing minus $1,690,000, financing $320,000: cash rose $140,000, matching the balance sheet. That's the check every statement must pass.

During the year, $500,000 of convertible bonds were converted to stock. No cash moved, so it's disclosed in a supplementary schedule, along with cash paid for interest and income taxes.

Quick check. Equipment with a book value of fifty thousand is sold at a twelve thousand dollar loss. Investing cash inflow? Pause and pick.

It's B, $38,000.

Microteach 14.6 · 1:08Course recap: Intermediate Accounting II

Liabilities, equity, investments, revenue, changes and cash flows complete the intermediate sequence.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 23.

Read the script

Fourteen lectures in two minutes: Bluestem from private company to public one.

We started with intangibles and goodwill, then liabilities: current liabilities and contingencies, bonds and the effective-interest method, notes, early extinguishment and the fair value option.

Then equity: issuing shares and treasury stock, preferred stock and dividends, convertibles and stock compensation, and basic and diluted EPS.

Finally, debt and equity investments, revenue recognition from the five steps to long-term contracts, accounting changes and error corrections, and the statement of cash flows.

Kieso's remaining chapters, income taxes, pensions, leases and full disclosure, are usually covered in a later course or in advanced accounting. You now have the toolkit for them.

Last quick check. Which of these is a financing activity? Pause and pick.

It's B, paying dividends. Good luck on the final, and thanks for growing up with Bluestem.

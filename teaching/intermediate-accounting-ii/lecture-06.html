Microteach 6.1 · 1:15Preferred stock

Preferred shares get dividend and liquidation preferences; mandatorily redeemable preferred is a liability.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

To fund a second plant without more debt, Bluestem sells preferred stock to a regional investment fund.

Preferred stock gives its holders priority over common shareholders for dividends and in liquidation, in exchange for giving up voting rights and, usually, upside.

Features vary. Cumulative preferred carries missed dividends forward. Participating preferred shares in extra dividends with common. Convertible preferred can become common shares. Callable preferred can be bought back by the company; redeemable preferred must be.

Bluestem issues 5,000 shares of $100 par, six percent cumulative preferred stock at par, raising $500,000. The annual preferred dividend is $30,000.

Watch out: preferred stock that must be redeemed on a fixed date is a liability, not equity, and its dividends are interest expense. Substance over legal form.

Quick check. Preferred stock that must be redeemed for cash in 2033 is reported as what? Pause and pick.

It's B, a liability.

Microteach 6.2 · 0:55Cash dividends

A dividend becomes a liability when declared; the record date sets who receives it.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Bluestem's board declares its first common dividend: forty cents a share.

Three dates matter. On the declaration date, the board's vote creates a liability. The record date fixes which shareholders receive it; no entry. On the payment date, cash goes out.

On declaration, Bluestem debits Retained Earnings, or a Dividends account closed to it, and credits Dividends Payable for $201,200.

On the payment date, the liability is paid. Dividends are never an expense.

To pay a cash dividend, a company needs enough retained earnings, under most state laws, and enough cash. Profitable companies can still be short of cash.

Quick check. On which date is a dividend liability recorded? Pause and pick.

It's B, the declaration date.

Microteach 6.3 · 1:12Dividend preferences

Cumulative preferred receives arrears and the current dividend before common gets anything.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

In a bad year, Bluestem skipped the preferred dividend. This year the board declares $120,000. Who gets what?

Missed dividends on cumulative preferred are dividends in arrears. They're not a liability until declared, but they must be disclosed in the notes.

Cumulative preferred first gets last year's $30,000 of arrears, then this year's $30,000. That's $60,000. The remaining $60,000 goes to common shareholders.

If the preferred were noncumulative, the missed dividend would be lost forever: preferred would get $30,000 and common $90,000.

Participating preferred would also share in dividends above a basic rate for common. It's rare in practice.

Quick check. Six percent cumulative preferred, two hundred thousand par, two years in arrears. Dividends declared fifty thousand. Amount to common? Pause and pick.

It's B, $14,000: preferred takes three years at $12,000.

Microteach 6.4 · 1:09Property and liquidating dividends

Property dividends are recorded at fair value with a gain or loss; liquidating dividends return paid-in capital.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Dividends don't have to be cash.

A property dividend pays shareholders in noncash assets, like shares of another company. It's measured at the fair value of the assets distributed.

Bluestem distributes shares it owns in a supplier, carried at $50,000 but worth $65,000. First it remeasures them to fair value, recognizing a $15,000 gain. Then it declares a $65,000 property dividend.

A liquidating dividend returns contributed capital, not earnings. It's debited to paid-in capital, and shareholders must be told the source. Mining companies winding down a mine often pay them.

Any dividend not based on retained earnings is a return of capital and must be identified as such.

Quick check. Land carried at eighty thousand, fair value seventy thousand, is distributed as a dividend. Result? Pause and pick.

It's B: a $10,000 loss and a $70,000 dividend.

Microteach 6.5 · 1:18Stock dividends and stock splits

Small stock dividends move fair value from retained earnings to paid-in capital; splits need no entry.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Bluestem wants to reward shareholders without spending cash. It declares a ten percent stock dividend.

Ten percent of 503,000 shares is 50,300 new shares. For a small stock dividend, under twenty to twenty-five percent, the shares' fair value of $15 each, $754,500, moves from retained earnings: $50,300 to common stock at par and $704,200 to paid-in capital.

A large stock dividend, over twenty to twenty-five percent, transfers only par value from retained earnings, because it behaves more like a split and the share price will drop.

A stock split, like two for one, doubles the shares and halves the par value. No account balances change, so there's no entry. Neither a stock dividend nor a split changes total equity or anyone's ownership percentage.

Both lower the price per share, which can broaden ownership. A stock dividend also formally capitalizes retained earnings.

Quick check. A two-for-one stock split affects total stockholders' equity how? Pause and pick.

It's C, no change.

Microteach 6.6 · 1:11Restrictions and payout

Disclose retained earnings restrictions; payout ratio shows how much of income is paid out.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 15.

Read the script

Bluestem's bond indenture limits how much it can pay in dividends.

Retained earnings can be restricted by law, for example to the cost of treasury stock held, or by contract, like Bluestem's bond covenant. Restrictions are usually disclosed in the notes rather than recorded.

Bluestem's bonds limit dividends to forty percent of net income while they're outstanding. The note discloses the restriction and how much is still available.

The payout ratio is cash dividends to common over net income available to common: $201,200 over $960,000, twenty-one percent, well within the covenant.

Growing companies like Bluestem keep most of their earnings to reinvest. Mature companies tend to pay out more.

Quick check. Net income five hundred thousand; preferred dividends fifty thousand; common dividends one thirty-five thousand. Payout ratio? Pause and pick.

It's B, thirty percent: one thirty-five over four fifty.

Microteach 2.1 · 1:28Payables and notes

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled within a year or the operating cycle; accrue interest on notes.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Bluestem owes money to suppliers, banks, employees, customers and governments. Most of it is due within the year.

A current liability is an obligation whose settlement is expected to use current assets or create other current liabilities, within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer.

Common examples: accounts payable, short-term notes payable, the current portion of long-term debt, dividends declared but unpaid, unearned revenues and customer deposits, and taxes and payroll liabilities.

On November 1, Bluestem signs a $120,000, six-month, eight percent note with its bank to fund winter production. By December 31, two months of interest, $1,600, has accrued. At maturity on May 1, Bluestem will owe $124,800.

The year-end entry accrues $1,600 of interest expense and interest payable. A zero-interest-bearing note would instead be recorded at its present value, with the discount amortized as interest.

Quick check. A ninety thousand dollar, ten percent, four-month note was signed December 1. Accrued interest at December 31? Pause and pick.

It's B, $750: one month.

Microteach 2.2 · 1:25Refinancing, deposits and gift cards

Debt refinanced before the statements are issued can be long-term; unearned revenue is a liability until earned.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Bluestem has a $500,000 note due in March. It plans to roll it into a five-year loan. Current or long-term?

Short-term debt can be classified as long-term only if the company intends to refinance it and actually does, or signs a firm, noncancelable financing agreement, before the financial statements are issued. Intent alone isn't enough.

Bluestem signs the five-year loan on February 10, before its statements are issued on March 1. The $500,000 is reported as long-term at December 31.

Bluestem's stores sold $50,000 of gift cards in December; customers redeemed $30,000 by year-end. The other $20,000 is unearned revenue, a liability, until the cards are used.

Some cards are never used. If Bluestem can estimate this breakage, it recognizes the expected unredeemed amount as revenue in proportion to the pattern of redemptions.

Quick check. Bluestem intends to refinance a note due in April but has no signed agreement when the statements are issued. How is the note classified? Pause and pick.

It's A, current. Intent alone isn't enough.

Microteach 2.3 · 1:32Sales taxes and payroll

Taxes collected for governments are liabilities; payroll has withholdings and employer taxes.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Bluestem collects money it doesn't own: sales taxes and amounts withheld from paychecks.

A Bluestem store rang up $21,200, including six percent sales tax. Sales are twenty-one two divided by one point oh six, $20,000, and sales tax payable is $1,200. Sales tax is never revenue.

Weekly gross wages are $100,000. Bluestem withholds seven point six five percent for Social Security and Medicare, $7,650, and $15,000 of federal income tax. Employees receive $77,350.

Bluestem also owes its own payroll taxes: a matching $7,650 of FICA and $2,000 of unemployment taxes. Payroll tax expense is $9,650 on top of wages.

Vacation pay is accrued as employees earn it, if the rights vest or accumulate and the amount can be estimated. Sick pay that doesn't vest may be expensed when taken.

Quick check. Receipts of fifty-three thousand five hundred include seven percent sales tax. Sales tax payable? Pause and pick.

It's B, $3,500: sales are $50,000.

Microteach 2.4 · 1:16Contingencies

Accrue probable, estimable losses; disclose reasonably possible ones; never recognize gain contingencies.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

A customer whose Bluestem kayak capsized has sued for $1 million.

A loss contingency is accrued if a loss is probable and can be reasonably estimated. If it's reasonably possible, or probable but not estimable, it's disclosed in the notes. If remote, nothing is required.

Bluestem's lawyers say a loss is probable, somewhere between $200,000 and $500,000, with no amount more likely than another. GAAP requires accruing the minimum, $200,000, and disclosing the range. If one amount were more likely, Bluestem would accrue that.

Debit Litigation Loss and credit Litigation Liability for $200,000. The notes describe the case and the possible additional $300,000.

Gain contingencies, like Bluestem's own patent suit against a copycat, aren't recognized until realized. They may be disclosed, carefully, to avoid misleading users.

Quick check. A loss is reasonably possible and estimated at eighty thousand. Treatment? Pause and pick.

It's B, disclose only.

Microteach 2.5 · 1:14Assurance-type warranties

Estimate warranty costs when the product is sold; actual repairs reduce the liability.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Every Bluestem kayak carries a two-year warranty against defects.

An assurance-type warranty promises the product meets agreed specifications. It's not a separate service, so the expected cost is accrued as an expense when the kayaks are sold.

Bluestem sold 4,000 kayaks and expects warranty costs of $25 each: $100,000 of warranty expense in 2028. Repairs during the year cost $30,000, leaving a $70,000 liability.

Accrue the $100,000 expense and liability. As repairs are made, reduce the liability and credit the cash, parts and labor used.

An extended warranty that customers can buy separately is a service-type warranty: a separate performance obligation whose revenue is deferred. You'll see it in the revenue lectures.

Quick check. Two thousand units sold; expected warranty cost thirty dollars a unit; repairs this year eighteen thousand. Year-end liability? Pause and pick.

It's B, $42,000.

Microteach 2.6 · 1:23Asset retirement obligations

Record the present value of legal retirement costs as a liability and add it to the asset.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Bluestem's lease on its paint shop requires it to remove underground solvent tanks when it leaves in ten years.

An asset retirement obligation is a legal obligation tied to retiring a long-lived asset. It's recorded at fair value, usually the present value of expected costs, when incurred.

Removal is expected to cost $80,000 in ten years. Discounted at Bluestem's six percent credit-adjusted risk-free rate, the obligation is $44,671.

Bluestem adds $44,671 to the cost of the asset and records the liability. The asset cost is depreciated over ten years, $4,467 a year.

Each year, the liability grows by accretion expense, six percent of its balance: $2,680 in year one. By year ten it reaches $80,000. Total year-one cost: $7,147.

Quick check. An ARO is recorded at fifty thousand; credit-adjusted rate eight percent. Year-one accretion expense? Pause and pick.

It's B, $4,000.

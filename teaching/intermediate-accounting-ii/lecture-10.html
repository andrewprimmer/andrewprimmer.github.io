Microteach 10.1 · 1:22Influence decides the method

Under 20%: fair value; 20–50% with significant influence: equity method; over 50%: consolidate.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Bluestem buys shares in two companies: a small stake in its aluminum supplier, and a big one in a paddle maker. Different stakes, different accounting.

The method depends on influence. With little or no influence, usually under twenty percent, use fair value with changes in net income. With significant influence, usually twenty to fifty percent, use the equity method. With control, usually over fifty percent, consolidate.

The percentages are guides. A board seat, major transactions between the companies, or shared management can give significant influence at fifteen percent. A forty percent stake may give none if another owner controls everything.

Unlike debt, equity securities don't have an available-for-sale category with OCI. Since 2018, changes in the fair value of equity investments go through net income.

Why switch methods at significant influence? Because an investor that helps steer the investee shares responsibility for its earnings, so it should report its share of them.

Quick check. Bluestem owns thirty-five percent of a company and has two of seven board seats. Which method? Pause and pick.

It's B, the equity method.

Microteach 10.2 · 1:12Fair value through net income

Record dividends as revenue and remeasure to fair value each period, with changes in net income.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Bluestem buys 5,000 shares of its aluminum supplier, a listed company, at $40 a share: a tiny stake.

Bluestem records the investment at cost, $200,000.

The supplier pays a $1 dividend: $5,000 of dividend revenue. At year-end the shares trade at $43, worth $215,000: an unrealized holding gain of $15,000.

The $15,000 gain is recognized in net income. Equity investments are volatile, so this can swing earnings, which some companies dislike.

For shares without a readily determinable fair value, like a stake in a private startup, companies can use a measurement alternative: cost less impairment, adjusted when an orderly transaction in similar shares reveals a price.

Quick check. Equity securities: cost ninety thousand; fair value eighty-four thousand. Effect on net income? Pause and pick.

It's B, a $6,000 loss.

Microteach 10.3 · 1:23The equity method

Investment grows with the investor's share of income and shrinks with dividends received.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Bluestem buys thirty percent of Northshore Paddle Company for $600,000, and gets two seats on its board.

Under the equity method, the investment is recorded at cost, then increased by the investor's share of the investee's income and decreased by dividends received.

Northshore earns $250,000 and pays $80,000 of dividends. Bluestem records thirty percent of the income, $75,000, as investment income, and its $24,000 share of dividends as a reduction of the investment. The investment ends at $651,000.

Income increases the investment. Dividends are a return of part of the investment, not income: counting them as income would let Bluestem's share of earnings be counted twice.

If Northshore loses money, Bluestem's share reduces the investment, but usually not below zero unless Bluestem has guaranteed Northshore's debts.

Quick check. A forty percent investee earns a hundred thousand and pays thirty thousand of dividends. Change in the investment account? Pause and pick.

It's B, $28,000: forty thousand less twelve thousand.

Microteach 10.4 · 1:25Paying more than book value

Excess cost is assigned to undervalued assets, which are depreciated, and to goodwill, which isn't.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Suppose Bluestem had paid $700,000 for its thirty percent of Northshore, whose book net assets were $2 million.

Bluestem's share of book value is $600,000, so it paid $100,000 extra. Northshore's equipment is undervalued by $200,000; Bluestem's thirty percent share is $60,000. The remaining $40,000 is goodwill.

The $60,000 assigned to equipment is depreciated over its ten-year remaining life, $6,000 a year, reducing investment income to $69,000. The goodwill isn't amortized.

None of this appears as separate equipment or goodwill on Bluestem's balance sheet. It's all inside the one-line investment account. That's why the equity method is called one-line consolidation.

If the investment's fair value falls below its carrying amount and the decline is other than temporary, Bluestem writes it down. The write-down isn't reversed.

Quick check. Share of investee income fifty thousand; excess cost assigned to equipment thirty thousand over five years. Investment income? Pause and pick.

It's B, $44,000.

Microteach 10.5 · 1:13Control and consolidation

With control, combine the subsidiary's statements line by line and show the noncontrolling interest.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Bluestem owns all of Northwind Canoe. That's control, and Northwind's books are consolidated with Bluestem's.

When an investor controls another company, usually by owning more than half the voting shares, it prepares consolidated statements: the parent and subsidiary combined line by line as one economic entity.

If Bluestem owned eighty percent, the other twenty percent would be a noncontrolling interest, shown within equity, with its share of income shown separately.

Control can also come through contracts rather than votes. A variable interest entity is consolidated by the party that directs its key activities and absorbs its losses or benefits. This closed a big off-balance-sheet route.

Consolidation mechanics, eliminating intercompany sales and balances, are covered in advanced accounting. Here, know when it applies.

Quick check. Bluestem owns eighty percent of a subsidiary. How is the other twenty percent reported? Pause and pick.

It's B, noncontrolling interest, within equity.

Microteach 10.6 · 1:11Comparing the methods

The same investee can produce very different investor income depending on the method.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Same investee, same year, three methods. What would Bluestem report?

Under fair value, Bluestem would report $24,000 of dividend revenue plus any change in the shares' fair value. Under the equity method, $75,000 of investment income. Under consolidation, all of Northshore's revenues and expenses, less a noncontrolling interest.

Companies can elect the fair value option for equity-method investments, reporting changes in fair value in income instead.

For significant equity-method investees, disclose the name and ownership percentage, the difference between carrying amount and underlying equity, quoted fair value if available, and summarized financial information.

Because methods produce different income, managers sometimes structure stakes around the thresholds. Analysts focus on influence, not just the percentage.

Quick check. Under the equity method, dividends received are treated how? Pause and pick.

It's B, a reduction of the investment.

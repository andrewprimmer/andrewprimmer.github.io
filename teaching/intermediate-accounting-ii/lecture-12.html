Microteach 12.1 · 1:18Sales with a right of return

Recognize revenue for goods not expected to be returned; record a refund liability and a return asset.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Bluestem lets dealers return unsold kayaks within ninety days. Some always come back.

Bluestem ships 1,000 kayaks at $500 and expects three percent, 30 kayaks, back. It recognizes revenue of $485,000 and a $15,000 refund liability. Of the $300,000 cost, $291,000 goes to cost of goods sold and $9,000 to a return asset: the right to get those kayaks back.

The entry records the receivable, revenue and refund liability, then cost of goods sold, the return asset and the reduction in inventory.

Expected returns are variable consideration, so the constraint applies and the estimate is updated each period. If Bluestem couldn't estimate returns at all, it would defer revenue until the return period ended.

The return asset is measured at the former carrying amount less expected costs to recover the products and any decline in their value.

Quick check. Sales two hundred thousand; expected returns five percent. Revenue recognized? Pause and pick.

It's B, $190,000.

Microteach 12.2 · 1:25Service-type warranties

A separately priced or extra-service warranty is a performance obligation; its revenue is deferred.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Bluestem offers kayak buyers a three-year extended warranty for $150, on top of the standard two-year assurance warranty.

An assurance-type warranty just promises the kayak works as specified: accrue the expected cost, as in Lecture 2. A service-type warranty provides an extra service, like coverage in years three through five. It's a separate performance obligation, so part of the price is deferred.

Bluestem sells 2,000 extended warranties for $300,000. The coverage starts when the standard warranty ends and runs for three years, so revenue is $100,000 a year over that period, typically straight-line.

At the sale, the $300,000 is unearned warranty revenue, a contract liability. The costs of repairs under the extended warranty are expensed as incurred.

Even a warranty bundled at no extra charge can be service-type if it promises more than assurance, like a much longer term than is standard. Then part of the product price is allocated to it.

Quick check. Extended warranties sold for ninety thousand cover three years. Revenue in the first year of coverage? Pause and pick.

It's B, $30,000.

Microteach 12.3 · 1:12Principal or agent?

A principal controls the good and reports gross revenue; an agent arranges the sale and reports net commission.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Bluestem's website starts selling camping stoves made and shipped by another company. Is a $200 stove sale $200 of Bluestem revenue?

A principal controls the good before it's transferred to the customer and reports revenue gross. An agent only arranges for another party to provide it and reports its fee or commission, net.

Indicators that Bluestem is the principal: it's primarily responsible for fulfilling the promise, it bears inventory risk, and it sets the price.

The stove maker ships the stoves, handles returns and sets the price; Bluestem never holds them. Bluestem is an agent. It reports $30 of commission revenue per stove, not $200.

The choice doesn't change profit, but gross reporting would make revenue much larger. That's why online marketplaces get close scrutiny.

Quick check. A travel website books hotel rooms for a fee and never controls the rooms. Its revenue is what? Pause and pick.

It's B, its fee: it's an agent.

Microteach 12.4 · 1:18Consignment and bill-and-hold

Consigned goods aren't sold until the consignee sells them; bill-and-hold needs strict criteria.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Bluestem ships 100 kayaks on consignment to a new dealer in Duluth.

In a consignment, the consignor keeps control of the goods until the consignee sells them to an end customer. So no revenue is recognized on shipment to the consignee.

The dealer sells 40 kayaks at $500, keeps a ten percent commission and remits $18,000. Bluestem recognizes $20,000 of revenue and $2,000 of commission expense, plus the cost of the 40 kayaks. The other 60 remain in Bluestem's inventory.

In a bill-and-hold arrangement, the customer is billed but asks Bluestem to keep the goods. Revenue is recognized only if there's a substantive reason, the goods are identified as the customer's, ready to ship, and can't be used for other customers.

And if Bluestem must, or can, buy goods back, the deal may be a lease or a financing, not a sale. Control hasn't really passed.

Quick check. Bluestem ships fifty kayaks on consignment; the consignee sells twenty. How many units of revenue does Bluestem recognize? Pause and pick.

It's B, 20.

Microteach 12.5 · 1:35Long-term contracts over time

Measure progress with cost-to-cost; recognize revenue and gross profit each period; billings vs revenue creates contract assets or liabilities.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

A state park hires Bluestem to build 200 custom rental kayaks with racks and docks. The park controls the work as it's built on site. Revenue is recognized over time.

The contract price is $900,000 and estimated total costs $720,000, for a planned gross profit of $180,000 over two years.

In year one, Bluestem incurs $288,000 of costs, forty percent of the total estimate. Under the cost-to-cost method, it recognizes forty percent of revenue, $360,000, and gross profit of $72,000.

Bluestem has billed the park $300,000. It's recognized $360,000 of revenue, so it reports a $60,000 contract asset. If billings exceeded revenue, it would be a contract liability.

If Bluestem couldn't reasonably measure progress, it would recognize revenue only to the extent of costs it expects to recover, with no profit, until it can. The old completed-contract method no longer exists under Topic 606.

Quick check. Contract price two million; estimated costs one point six million; costs to date four hundred thousand. Revenue to date? Pause and pick.

It's B, $500,000: twenty-five percent.

Microteach 12.6 · 1:19Losses on long-term contracts

Revise estimates as they change; recognize an expected loss on the whole contract immediately.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

In year two, aluminum prices soar. Bluestem's engineers now expect the park contract to cost $950,000 in total.

With total costs now $950,000 against a price of $900,000, the whole contract will lose $50,000.

The full expected loss must be recognized immediately, not spread over the remaining work. Bluestem already recognized $72,000 of profit in year one, so it reports a $122,000 loss in year two, bringing the cumulative result to the $50,000 loss.

If the contract were still profitable but less so, the change in estimate would be handled with a cumulative catch-up in the current period, not by restating year one.

Because cost estimates drive reported profit, they're a major audit focus, and a classic place for earnings management.

Quick check. Year one gross profit recognized forty thousand. In year two the contract is expected to lose fifteen thousand overall. Year two loss reported? Pause and pick.

It's B, $55,000.

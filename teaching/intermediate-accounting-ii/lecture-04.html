Microteach 4.1 · 1:32Notes payable at present value

A note with no or unreasonable interest is recorded at present value; the discount becomes interest expense.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Bluestem buys a lot for its new plant. The seller accepts a $300,000 note due in three years, with no interest.

Nobody lends for free. A note with no stated interest has interest hidden in its face. Recording the land at $300,000 would overstate the land and understate interest expense.

Bluestem's borrowing rate for similar notes is eight percent. The present value, $238,149, is the land's cost. The $61,851 discount will be recognized as interest over three years.

Year-one interest is eight percent of $238,149, $19,052, added to the note's carrying amount. Year two's interest is eight percent of the new balance, and so on to $300,000.

If the land's fair value were reliably known, say from a recent appraisal, Bluestem would use it, and the implied rate would follow. When neither is clear, impute a rate the company would pay on similar debt.

Quick check. Equipment is bought for a one hundred thousand dollar zero-interest note due in two years; imputed rate ten percent. Equipment cost? Pause and pick.

It's B, $82,645.

Microteach 4.2 · 1:21Extinguishing debt early

Gain or loss = carrying amount − reacquisition price, reported in income.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Two years after issuing its discount bonds, rates have dropped. Bluestem calls the bonds at 102.

After four semiannual periods of effective-interest amortization, the bonds' carrying amount has grown from $1,837,774 to $1,895,147.

Calling at a hundred and two costs $2,040,000. The difference from the carrying amount, $144,853, is a loss on extinguishment of debt.

Bluestem removes the bonds at carrying amount, pays $2,040,000, and records the loss in income from continuing operations. Any unamortized issuance costs would also be written off as part of the loss.

Why take a loss? Because refinancing at a much lower rate can save more in future interest than the call premium costs. The loss is the price of that saving.

Quick check. Bonds with carrying amount nine ninety thousand are retired for nine seventy-five thousand. Result? Pause and pick.

It's B, a $15,000 gain.

Microteach 4.3 · 1:25The fair value option

Companies may measure debt at fair value; changes due to own credit risk go to OCI.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

GAAP lets companies choose to measure some financial liabilities at fair value instead of amortized cost.

The fair value option is an election, made instrument by instrument when the debt is issued, and it's irrevocable. Changes in fair value are then recognized each period.

Suppose Bluestem elected the fair value option. At the end of 2028, the bonds' amortized cost is $1,865,336, but market rates have risen and their fair value is $1,820,000. The liability falls by $45,336.

The part of the change caused by general interest rates goes to net income. The part caused by Bluestem's own credit getting worse goes to other comprehensive income, so a company doesn't report profit just because it became riskier.

Companies elect it mainly to reduce volatility caused by measuring related assets at fair value and liabilities at cost. Most companies don't use it for their own bonds.

Quick check. Under the fair value option, a decline in a liability's fair value caused by the company's worsening credit goes where? Pause and pick.

It's B, other comprehensive income.

Microteach 4.4 · 1:24Troubled debt settlements

A debtor settling debt with assets recognizes a gain on disposal and a gain on settlement.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

One of Bluestem's dealers, Lakes Paddle Co., is in trouble. It owes its bank $500,000 and can't pay.

A troubled debt restructuring happens when a creditor, for economic or legal reasons related to the debtor's financial difficulties, grants a concession it wouldn't otherwise consider.

Lakes settles its $500,000 loan by giving the bank land worth $380,000, with a book value of $300,000. It records two gains: $80,000 on disposing of the land, and $120,000 on settling the debt for less than owed.

If instead the bank modifies the terms, lowering the rate or extending the term, the debtor recognizes a gain only if total future cash payments, undiscounted, are less than the carrying amount.

If Lakes owed Bluestem instead, Bluestem would treat it as a creditor and measure expected credit losses under CECL, from Intermediate One.

Quick check. Debt of two hundred thousand is settled with equipment worth one fifty thousand, book value one seventy thousand. Gain on settlement? Pause and pick.

It's B, $50,000. There's also a $20,000 loss on disposal.

Microteach 4.5 · 1:06Off-balance-sheet financing

Companies try to keep debt off the balance sheet; standards have closed many routes, so read the notes.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Bluestem's founder asks: can we borrow without the debt showing up?

Off-balance-sheet financing is an attempt to borrow money without recording the obligation, keeping debt ratios and covenants looking better.

Common forms include non-consolidated entities and joint ventures; take-or-pay contracts that commit the company to buy output; and receivables sold with recourse. Operating leases were the biggest until the lease standard put them on the balance sheet.

Standards have closed many routes: variable interest entities must be consolidated, leases are recognized, and commitments must be disclosed. So the answer to the founder is mostly no, and trying would worry lenders.

Analysts read the notes on commitments, guarantees and unconsolidated entities to find obligations that aren't in the liability totals.

Quick check. Which change brought most leases onto the balance sheet? Pause and pick.

It's B, the lease standard.

Microteach 4.6 · 1:15Presenting and analyzing long-term debt

Disclose maturities, rates, covenants and fair values; debt to assets and times interest earned measure risk.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 14.

Read the script

Bluestem's lenders and new bondholders watch two numbers closely.

Notes disclose aggregate maturities for each of the next five years, interest rates and terms, assets pledged as collateral, covenants and any violations, and the fair value of the debt.

After the bonds and the Northwind purchase, Bluestem's total liabilities are $3.6 million and total assets $7.2 million: debt to assets of fifty percent, up from about thirty-nine percent.

Times interest earned is earnings before interest and taxes over interest expense: $1.28 million over $180,000, seven point one times. Bluestem earns its interest seven times over, a comfortable cushion.

Covenants are often written in these terms. A breach can make the debt callable at once, which would also make it a current liability.

Quick check. Income before tax six hundred thousand; interest expense one fifty thousand. Times interest earned? Pause and pick.

It's B, five times: seven fifty over one fifty.

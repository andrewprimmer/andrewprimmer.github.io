Bad Debts in Six Videos

Accounts receivable: the direct write-off method and the allowance method compared, in six two-minute lessons made for your phone.

All six follow one company, Prairie Supply, and one customer that fails to pay, Dale’s Diner, so you can see the same facts run through both methods. Watch them in order the first time. When a video says pause, stop it and try the question before you see the answer.

Under each video you can open the full script if you would rather read along, or are watching somewhere you cannot play sound.

Video one · 1:38Two ways to account for bad debts

Why bad debts are a timing question, and where GAAP and the tax return each stand.

Read the script

Accounts receivable in two minutes. When a customer doesn't pay, there are two ways to account for it: the direct write-off method and the allowance method.

Prairie Supply sells on credit. Every credit sale creates an account receivable: a promise that the customer will pay later. But some customers never pay. That loss is called bad debt expense. It's a normal cost of selling on credit. Everyone agrees it's an expense. The only question is when to record it.

The direct write-off method waits. You record the expense only when a specific customer's account turns out to be uncollectible. The allowance method doesn't wait. At the end of each period you estimate the bad debts, and record the expense in the same period as the sale.

Two things are at stake. First, matching. The expense should land in the same period as the revenue it helped to earn. Second, the balance sheet. Receivables should be shown at net realizable value: the cash you actually expect to collect.

Under GAAP, you must use the allowance method, unless bad debts are immaterial. On the federal tax return it's the other way round. Most businesses must use direct write-off. So you need to know both. Next video: the direct write-off method.

Video two · 1:38The direct write-off method

A credit sale to Dale's Diner, the bankruptcy a year later, and the two problems with waiting.

Read the script

The direct write-off method. It's the simple one: do nothing until a customer's account actually goes bad.

In November of year one, Prairie Supply sells two thousand dollars of goods on credit to Dale's Diner. Debit accounts receivable two thousand, credit sales revenue two thousand. At year end, under direct write-off, there's no adjusting entry and no estimate. The receivable stays on the balance sheet at the full two thousand.

In June of year two, Dale's Diner files for bankruptcy. Prairie Supply will collect nothing. Pause here and write the journal entry. Debit bad debt expense two thousand, credit accounts receivable two thousand. The expense is recorded now, and the receivable comes off the books.

Here's the first problem. The revenue was reported in year one, but the expense lands in year two. That's poor matching. Second problem. At the end of year one, the balance sheet showed a receivable that was never going to be collected. Assets were overstated.

So when is direct write-off acceptable? Under GAAP, only when bad debts are immaterial. And it's the method most businesses must use on the tax return. It's simple, but it's late. Next video: the allowance method, which fixes the timing.

Video three · 1:35The allowance method: the estimate

The year-end adjusting entry, the contra asset, and net realizable value on the balance sheet.

Read the script

The allowance method, part one. Instead of waiting for customers to default, we estimate the bad debts at the end of the period.

At the end of year one, Prairie Supply has a hundred thousand dollars of accounts receivable. It doesn't know which customers will fail to pay. But experience says about four thousand dollars will never be collected. Pause and think. Which account do we debit? And why can't we credit accounts receivable?

Debit bad debt expense four thousand. Credit allowance for doubtful accounts four thousand. We can't credit accounts receivable, because we don't yet know which customer's account to reduce. So we use the allowance instead. The allowance is a contra asset. It has a credit balance and sits directly under accounts receivable. Some textbooks call it the allowance for credit losses.

Here's the balance sheet. Accounts receivable, one hundred thousand. Less the allowance, four thousand. That leaves ninety-six thousand: the net realizable value. It's the cash Prairie Supply actually expects to collect. And the expense is in year one, the same year as the sales. That's matching.

Estimate, expense, allowance. Next video: what happens when a customer actually defaults.

Video four · 1:24The allowance method: write-offs

Writing off Dale's Diner against the allowance, why there is no expense, and a later recovery.

Read the script

The allowance method, part two. We've made the estimate. Now a real customer defaults.

June of year two. Dale's Diner goes bankrupt, owing two thousand dollars. The allowance has a four thousand dollar credit balance. Pause and write the entry. Careful: is there an expense? Debit allowance for doubtful accounts two thousand. Credit accounts receivable two thousand. There's no expense. It was already recorded in year one.

Look at the balance sheet before and after. Receivables fall from a hundred thousand to ninety-eight thousand. The allowance falls from four thousand to two thousand. Net realizable value is ninety-six thousand both times. A write-off changes neither net receivables nor net income.

Sometimes a written-off customer pays after all. Say Dale's Diner sends the two thousand in October. It takes two entries. First, reverse the write-off. Debit accounts receivable, credit the allowance. Second, record the collection. Debit cash, credit accounts receivable.

So remember: the expense comes with the estimate, not with the write-off. Next video: how to make the estimate.

Video five · 1:47Estimating bad debts

Percentage of sales against an aging schedule, and what an existing allowance balance does to the expense.

Read the script

How big should the estimate be? There are two approaches: percentage of sales, and aging of receivables. They answer different questions.

Percentage of sales is the income statement approach. Prairie Supply had five hundred thousand dollars of credit sales, and expects zero point eight percent to go bad. Five hundred thousand times zero point eight percent is four thousand. That number is the expense itself. Any balance already in the allowance is ignored.

Aging is the balance sheet approach. Sort the receivables by how long they've been outstanding. The older the debt, the less likely it is to be collected. Current: sixty thousand at one percent. Thirty-one to sixty days: twenty-five thousand at four percent. Sixty-one to ninety days: ten thousand at eight percent. Over ninety days: five thousand at thirty-two percent. Add them up: four thousand. That's the balance the allowance needs to end with.

Here's the catch. Suppose the allowance already has a five hundred dollar credit balance before adjusting. Pause. Under aging, what's the expense? Three thousand five hundred. You only record enough to bring the balance up to four thousand. And if it had been a five hundred dollar debit balance, you'd add it: four thousand five hundred.

Sales gives you the expense. Aging gives you the ending balance. Next: the two methods side by side.

Video six · 1:34The two methods side by side

The same facts run through both methods, then the full comparison in one table.

Read the script

Direct write-off and allowance, side by side. Same company, same customers, two different sets of numbers.

Year one: Prairie Supply ends the year with a hundred thousand dollars of receivables, and expects four thousand to go bad. Year two: Dale's Diner defaults on two thousand dollars. Pause. Under each method, what's the bad debt expense in year one?

Year one expense: zero under direct write-off, four thousand under the allowance method. Year one receivables on the balance sheet: a hundred thousand, against ninety-six thousand. And when Dale's defaults in year two: two thousand of expense under direct write-off. Nothing under the allowance method, because the loss was already provided for.

So, the comparison. Timing: direct write-off records the expense when the account goes bad. The allowance method records it in the period of the sale. Matching: poor, against good. Receivables: overstated, against net realizable value. Estimates: none needed, against required. And the rules: direct write-off for the tax return, or when amounts are immaterial. The allowance method for GAAP.

Direct write-off waits for the facts. The allowance method estimates in time. That's the whole comparison. Rewatch any part you need.

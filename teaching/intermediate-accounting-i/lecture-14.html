Microteach 14.1 · 1:30Impairment of assets held for use

Test recoverability with undiscounted cash flows; if impaired, write down to fair value.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapters 10 and 11.

Read the script

Remember the fishing-boat molds? Bluestem kept them to make boats for a single dealer. Sales have collapsed.

First, an indicator, like a big drop in demand, triggers a review. Second, the recoverability test: are the expected undiscounted future cash flows less than the carrying amount? If so, the asset is impaired, and the loss is the carrying amount minus fair value.

The molds' carrying amount is $300,000. Expected undiscounted cash flows are only $240,000, so they fail the recoverability test. Fair value is $180,000, so the impairment loss is $120,000.

Bluestem debits Loss on Impairment, reported in income from operations, and credits Accumulated Depreciation. The $180,000 fair value becomes the new cost basis for depreciation.

Watch out: under US GAAP, an impairment loss on assets held for use can't be reversed, even if values recover. IFRS uses a one-step test and allows reversals.

Quick check. Carrying amount five hundred thousand; undiscounted cash flows five twenty; fair value four fifty. Impairment loss? Pause and pick.

It's B, zero. It passes the recoverability test.

Microteach 14.2 · 1:07Assets held for disposal

Assets held for sale are carried at the lower of carrying amount or fair value less costs to sell, and not depreciated.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapters 10 and 11.

Read the script

A year later, Bluestem decides to sell the molds. Different rules apply.

Once management commits to a plan to sell, the asset is reported at the lower of its carrying amount or fair value less costs to sell.

After another year of depreciation, the molds are carried at $150,000. Fair value is $140,000 and selling costs $8,000, so they're written down to $132,000: an $18,000 loss.

Assets held for sale aren't depreciated, because they're no longer being used. They're shown separately on the balance sheet.

Unlike assets held for use, later increases in fair value less costs to sell can be recognized, but only up to the losses previously recorded.

Quick check. Carrying amount eighty thousand; fair value eighty-five; costs to sell ten thousand. Value of the asset held for sale? Pause and pick.

It's B, $75,000.

Microteach 14.3 · 1:16Depletion of natural resources

Depletion = (cost + development + restoration − residual) ÷ estimated units × units extracted.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapters 10 and 11.

Read the script

Bluestem's new wooden-paddle line needs ash and maple. It buys a hardwood timber tract in northern Minnesota.

The tract cost $600,000, plus $120,000 to build access roads. The land will be worth $120,000 after harvesting, so the depletion base is $600,000. Restoration costs, if required, would be added.

The tract should yield 200,000 board feet, so depletion is three dollars a board foot. Harvesting 30,000 board feet this year gives depletion of $90,000.

Depletion is a product cost: it becomes part of the cost of the lumber inventory, and reaches cost of goods sold when the paddles are sold.

If foresters revise the estimated harvest, Bluestem revises the rate for future years, just like a change in useful life.

Quick check. Depletion base nine hundred thousand; three hundred thousand tons estimated; forty thousand extracted. Depletion? Pause and pick.

It's B, $120,000: three dollars a ton.

Microteach 14.4 · 1:26Disposals

Depreciate to the disposal date, remove cost and accumulated depreciation, recognize the gain or loss.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapters 10 and 11.

Read the script

Bluestem sells an old forklift on July 1.

The forklift cost $30,000 and had $21,000 of accumulated depreciation at January 1. First, record six months of depreciation, $2,000. Book value is then $7,000. Selling it for $8,000 produces a $1,000 gain.

Debit Cash $8,000 and Accumulated Depreciation $23,000; credit Equipment for its $30,000 cost and Gain on Disposal for $1,000.

Involuntary conversions, like a fire or a government taking land, are treated the same way: the gain or loss is the insurance or condemnation proceeds minus book value. It's reported in continuing operations, not as an extraordinary item.

A common error is forgetting to record depreciation up to the disposal date. That understates depreciation expense and overstates the loss, or understates the gain, by the same amount.

Quick check. Cost fifty thousand; accumulated depreciation thirty-eight thousand; sold for nine thousand. Gain or loss? Pause and pick.

It's B, a $3,000 loss: book value $12,000.

Microteach 14.5 · 1:10Presentation and analysis

Asset turnover and return on assets show how well PP&E and other assets generate sales and profit.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapters 10 and 11.

Read the script

Bluestem has invested heavily in molds, machines and a warehouse. Is it getting enough out of them?

Asset turnover is net sales over average total assets: $4.2 million over $3.8 million, one point one one. Each dollar of assets generated a dollar eleven of sales.

Return on assets is net income over average total assets: ten point one percent. It's profit margin, nine point one percent, times asset turnover.

Disclosures include major classes of property and accumulated depreciation, depreciation methods and expense, impairment details, assets pledged as collateral, and purchase commitments.

Turnover depends on the industry. A manufacturer like Bluestem will turn assets more slowly than its dealers. Compare with peers.

Quick check. Net sales two million; average total assets one point six million. Asset turnover? Pause and pick.

It's B, one point two five.

Microteach 14.6 · 1:23Course recap: Intermediate Accounting I

Standards, statements, present value, receivables, inventory and PP&E: the core of the balance sheet.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapters 10 and 11.

Read the script

Fourteen lectures in two minutes: how Bluestem's financial statements are built.

We began with who sets the rules and the Codification, then the conceptual framework, adjusting entries, and the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow classifications.

Then the tool that runs through intermediate accounting: time value of money, from single sums to annuities, bonds, loans and expected cash flows.

And the major operating assets: cash and receivables with expected credit losses; inventory, from what to count to FIFO, LIFO and lower of cost and net realizable value; and property, plant and equipment, from acquisition through depreciation, impairment and disposal.

In ACCT 312, Intermediate Accounting Two, Bluestem grows up: intangible assets, liabilities and bonds, stockholders' equity, EPS, investments, revenue recognition, accounting changes and the statement of cash flows.

Last quick check. Which test comes first in a US GAAP impairment review of assets held for use? Pause and pick.

It's B, recoverability using undiscounted cash flows. Good luck on the final, and thanks for paddling along with Bluestem.

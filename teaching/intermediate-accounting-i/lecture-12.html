Microteach 12.1 · 1:16The cost of equipment

Capitalize every cost needed to get the asset ready for its intended use.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Bluestem buys a new paddle-molding machine. The invoice says $250,000. The asset will cost more than that on the books.

Property, plant and equipment are tangible assets acquired for use in operations, not for resale, with lives longer than a year: land, buildings, machinery, vehicles.

Historical cost includes everything needed to acquire the asset and get it ready for its intended use: the $250,000 price, $6,000 of freight and transit insurance, $14,000 of installation and $5,000 of test runs. The machine costs $275,000.

Watch out: a $3,000 repair because the installers dropped a part isn't necessary to get the machine ready. It's an expense. So are training and later operating costs.

PP&E is recorded at historical cost because it's verifiable, and under US GAAP it isn't revalued upward later.

Quick check. Which cost is not added to a machine's cost? Pause and pick.

It's C, operator training: it's expensed.

Microteach 12.2 · 1:02Land and buildings

Land includes costs to prepare it for use; land improvements with limited lives are separate and depreciated.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Bluestem buys a lot next to its plant, with an old shed on it, for a new warehouse.

Land includes the price, legal and title costs, and costs to get the land ready: demolishing the old shed, net of $5,000 of salvage, and grading. Land costs $445,000. It isn't depreciated.

A parking lot, fencing and lighting are land improvements: they wear out, so they're recorded separately, $60,000 here, and depreciated.

A building's cost includes materials, labor and overhead, architect and permit fees, excavation for the foundation, and, as we'll see next, interest during construction.

If a company buys land with a building it intends to tear down, the whole price, plus demolition less salvage, goes to land.

Quick check. Which of these is depreciated? Pause and pick.

It's C, the parking lot, a land improvement.

Microteach 12.3 · 1:32Self-constructed assets and capitalized interest

Capitalize avoidable interest: weighted-average expenditures times the appropriate rates, capped at actual interest.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Bluestem builds the warehouse itself over 2027. Interest on borrowed money is part of its cost.

GAAP capitalizes avoidable interest: the interest Bluestem would have avoided if it hadn't spent money on construction. It's capitalized only during construction and only for qualifying assets, not routine inventory.

Weight each payment by how long it was outstanding during the year. $300,000 paid January 1 counts in full; $600,000 paid April 1 counts for nine months, and $400,000 paid October 1 for three. Weighted-average expenditures are $850,000.

The first $500,000 is financed by an eight percent construction loan: $40,000. The other $350,000 uses the ten percent rate on Bluestem's other debt: $35,000. Avoidable interest is $75,000. Actual interest is $140,000. Capitalize the lower, $75,000, and expense the rest.

Bluestem adds $75,000 to the building and expenses $65,000 of interest.

Quick check. Avoidable interest ninety thousand; actual interest seventy thousand. Interest capitalized? Pause and pick.

It's B, $70,000: never more than actual interest.

Microteach 12.4 · 1:33Lump-sum and other acquisitions

Allocate lump-sum prices by relative fair value; use fair value for stock issued and present value for deferred payments.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Bluestem buys a small competitor's plant in Redwood Falls: land, building and equipment, for one price of $1.2 million.

Total fair value is $1.5 million, but Bluestem paid $1.2 million, eighty percent. Allocate the price by relative fair value: land $320,000, building $640,000, equipment $240,000. The split matters because land isn't depreciated.

Other acquisitions: assets bought with deferred payments are recorded at present value, as in Lecture 6. Assets bought with stock are recorded at the fair value of the stock or the asset, whichever is clearer. Donated assets are recorded at fair value with revenue.

If Bluestem issued 10,000 shares worth $18 each for a lot, it would record land at $180,000, with $10,000 of common stock at par and $170,000 of paid-in capital.

Notice: the seller's book value never matters. Bluestem records what it gave up or received, at fair value.

Quick check. Price nine hundred thousand; fair values: land three hundred thousand, building seven hundred thousand. Building's cost? Pause and pick.

It's B, $630,000: seventy percent of $900,000.

Microteach 12.5 · 1:26Exchanges of nonmonetary assets

With commercial substance, record the new asset at fair value and recognize gains; without it, defer gains.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Bluestem trades in an old delivery van and pays $30,000 cash for a new one.

The old van cost $40,000 and has $28,000 of accumulated depreciation, a book value of $12,000. The dealer values it at $15,000. That's a $3,000 gain.

Does the exchange have commercial substance? That is, will Bluestem's future cash flows change because of it? A newer, bigger van with lower running costs does change them.

With commercial substance, the new van is recorded at fair value given up, $15,000 plus $30,000 cash, or $45,000. The old van and its accumulated depreciation are removed, and the $3,000 gain is recognized.

Without commercial substance, the gain is deferred: the new asset is recorded at the old book value plus cash, $42,000. Losses are always recognized immediately.

Quick check. Old asset book value twenty thousand, fair value sixteen thousand, plus fifty thousand cash. New asset cost, with commercial substance? Pause and pick.

It's B, $66,000, with a $4,000 loss.

Microteach 12.6 · 1:16Costs after acquisition

Capitalize costs that extend life or improve capacity; expense ordinary repairs.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 10.

Read the script

Five years later, the paddle-molding machine needs work. Expense or capitalize?

Costs after acquisition are capitalized if they provide future benefits: a longer life, more capacity, better quality output or lower operating costs. Otherwise, they're expensed.

Ordinary repairs are expensed. An addition, like a new warehouse wing, is capitalized. A major replacement, like a new mold heater, is capitalized, and the old part's cost removed. Rearranging the floor is usually expensed unless material and beneficial for several years.

The new heater costs $32,000. The old one cost $20,000 with $15,000 of depreciation. Bluestem removes it, recognizes a $5,000 loss, and capitalizes the new heater.

Remember Bluestem's $2,500 capitalization threshold from Lecture 2: small items are expensed regardless.

Quick check. An overhaul extends a machine's life by four years. How is its cost treated? Pause and pick.

It's B, capitalized.

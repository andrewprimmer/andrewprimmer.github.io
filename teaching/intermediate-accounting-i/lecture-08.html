Microteach 8.1 · 1:12What counts as cash

Cash includes cash equivalents; restricted cash and overdrafts need separate treatment.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 7.

Read the script

Cash looks like the easiest asset. It has more rules than you'd expect.

Cash includes coins, currency, checking and savings balances, and checks received. Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that mature within three months of purchase, like Treasury bills and money market funds.

Not cash: postdated checks and travel advances are receivables, stamps are prepaid expenses, and certificates of deposit over three months are short-term investments.

Restricted cash, like a fund set aside to repay a loan in five years, is shown separately and classified as current or noncurrent by when it will be used. Compensating balances that a bank requires are disclosed.

And a bank overdraft is usually a current liability, not a negative cash balance, unless the company has other balances at the same bank to offset.

Quick check. How is a six-month certificate of deposit reported? Pause and pick.

It's B, a short-term investment. Cash equivalents mature within three months.

Microteach 8.2 · 1:16Recognizing accounts receivable

Record receivables at the amount expected to be collected; cash discounts are variable consideration.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 7.

Read the script

Bluestem sells $20,000 of kayaks to Big Sky Paddles on terms of two ten, net thirty.

Two ten, net thirty means a two percent discount if the dealer pays within ten days, otherwise the full amount is due in thirty days. Trade discounts off a list price are never recorded at all.

Under the revenue standard, a discount is variable consideration. Bluestem's dealers almost always pay early, so it records the receivable and revenue at the amount it expects: $19,600.

If Big Sky pays the full $20,000 after the discount period, Bluestem records the extra $400 as sales discounts forfeited, a revenue item.

Expected returns work the same way: they reduce revenue at the time of sale. You'll study the details under revenue recognition in Intermediate Accounting Two.

Quick check. A fifty thousand dollar sale on one ten, net thirty; the customer is expected to pay early. Revenue recorded? Pause and pick.

It's B, $49,500.

Microteach 8.3 · 1:24Estimating credit losses

Under CECL, the allowance reflects lifetime expected credit losses; an aging schedule estimates it.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 7.

Read the script

Some dealers won't pay. Bluestem must estimate how much before it knows who.

Under the current expected credit loss model, CECL, companies record an allowance for all credit losses expected over the life of the receivables, using past experience, current conditions and reasonable forecasts. The direct write-off method isn't GAAP when losses are material.

Bluestem ages its $450,000 of receivables. Current balances need a two percent allowance, slightly late ones five percent, and so on up to seventy percent for balances over ninety days. The required allowance is $30,000.

The allowance already has an $8,000 credit balance, so the adjustment brings it up to $30,000: credit loss expense of $22,000.

Receivables are reported at $450,000 less the $30,000 allowance: a net amount of $420,000, what Bluestem expects to collect.

Quick check. The required allowance is twenty-five thousand; the allowance has a three thousand dollar debit balance. Credit loss expense? Pause and pick.

It's B, $28,000.

Microteach 8.4 · 1:02Write-offs and recoveries

Write-offs reduce the allowance and the receivable; they don't change net receivables or expense.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 7.

Read the script

In March, Lakes Paddle Co. goes bankrupt owing Bluestem $5,000.

Bluestem writes off the account: debit the allowance and credit Accounts Receivable for $5,000. No expense: the loss was already expected and expensed through the allowance.

Net receivables are $420,000 before and after the write-off. Both the receivable and the allowance fell by $5,000.

If the bankruptcy trustee later pays $2,000, Bluestem reverses that much of the write-off to restore the customer's record, then records the collection.

That's why the allowance can have a debit balance before the year-end adjustment: write-offs during the year exceeded last year's estimate.

Quick check. Writing off a three thousand dollar account under the allowance method does what? Pause and pick.

It's C, no effect on net receivables.

Microteach 8.5 · 1:30Notes receivable

Notes are recorded at present value; a zero-interest note's discount becomes interest revenue.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 7.

Read the script

Remember the dealer note from Lecture 6? Bluestem sells a fleet of rental kayaks to a resort for a $500,000 note due in four years, with no stated interest.

At a five percent market rate, the note's present value is $411,351. That's the real sale price. The $88,649 difference is interest Bluestem will earn over four years.

Bluestem records the note and the revenue at $411,351. Recording $500,000 of revenue would overstate this year's sales and understate future interest.

At the end of year one, interest revenue is five percent of $411,351: $20,568. The note's carrying amount grows each year until it reaches $500,000 at maturity.

If a note carries a stated interest rate equal to the market rate, its present value is its face, so it's simply recorded at face, with interest accrued as earned.

Quick check. A one hundred thousand dollar zero-interest note due in two years; market rate ten percent. Amount recorded? Pause and pick.

It's B, $82,645.

Microteach 8.6 · 1:22Transfers of receivables and analysis

Selling receivables can be a sale or a secured borrowing; turnover shows how fast receivables are collected.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 7.

Read the script

Bluestem needs cash before spring. It can turn receivables into cash now.

A secured borrowing uses receivables as collateral: they stay on the books and a liability is recorded. A sale, or factoring, removes them, but only if Bluestem surrenders control.

Bluestem factors $200,000 of receivables without recourse. The factor charges three percent, $6,000, and holds back five percent, $10,000, for returns. Bluestem gets $184,000 cash, records a $10,000 receivable from the factor and a $6,000 loss on sale.

How fast does Bluestem collect? Receivables turnover is net sales over average net receivables: ten point two times a year. That's about thirty-six days, comfortably within thirty-day terms plus a little slack.

If Bluestem sells with recourse, guaranteeing uncollected accounts, it can still record a sale if it gives up control, but it must record a recourse liability for expected losses.

Quick check. Net sales one point eight million; average receivables one fifty thousand. Receivables turnover? Pause and pick.

It's B, twelve times.

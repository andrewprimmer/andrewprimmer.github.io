Microteach 7.1 · 1:25Deferred annuities

Value the annuity at the start of its payments, then discount that amount back to today.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Bluestem licenses its kayak design to a Canadian maker. The licensee will pay $30,000 a year for four years, but the first payment isn't until the end of year three.

That's a deferred annuity: the payments start after more than one period has passed. Here, two years go by with no payments, then four payments arrive at the ends of years three through six.

Step one: value the four payments as an ordinary annuity as of the end of year two. The factor is three point three one two one three, so $99,364. Step two: discount that back two years, times point eight five seven three four. The license is worth $85,189 today.

Another way: take the six-period annuity factor and subtract the two-period factor, for the years with no payments. Two point eight three nine six two times $30,000 gives the same $85,189.

The timeline matters: count the periods with no payment. A common error is to discount for three years here instead of two.

Quick check. An ordinary annuity whose first payment comes at the end of year four is deferred how many periods? Pause and pick.

It's B, three.

Microteach 7.2 · 1:17Valuing a bond

Bond price = PV of principal + PV of interest, both at the market rate.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Bluestem has spare cash and is looking at a five-year, $100,000 corporate bond paying six percent interest, semiannually. Similar bonds yield eight percent. What should it pay?

A bond is two cash flow streams: the principal of $100,000 at maturity, and interest of three thousand dollars every six months. Value both at the market rate, not the stated rate.

Semiannual means ten periods at four percent. The principal is worth $67,556 today and the interest annuity $24,333. The bond's price is $91,889.

When the market rate is above the stated rate, the bond sells at a discount. When they're equal, it sells at par. When the market rate is lower, it sells at a premium.

The same math prices bonds Bluestem issues, which you'll account for in Intermediate Accounting Two.

Quick check. A bond's stated rate is seven percent and the market rate six percent. It sells at what? Pause and pick.

It's B, a premium.

Microteach 7.3 · 1:39The effective-interest method

Interest = carrying amount × market rate; the difference from cash amortizes the discount or premium.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Bluestem bought the bond for $91,889. Over five years it will receive $100,000 at maturity. The $8,111 difference is extra interest. How is it recognized?

The effective-interest method computes interest each period as the bond's carrying amount times the market rate. That gives a constant rate of return.

In the first six months, interest revenue is $91,889 times four percent, $3,676. Cash received is $3,000. The $676 difference raises the carrying amount to $92,565.

Bluestem debits Cash $3,000 and Debt Investments $676, and credits Interest Revenue for $3,676. Each period the carrying amount grows, so interest grows, until it reaches $100,000 at maturity.

Straight-line amortization, $811 a period, is simpler but gives a changing rate of return. GAAP allows it only if the results aren't materially different.

Quick check. Carrying amount ninety-five thousand; market rate five percent per period; cash interest four thousand. Discount amortized? Pause and pick.

It's B, $750: forty-seven fifty less four thousand.

Microteach 7.4 · 1:22Solving for loan payments

Payment = loan ÷ PV annuity factor; each payment splits into interest and principal.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Bluestem borrows $300,000 for five years at seven percent, repaid in equal annual payments. What's the payment?

The loan is the present value of the payments. Divide $300,000 by the five-period, seven percent annuity factor, four point one zero zero two: the payment is $73,167.

In year one, interest is seven percent of $300,000, $21,000. The rest of the payment, $52,167, reduces principal, leaving $247,833.

Debit Interest Expense $21,000 and Notes Payable $52,167, and credit Cash for the payment. Each year, interest shrinks and principal grows.

In a spreadsheet, the PMT function gives the payment, and a simple table builds the full schedule.

Quick check. What's year two interest on Bluestem's loan, with a balance of two hundred forty-seven thousand eight hundred thirty-three at seven percent? Pause and pick.

It's B, $17,348.

Microteach 7.5 · 1:21Present value measurement and expected cash flows

When cash flows are uncertain, use probability-weighted expected cash flows discounted at a risk-free rate.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

So far, cash flows were certain. What if Bluestem doesn't know how much it will pay?

GAAP's expected cash flow approach weights each possible outcome by its probability, then discounts the result. Because the risk is already in the probabilities, the discount rate is a risk-free rate.

Bluestem must clean up a leaking tank next year. There's a thirty percent chance it costs $40,000, fifty percent $60,000, and twenty percent $90,000. The expected cash flow is $60,000. Discounted at a five percent risk-free rate, the present value is $57,143.

The traditional approach uses one most-likely cash flow and a risk-adjusted rate. The expected cash flow approach handles uncertainty more transparently, and is used for things like asset retirement obligations.

Picking the rate is a judgment. Specific standards sometimes say which rate to use, like a credit-adjusted risk-free rate for asset retirement obligations.

Quick check. Sixty percent chance of ten thousand, forty percent chance of twenty thousand. Expected cash flow? Pause and pick.

It's B, $14,000.

Microteach 7.6 · 1:12Midterm review: Lectures 1 to 7

Framework, statements and present value: the foundations for the rest of the course.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 6.

Read the script

Next week is the midterm. Here's the first half in two minutes.

We started with who sets the rules: the SEC, the FASB and the Codification. Then the conceptual framework, with relevance and faithful representation, the five assumptions and the recognition principles. And we reviewed adjusting and closing entries.

Microteach 13.1 · 1:17What depreciation is

Depreciation allocates cost less salvage over useful life; it isn't a valuation.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 11.

Read the script

The paddle-molding machine cost $275,000. It will help make paddles for five years. How should its cost reach the income statement?

Depreciation is the process of allocating the cost of a tangible asset, less its salvage value, to expense in a systematic and rational way over the periods that benefit. It's cost allocation, not valuation.

The machine cost $275,000 and Bluestem expects to sell it for $25,000 at the end, so the depreciable base is $250,000. Its useful life is five years, or 100,000 machine hours.

Every depreciation plan needs three decisions: the base, the useful life, which reflects both physical wear and economic factors like obsolescence, and the method.

Useful life is often shorter than physical life. A molding machine may still run after ten years but be obsolete after five.

Quick check. Cost eighty thousand; salvage eight thousand. Depreciable base? Pause and pick.

It's B, $72,000.

Microteach 13.2 · 1:05The activity method

Depreciation per unit of use × units used: best when wear depends on use.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 11.

Read the script

A machine that sits idle wears out slowly. The activity method charges depreciation by use.

The depreciable base of $250,000 divided by 100,000 estimated hours gives two dollars fifty per machine hour.

In year one, the machine runs 24,000 hours. Depreciation is $60,000. A slow year would mean lower depreciation.

The activity method matches cost to use, so cost per paddle stays steady. Its weakness: it ignores obsolescence that happens with time, whatever the hours.

The activity base can be output, like paddles produced, instead of hours. Choose the measure that best reflects how the asset is consumed.

Quick check. Base a hundred twenty thousand; sixty thousand hours estimated; nine thousand used. Depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, $18,000: two dollars an hour.

Microteach 13.3 · 1:11Straight-line and sum-of-the-years'-digits

Straight-line spreads cost evenly; SYD accelerates it using a declining fraction.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 11.

Read the script

The most common method is the simplest. The second one front-loads the expense.

Straight-line divides the base by the life: $50,000 every year. It's used by most US companies for financial reporting.

Sum-of-the-years'-digits adds the years: one through five sum to fifteen. Year one's fraction is five fifteenths, then four, three, two and one. Depreciation falls from $83,333 to $16,667, totaling $250,000.

Accelerated methods make sense when an asset is more productive early in its life, or when repairs rise as it ages, keeping total cost per year steadier.

For long lives, use the formula: n times n plus one, over two. For five years, five times six over two is fifteen.

Quick check. Sum-of-the-years'-digits, four-year life, base one hundred thousand. Year one depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, $40,000: four tenths.

Microteach 13.4 · 1:24Declining-balance methods

Apply a constant rate to book value, ignoring salvage, but stop at salvage.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 11.

Read the script

Double-declining-balance applies twice the straight-line rate to the asset's falling book value.

The straight-line rate for five years is twenty percent; double it to forty percent. Apply it to the book value at the start of each year, without subtracting salvage.

Year one: forty percent of $275,000, $110,000. Year two: forty percent of $165,000, $66,000. Then $39,600 and $23,760. In year five, forty percent would take book value below the $25,000 salvage, so depreciation is limited to $10,640.

Watch out: salvage isn't subtracted when computing the rate's base, but book value can never go below salvage. Many companies switch to straight-line when it gives more depreciation.

Other versions use one hundred fifty percent of the straight-line rate: thirty percent for a five-year asset.

Quick check. Cost forty thousand; four-year life; double-declining-balance. Year two depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, $10,000: fifty percent of $20,000.

Microteach 13.5 · 1:22Partial periods, group methods and tax

Prorate for partial years; group and composite methods average many assets; tax depreciation differs from book.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 11.

Read the script

Real assets aren't bought on January 1. And companies own thousands of them.

If the machine were bought April 1, year-one straight-line depreciation is nine twelfths of $50,000, $37,500. The last three months fall in a sixth calendar year. Some companies use simpler conventions, like a half year in the year of purchase.

Group and composite methods depreciate many assets with one average rate: group for similar assets, composite for mixed ones. When an asset is retired, no gain or loss is recorded; the difference goes to accumulated depreciation.

For taxes, Bluestem uses MACRS, with prescribed lives and accelerated rates, plus bonus depreciation when available. Book and tax depreciation differ, creating deferred taxes, covered in later courses.

Companies disclose depreciation methods, useful lives, depreciation expense, and major classes of assets with accumulated depreciation.

Quick check. Annual straight-line depreciation twenty-four thousand; asset bought October 1. First-year depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, $6,000: three months.

Microteach 13.6 · 1:11Revising estimates

A change in life or salvage is a change in estimate: spread the remaining book value over the remaining life.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 11.

Read the script

After two years of straight-line, Bluestem's engineers say the machine will last seven years in total, with only $15,000 of salvage.

After two years at $50,000, book value is $175,000.

Subtract the new salvage of $15,000: $160,000 remains to depreciate over the five remaining years, $32,000 a year from now on.

A change in estimate is accounted for prospectively: current and future years only. Prior years aren't restated, because they used the best information available at the time.

If the effect is material, Bluestem discloses it, including the effect on income. Changing estimates just to hit a target, like the covenant case in Lecture 1, is an ethical problem.

Quick check. Book value ninety thousand after three years; new salvage ten thousand; four years remain. New annual depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, $20,000.

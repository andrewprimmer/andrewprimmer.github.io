Microteach 3.1 · 1:16The accounting cycle

Every transaction flows through the same cycle, from journal entry to post-closing trial balance.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Intermediate accounting builds on the cycle you learned in principles. Today we review it fast, then focus on where the hard judgments are: adjustments.

Transactions are journalized and posted. An unadjusted trial balance checks the ledger. Adjusting entries bring accounts up to date. Then the adjusted trial balance, the statements, and closing entries, which leave a post-closing trial balance.

Double entry hasn't changed. When Bluestem buys a $180,000 kayak mold on account, Equipment goes up with a debit and Accounts Payable up with a credit. Debits equal credits.

Remember the normal balances: assets, expenses and dividends increase with debits; liabilities, equity and revenues increase with credits.

Why adjust? Because of the accrual basis and periodicity from Lecture 2. Some events, like insurance expiring or wages building up, happen without any transaction to trigger a journal entry.

Quick check. Which step comes immediately after the adjusting entries? Pause and pick.

It's B, the adjusted trial balance.

Microteach 3.2 · 1:13Prepayments

Prepaid expenses expire into expense; unearned revenues are earned into revenue.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Prepayments: cash moved first, and the expense or revenue comes later.

On October 1, Bluestem paid $24,000 for a year of insurance. That's $2,000 a month. By December 31, three months have expired: $6,000.

The adjusting entry moves $6,000 from the asset to expense. Prepaid insurance is left at $18,000 for the nine months still covered.

On December 1, Lakes Paddle Co. paid a $30,000 deposit for a kayak order. By December 31, forty percent had been delivered, so $12,000 is earned.

Debit Unearned Sales Revenue, a liability, and credit Sales Revenue for $12,000. The other $18,000 stays a liability: Bluestem still owes kayaks.

Quick check. Rent of twelve thousand dollars for six months was prepaid on November 1. What's rent expense at December 31? Pause and pick.

It's B, $4,000: two months at $2,000.

Microteach 3.3 · 1:12Accruals

Accrued revenues and expenses record what has happened before cash moves.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Accruals: the revenue or expense has happened, but cash hasn't moved yet.

On September 1, Bluestem accepted a $50,000 note from a dealer at six percent. By December 31, four months of interest has been earned: fifty thousand times six percent times four twelfths, $1,000.

Debit Interest Receivable and credit Interest Revenue for $1,000, even though the dealer pays with the note next September.

Bluestem's weekly payroll is $20,000, paid each Friday, or $4,000 a day. December 31 falls on a Wednesday, so three days, $12,000, has been earned but not paid.

Debit Salaries and Wages Expense and credit Salaries and Wages Payable for $12,000.

Quick check. A sixty thousand dollar, five percent note was received July 1. Accrued interest at December 31? Pause and pick.

It's B, $1,500: sixty thousand times five percent times six twelfths.

Microteach 3.4 · 1:18Depreciation and the adjusted trial balance

Depreciation allocates cost over useful life; contra accounts keep the original cost visible.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Depreciation is the adjustment everyone knows and few think about. It's an allocation, not a valuation.

The kayak mold cost $180,000, has a ten-year life and no salvage value. Straight-line depreciation is $18,000 a year. We'll cover other methods in Lecture 13.

Debit Depreciation Expense and credit Accumulated Depreciation, a contra asset. The equipment account keeps showing the original $180,000 cost.

On the balance sheet, equipment of $180,000 less accumulated depreciation of $18,000 gives a book value of $162,000. Book value isn't market value.

After all adjustments, the adjusted trial balance must still balance. The income statement, retained earnings statement and balance sheet are prepared directly from it.

Quick check. Equipment costs ninety thousand dollars, salvage ten thousand, life eight years. Annual straight-line depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, $10,000: eighty thousand divided by eight.

Microteach 3.5 · 1:10Closing entries

Closing resets temporary accounts to zero and moves net income to retained earnings.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

At year-end, the revenue and expense accounts start over. Closing entries do that.

Temporary accounts, revenues, expenses and dividends, measure one period and are closed to zero. Permanent accounts, assets, liabilities and equity, carry forward.

Bluestem closes $4.2 million of revenues to Income Summary, then $3.7 million of expenses. Income Summary now holds net income of $500,000.

Close Income Summary's $500,000 credit balance to Retained Earnings. Then close Dividends of $120,000 directly to Retained Earnings. Retained earnings rose by $380,000.

The post-closing trial balance lists only permanent accounts, and proves the ledger balances before the new year begins.

Quick check. Which account appears on a post-closing trial balance? Pause and pick.

It's C, accumulated depreciation: a permanent account.

Microteach 3.6 · 1:34Reversing entries and cash-to-accrual conversion

Reversing entries simplify next period's bookkeeping; cash records convert to accrual using changes in balances.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 3.

Read the script

Two practical tools: reversing entries, and converting cash records to accrual.

On January 1, Bluestem can reverse December's $12,000 wage accrual. Then on Friday the full $20,000 payroll is debited to expense as usual, and January expense ends up at the correct $8,000. Reversing entries are optional; they just simplify routine recording.

Bluestem's online store kept cash records. It collected $900,000. Add the $40,000 increase in receivables: sales not yet collected. Subtract the $15,000 increase in unearned revenue: cash not yet earned. Accrual-basis revenue is $925,000.

Expenses work the same way. Cash paid of $600,000, plus the $8,000 increase in amounts owed, less the $5,000 increase in prepaids, gives accrual expenses of $603,000.

Going the other way, from accrual to cash, is how the operating section of the statement of cash flows is built. You'll meet it again in Intermediate Accounting Two.

Quick check. Cash collected five hundred thousand; accounts receivable fell by twenty thousand. Accrual revenue? Pause and pick.

It's B, $480,000. Some cash collected was for last year's sales.

Microteach 1.1 · 1:24Why financial reporting matters

Financial reports help investors and creditors allocate capital to the businesses that use it best.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Meet Bluestem Outdoor, a Marshall, Minnesota company that makes kayaks, tents and camping gear. It wants a bank loan this year and plans to sell shares to the public in three years. Everyone lending or investing will read its financial statements.

Financial accounting is the process of producing reports for people outside the company: investors, lenders, suppliers and regulators. Managerial accounting, which you met in cost accounting, serves people inside.

The core statements are the balance sheet, the income statement, the statement of cash flows and the statement of stockholders' equity. The notes are part of the statements, not an extra.

Why does it matter? Capital allocation. Investors and lenders have limited money. Reliable, comparable reports help them send it to the companies that will use it best, and lower the cost of capital for honest reporters.

Financial statements are general-purpose reports. They're built for the needs of investors and creditors as a group, so no single user, like Bluestem's bank, gets exactly what it would design for itself.

Quick check. Who are the primary users of general-purpose financial reports? Pause and pick.

It's B, investors and creditors.

Microteach 1.2 · 1:21Who sets the rules

The SEC has legal authority; the FASB writes US GAAP; the PCAOB oversees audits of public companies.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

US accounting rules come from a partnership between a government agency and a private standard setter.

The SEC, created in 1934, has legal authority over financial reporting by public companies. It lets the private FASB, created in 1973, write the standards. The PCAOB, set up by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, oversees audits of public companies. The AICPA represents the CPA profession.

Together they produce GAAP, generally accepted accounting principles: the standards, rules and practices US companies follow when they report.

The SEC enforces GAAP by reviewing filings and taking action against companies that break the rules. Independent auditors, like Bluestem's auditor Redwood and Hart, give an opinion on whether the statements are fairly presented.

Bluestem isn't public yet, but its bank requires audited GAAP statements anyway. A Private Company Council advises the FASB on simpler alternatives for companies like it.

Quick check. Which body writes US GAAP standards? Pause and pick.

It's C, the FASB. The SEC has the legal authority but lets the FASB set standards.

Microteach 1.3 · 1:17How standards are made

The FASB follows due process, and all authoritative GAAP sits in the Codification.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Before a standard becomes GAAP, everyone affected gets a chance to argue about it.

That's due process. The FASB adds a topic to its agenda, researches it, may issue a discussion paper, then publishes an exposure draft. Companies, auditors and investors comment and attend roundtables. Finally, the board issues an Accounting Standards Update.

Every update goes into the FASB Accounting Standards Codification, the single source of authoritative US GAAP. If it isn't in the Codification, or SEC rules for public companies, it isn't authoritative.

It's organized by topic. Topic 606 is revenue, 330 is inventory, 360 is property, plant and equipment, and 842 is leases. Topics break down into subtopics, sections and paragraphs.

Learning to search the Codification is a professional skill. The CPA exam's research questions expect it.

Quick check. What does the FASB issue at the end of due process? Pause and pick.

It's B, an Accounting Standards Update, which amends the Codification.

Microteach 1.4 · 1:14Issues in financial reporting

Standard setting is political; non-GAAP measures and the expectations gap are live issues.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Accounting rules change behavior, so setting them is never purely technical.

Standards have economic consequences. A rule that puts leases on the balance sheet or expenses stock options changes reported profit and debt, so affected companies lobby hard. The FASB tries to stay neutral: report what happened, not what someone wants to happen.

The expectations gap is the difference between what the public thinks accountants and auditors should catch and what they can reasonably do. It widens after every scandal.

Current issues include companies reporting adjusted, non-GAAP earnings; how much to use fair value instead of historical cost; faster, digital reporting; and sustainability disclosures.

When Bluestem's founder talks about adjusted EBITDA, remember: public companies must reconcile any non-GAAP measure to the closest GAAP number and not give it more prominence.

Quick check. What's the difference between what the public expects auditors to catch and what they can do called? Pause and pick.

It's B, the expectations gap.

Microteach 1.5 · 1:15IFRS and the IASB

Most of the world uses IFRS; US GAAP and IFRS are similar but differ in important places.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Bluestem sells kayaks in Canada and Europe. Its competitors there use a different rulebook.

IFRS, International Financial Reporting Standards, are written by the IASB in London and required or permitted in well over a hundred countries.

They differ in places. US GAAP allows LIFO inventory; IFRS doesn't. IFRS lets companies revalue property upward and reverse impairments; US GAAP doesn't. IFRS capitalizes some development costs that US GAAP expenses. We'll flag differences throughout the course.

The FASB and IASB worked together on revenue and leases, but convergence has slowed. The SEC accepts IFRS from foreign companies listed in the US, but US companies still use GAAP.

A common generalization: IFRS relies more on broad principles and judgment; US GAAP has more detailed rules. In practice both have plenty of each.

Quick check. Which inventory method does IFRS prohibit? Pause and pick.

It's C, LIFO.

Microteach 1.6 · 1:12Ethics in financial reporting

Pressure to hit targets creates ethical dilemmas; identify the stakeholders and the consequences.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

It's December 28th. Bluestem is $40,000 short of the profit its bank covenant requires. The founder has an idea.

He asks controller Sam Nygaard to hold $40,000 of December repair invoices and record them in January. Profit would just clear the covenant.

Work through it in steps: recognize the ethical issue, identify the stakeholders, list the alternatives, weigh the consequences for each stakeholder, then decide and act.

The stakeholders include the bank, which relies on honest covenant reporting; future investors; employees and the auditor; and Sam, whose career and future CPA license are at stake.

Recording December's expenses in January misstates both years. Sam should record them, and Bluestem should talk to the bank now. Lenders often grant waivers. They rarely forgive being misled.

Quick check. What's the first step in resolving an ethical dilemma? Pause and pick.

It's B: recognize that there's an ethical issue at all.

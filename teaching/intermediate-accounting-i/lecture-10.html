Microteach 10.1 · 1:26Why cost flow assumptions

When identical units cost different amounts, a cost flow assumption decides which costs go to COGS.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Bluestem bought touring kayaks four times this year at four different costs. It sold 700. Which costs went out the door?

Bluestem started with 200 kayaks at $400. It bought 300 at $420, 400 at $440 and 100 at $460. That's 1,000 units available, costing $428,000. It sold 700 and has 300 left.

Because the kayaks are identical, Bluestem uses a cost flow assumption: a rule for assigning the $428,000 between cost of goods sold and ending inventory. The physical flow of goods doesn't have to match.

GAAP allows four: specific identification, average cost, FIFO and LIFO. The objective is the method that most clearly reflects periodic income.

Specific identification tracks each item's actual cost. It suits unique, high-value items, like Bluestem's custom expedition canoes, but lets managers pick which costs to expense, so it's not used for identical goods.

Quick check. Goods available for sale are split between what two things? Pause and pick.

It's B, cost of goods sold and ending inventory.

Microteach 10.2 · 1:16Average cost

Weighted average (periodic) or moving average (perpetual) spreads costs evenly across units.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

The simplest compromise: every kayak costs the average.

Under weighted average with a periodic system, divide goods available, $428,000, by 1,000 units: $428 each. Ending inventory is 300 times $428, $128,400. Cost of goods sold is $299,600.

In a perpetual system, the average is recomputed after each purchase. That's the moving-average method, and it gives a slightly different answer because sales happen at different averages through the year.

Average cost smooths out price changes. When prices are rising, its cost of goods sold and ending inventory fall between FIFO and LIFO.

It's allowed under both GAAP and IFRS and is common for commodities and fungible goods like Bluestem's aluminum.

Quick check. Goods available: five hundred units costing eleven thousand dollars. Two hundred units on hand. Weighted-average ending inventory? Pause and pick.

It's B, $4,400: $22 a unit.

Microteach 10.3 · 1:20FIFO

FIFO: oldest costs to COGS, newest costs in ending inventory.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

First-in, first-out assumes the oldest kayaks are sold first.

Under FIFO, the 300 kayaks left are the newest: 100 at $460 and 200 at $440. Ending inventory is $134,000. Cost of goods sold is $428,000 less that: $294,000.

A useful fact: FIFO gives the same answer under periodic and perpetual systems, because the oldest costs leave first either way.

FIFO's strength is the balance sheet: inventory is close to current cost. Its weakness is the income statement: in rising prices, old, low costs are matched with current revenues, inflating profit with holding gains.

When prices rise, FIFO reports the highest income and so the highest income taxes of the methods.

Quick check. Beginning, a hundred units at ten dollars; purchases, a hundred at twelve and a hundred at fourteen. A hundred fifty sold. FIFO ending inventory? Pause and pick.

It's B, $2,000: 100 at $14 and 50 at $12.

Microteach 10.4 · 1:15LIFO

LIFO: newest costs to COGS, oldest costs in ending inventory; allowed in the US, not under IFRS.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Last-in, first-out assumes the newest kayaks are sold first. Almost no business really works that way. So why use it?

Under periodic LIFO, the 300 kayaks left carry the oldest costs: 200 at $400 and 100 at $420, or $122,000. Cost of goods sold is $306,000.

LIFO's argument is matching: current costs are matched against current revenues, so income excludes inventory holding gains.

And taxes. LIFO's cost of goods sold is $12,000 higher than FIFO's, so taxable income is $12,000 lower, saving $3,000 of tax at twenty-five percent.

The catch: the tax law's LIFO conformity rule requires companies using LIFO for taxes to use it in their financial statements too. And IFRS doesn't allow LIFO at all.

Quick check. With rising prices, which method gives the lowest net income? Pause and pick.

It's C, LIFO.

Microteach 10.5 · 1:16The LIFO reserve and LIFO liquidation

The LIFO reserve shows the FIFO–LIFO gap; liquidating old layers inflates income.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Many LIFO companies keep FIFO records internally. The difference has a name.

The LIFO reserve, or allowance to reduce inventory to LIFO, is the difference between FIFO and LIFO inventory: $12,000 for the touring kayaks. Companies disclose it so analysts can compare with FIFO companies.

If the reserve rises by $12,000 during the year, the adjustment increases cost of goods sold by $12,000. That's the LIFO effect.

A LIFO liquidation happens when a company sells more than it buys and dips into old layers carrying very low costs. Cost of goods sold falls, and income jumps, with a tax bill attached.

Dollar-value LIFO reduces liquidation problems by grouping similar items into pools measured in dollars and adjusted for price changes with an index. Most LIFO companies use it.

Quick check. FIFO inventory five hundred thousand; LIFO reserve eighty thousand. LIFO inventory? Pause and pick.

It's B, $420,000.

Microteach 10.6 · 1:13Choosing a method

Choose on matching, taxes, cash flow and comparability, then apply it consistently.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Bluestem's founder wants the method with the best numbers. Sam lays out the trade-offs.

Side by side: FIFO's cost of goods sold is $294,000, average $299,600, LIFO $306,000. Ending inventory moves the other way.

Weigh the income and tax effects; cash flow, since LIFO saves tax when prices rise; how current the balance sheet is; comparability with competitors and with IFRS reporters; and bookkeeping cost.

Bluestem chooses FIFO. Its costs aren't rising steadily, it may list in Toronto where LIFO is banned, and FIFO inventory gives lenders a more current balance sheet.

Whatever it chooses, consistency matters. Changing methods later is a change in accounting principle that must be justified as preferable and applied retrospectively. That's in Intermediate Two.

Quick check. Which method isn't permitted under IFRS? Pause and pick.

It's C, LIFO.

Microteach 9.1 · 1:15Classifying inventory

Manufacturers report raw materials, work in process and finished goods; merchandisers report one inventory.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Inventory is Bluestem's largest current asset: $760,000 of aluminum, half-built kayaks and finished boats.

Inventories are assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business, in production for sale, or to be used in producing goods for sale.

A merchandiser, like Bluestem's dealers, buys finished goods to resell and has one inventory account. A manufacturer like Bluestem has three: raw materials, work in process and finished goods.

Bluestem reports raw materials of $210,000, work in process of $95,000, and finished goods of $455,000: $760,000 in total, with the breakdown in the notes.

Inventory errors flow straight into cost of goods sold, gross profit, net income and current assets. Getting it right matters more for Bluestem than for almost any other asset.

Quick check. Half-built kayaks on the factory floor are reported as what? Pause and pick.

It's B, work in process.

Microteach 9.2 · 1:25Perpetual and periodic systems

Perpetual systems update inventory with every transaction; periodic systems compute COGS at period end.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Two systems keep track of inventory. Bluestem's factory uses one; its small retail shop used to use the other.

In a perpetual system, purchases go straight to Inventory and every sale records cost of goods sold. In a periodic system, purchases go to a Purchases account, and cost of goods sold is computed only at the end of the period.

Under periodic, cost of goods sold is beginning inventory plus purchases, which gives goods available for sale, less ending inventory from a physical count. For Bluestem: $680,000 plus $2.6 million, less $760,000, gives $2.52 million.

Under perpetual, a sale of kayaks for $9,600 that cost $6,000 records both the revenue and the cost of goods sold at once.

Even with perpetual records, companies count inventory at least once a year. Differences reveal theft, damage and errors, recorded as inventory over and short.

Quick check. Beginning inventory fifty thousand; purchases two hundred thousand; ending inventory seventy thousand. Cost of goods sold? Pause and pick.

It's A, $180,000.

Microteach 9.3 · 1:23Which goods to include

Inventory belongs to whoever has control: check shipping terms and consignment arrangements.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

The count on December 31 says $732,000. But not everything Bluestem owns was in the warehouse, and not everything in the warehouse is Bluestem's.

Goods in transit belong to whoever has control. Under FOB shipping point, control passes when the goods leave the seller, so they're the buyer's. Under FOB destination, they stay the seller's until delivered.

On consignment, the owner, the consignor, ships goods to a dealer, the consignee, who sells them for a commission. The goods stay in the consignor's inventory until sold.

Bluestem adds $15,000 of aluminum shipped to it FOB shipping point, and $22,000 of its kayaks sitting at dealers on consignment. It subtracts $9,000 of paddles it holds for a supplier on consignment. Correct inventory: $760,000.

Other special cases include sales with buyback agreements, which may really be financing, and sales with high rates of return. Control, not location, decides.

Quick check. Goods shipped to Bluestem FOB destination, still in transit at year-end, belong to whom? Pause and pick.

It's B, the seller.

Microteach 9.4 · 1:18Which costs to include

Product costs, including freight-in, go into inventory; selling and administrative costs are period costs.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

What goes into the cost of a kayak? More than the price on the supplier's invoice.

Product costs attach to inventory: materials, freight-in, direct labor and manufacturing overhead. Period costs, like selling expenses, administration and freight-out, are expensed when incurred.

Bluestem buys aluminum for $50,000, pays $1,800 to have it shipped in, and takes a two percent discount for paying early. Inventory cost is $50,800.

Under the gross method, purchases are recorded at the invoice price and discounts taken reduce cost. Under the net method, purchases are recorded net of the discount, and any discount missed is recorded as purchase discounts lost, a financing expense. The net method shows management's missed savings.

Abnormal costs, like spoiled materials or idle plant capacity, are expensed as incurred, not added to inventory. And interest is generally not capitalized on routinely produced inventory.

Quick check. Which cost is added to inventory? Pause and pick.

It's C, freight-in.

Microteach 9.5 · 1:12Inventory errors

An ending inventory error misstates COGS and income this year and reverses next year.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Suppose Bluestem's count had missed the $22,000 of kayaks at dealers. What goes wrong?

Understated ending inventory means overstated cost of goods sold, so 2027 income and retained earnings are understated by $22,000, before tax.

In 2028, beginning inventory is understated, so cost of goods sold is understated and income overstated by the same amount. By the end of 2028, retained earnings is correct. The error counterbalances.

Counterbalancing doesn't make the error harmless. Both years' income, ratios and trends are wrong, and a covenant could be breached in either year.

If a purchase is both unrecorded and left out of the count, cost of goods sold and income are right, but inventory and accounts payable are both understated.

Quick check. Ending inventory is overstated by ten thousand dollars. Net income before tax is what? Pause and pick.

It's B, overstated by $10,000.

Microteach 9.6 · 1:02Inventory and ethics

Inventory is a common place for fraud: cutoff, fake counts and channel stuffing.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Because inventory errors move income dollar for dollar, inventory is a favorite target for fraud.

Classic schemes include inflating the count, recording January sales in December, counting goods already shipped, and channel stuffing: pushing unordered goods onto dealers to book revenue.

Good cutoff means each purchase and sale lands in the right period. Auditors test shipments and receipts around year-end and observe the physical count.

Red flags for analysts: inventory growing much faster than sales, falling turnover, and gross margins that look too good.

Bluestem uses count teams independent of the warehouse staff, numbered count tags, and a cutoff review of the last ten shipping documents each year.

Quick check. Pushing extra unordered goods onto dealers at year-end to boost sales is called what? Pause and pick.

It's B, channel stuffing.

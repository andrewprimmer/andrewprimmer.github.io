Microteach 5.1 · 1:04What the balance sheet tells you

The balance sheet shows liquidity, solvency and financial flexibility, but omits much of a company's value.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Bluestem's bank reads the balance sheet first. It wants to know: can this company pay us back?

The balance sheet helps assess liquidity, how soon assets turn into cash; solvency, the ability to pay debts as they come due; and financial flexibility, the ability to raise cash and adapt to surprises.

But it has limits. Most assets are at historical cost, not current value. Many amounts are estimates. And some of the most valuable things, like Bluestem's brand and its designers' know-how, don't appear at all.

That's why companies often sell for much more than their book equity. When Bluestem goes public, its market value will likely be several times its book value.

And remember, it's a snapshot at one date. The income statement and cash flow statement cover a period.

Quick check. The ability to pay debts as they mature over the long term is called what? Pause and pick.

It's B, solvency.

Microteach 5.2 · 1:13Classifying assets

Current assets will be used or turned into cash within a year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

A classified balance sheet groups similar items so users can see liquidity at a glance.

Current assets are cash and other assets expected to be converted to cash, sold or consumed within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. They're listed in order of liquidity.

Typical current assets: cash and cash equivalents; short-term investments; receivables, net of the allowance for doubtful accounts; inventories; and prepaid expenses.

Noncurrent assets include long-term investments, like bonds held to maturity or land held for a future plant; property, plant and equipment, net of accumulated depreciation; intangible assets; and other assets.

Watch out: cash restricted for a long-term purpose, like a sinking fund to repay bonds in five years, isn't a current asset. Classification follows the expected use.

Quick check. Land held for a future plant site is classified as what? Pause and pick.

It's B, a long-term investment. It isn't used in operations yet.

Microteach 5.3 · 1:16Classifying liabilities and equity

Current liabilities are paid from current assets within a year; equity shows contributed capital, retained earnings and AOCI.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

The right side of the balance sheet: who has claims on the assets?

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be paid from current assets, or by creating other current liabilities, within a year or the operating cycle: accounts payable, accrued liabilities, unearned revenue, and the current portion of long-term debt.

Bluestem's term loan is repaid $100,000 a year. Next year's $100,000 is reported as current; the rest stays long-term.

Stockholders' equity includes capital stock at par value; additional paid-in capital above par; retained earnings; accumulated other comprehensive income; less treasury stock the company has bought back. Noncontrolling interests in subsidiaries also appear within equity.

Some instruments, like mandatorily redeemable preferred stock, look like equity but are liabilities. You'll sort those out in Intermediate Accounting Two.

Quick check. A five-year note has forty thousand dollars due next year. How is that forty thousand reported? Pause and pick.

It's B, a current liability.

Microteach 5.4 · 1:27Bluestem's balance sheet and ratios

Current ratio and debt to assets turn the balance sheet into liquidity and solvency measures.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Here's Bluestem's balance sheet at December 31, 2027, condensed.

Current assets total $1.4 million: cash, receivables, inventories and prepaids. Long-term investments, property and intangibles add $2.6 million. Total assets: $4 million.

Current liabilities are $650,000 and long-term debt $900,000: total liabilities $1.55 million. Equity is common stock and paid-in capital of $367,500, retained earnings of $2,062,500, and accumulated OCI of $20,000. It balances at $4 million.

The current ratio is current assets over current liabilities: two point one five, comfortable. Debt to assets is total liabilities over total assets: thirty-eight point eight percent.

But more than half of current assets is inventory. The quick ratio, cash plus receivables over current liabilities, is only point nine two. Kayaks aren't cash until they're sold.

Quick check. Current assets nine hundred thousand; current liabilities six hundred thousand. Current ratio? Pause and pick.

It's B, one point five.

Microteach 5.5 · 1:15The statement of cash flows: an overview

Cash flows are classified as operating, investing or financing; free cash flow shows discretionary cash.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Profit isn't cash. The statement of cash flows shows where Bluestem's cash actually came from and went.

Operating activities are the cash effects of earning income: collections from customers and payments to suppliers, employees and the government. Investing covers buying and selling long-term assets and investments. Financing covers borrowing, repaying, issuing stock and paying dividends.

Bluestem generated $520,000 from operations, used $410,000 in investing, mostly new molds, and $60,000 in financing: borrowing less dividends and loan repayments. Cash rose $50,000.

Free cash flow is cash from operations less capital expenditures and dividends: just $20,000. Bluestem has little cash left for new projects without borrowing.

Here we're reading the statement. Building it from scratch is a big topic in Intermediate Accounting Two.

Quick check. Paying dividends is a cash outflow from which activity? Pause and pick.

It's C, financing activities.

Microteach 5.6 · 1:18Notes and disclosure

Notes explain policies, contingencies, commitments and subsequent events; they're part of the statements.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

The numbers are only half of Bluestem's annual report. The notes often matter more.

Common notes include the summary of significant accounting policies, like Bluestem's inventory method and depreciation lives; contingencies, like lawsuits; commitments and debt terms; and events after the balance sheet date.

Subsequent events happen after the balance sheet date but before the statements are issued. If they give evidence about conditions at year-end, like a customer's bankruptcy that confirms a bad receivable, adjust the numbers. If they're new conditions, like a fire in February, disclose them.

Information can be disclosed through parenthetical explanations on the face of the statements, notes, cross-references and contra accounts, and supporting schedules.

Disclosure is not a substitute for proper recognition. If an item meets the recognition criteria, a note explaining it doesn't make the omission acceptable.

Quick check. In February, a fire destroys Bluestem's warehouse before the statements are issued. Treatment? Pause and pick.

It's B: disclose it. The fire is a new condition after year-end.

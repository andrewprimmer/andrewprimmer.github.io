Microteach 11.1 · 1:27Lower of cost and net realizable value

FIFO and average-cost inventory is written down when net realizable value falls below cost.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Bluestem has 600 fishing kayaks in last year's colors. Dealers won't pay what they cost. Inventory can't stay on the books at more than it will bring in.

Net realizable value is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, less reasonably predictable costs of completion, disposal and transportation.

Bluestem compares cost and net realizable value item by item. Touring kayaks go down $2,000 to $132,000. Fishing kayaks go down $15,000 to $45,000. Paddles stay at cost, because their value is higher. Inventory is $217,000, a write-down of $17,000.

This rule applies to inventory measured using FIFO or average cost. LIFO and the retail method use the older lower-of-cost-or-market rule, next video.

Once written down, inventory isn't written back up under US GAAP, even if prices recover. IFRS allows the write-down to be reversed, up to original cost.

Quick check. Cost five hundred dollars; selling price five sixty; costs to sell eighty. Inventory value per unit? Pause and pick.

It's B, $480, the net realizable value.

Microteach 11.2 · 1:26Lower of cost or market for LIFO and retail

Market is replacement cost, bounded by a ceiling of NRV and a floor of NRV less normal profit.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Companies on LIFO or the retail method still use a three-part test called lower of cost or market.

Market means current replacement cost, but it can't exceed a ceiling, net realizable value, or fall below a floor, net realizable value less a normal profit margin. Pick the middle of the three.

A kayak costs $400. Replacement cost is $360. The ceiling, net realizable value, is $380. The floor, after a $50 normal profit, is $330. Replacement cost, $360, falls between them, so it's market. It's lower than cost, so the kayak is valued at $360.

The ceiling keeps inventory from being valued above what it will bring in. The floor prevents writing it down so far that the next sale shows an abnormally large profit.

Both tests can be applied item by item, by category or to total inventory. Item by item gives the lowest value; whatever is chosen must be applied consistently.

Quick check. Cost fifty; replacement forty-two; NRV forty-eight; NRV less normal profit forty. Value? Pause and pick.

It's C, $42: replacement cost is the middle value and below cost.

Microteach 11.3 · 1:13Recording write-downs and purchase commitments

Write-downs go to COGS or a separate loss; losses on firm purchase commitments are recognized when prices fall.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Bluestem has to record the $17,000 write-down. There are two ways.

The cost-of-goods-sold method writes inventory down directly and buries the loss in cost of goods sold. It's simple, but it hides the size of the loss.

The loss method reports a separate loss line and uses an allowance account, so inventory records stay at cost. Users can see the write-down clearly. Bluestem uses this method.

Bluestem also signed a noncancelable contract to buy $100,000 of aluminum in March at a fixed price. By December 31, the market price for that aluminum has fallen to $85,000.

Bluestem recognizes the $15,000 loss now, with a liability. When it buys the aluminum in March, inventory is recorded at $85,000, the liability is removed, and cash of $100,000 is paid.

Quick check. Which method shows the inventory write-down as a separate line? Pause and pick.

It's B, the loss method.

Microteach 11.4 · 1:23The gross profit method

Estimate ending inventory from sales and a historical gross profit rate; not acceptable for annual reports.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

On March 31, a fire hits Bluestem's warehouse. The insurer wants to know what was inside. There's no count.

Start with goods available: $760,000 plus $640,000 of purchases, $1.4 million. Sales were $1 million, and Bluestem's gross profit rate is forty percent, so estimated cost of goods sold is $600,000. Estimated inventory is $800,000. Salvaged goods are worth $120,000, so the loss is $680,000.

Watch the base. A fifty percent markup on cost means a gross profit rate on sales of fifty over one fifty, thirty-three and a third percent. Convert before using the method.

The gross profit method is used for insurance claims, interim reports and as a reasonableness check on counts. It's not acceptable for annual financial statements, because it assumes last year's margin still holds.

If product lines have different margins, apply separate rates to each line's sales, or the estimate will be off.

Quick check. A markup of twenty-five percent on cost equals what gross profit rate on sales? Pause and pick.

It's B, twenty percent: twenty-five over one twenty-five.

Microteach 11.5 · 1:31The retail inventory method

Retail stores convert ending inventory at retail to cost using a cost-to-retail ratio.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

Bluestem's three retail shops count inventory at shelf prices. The retail method turns that into cost.

Goods available cost $400,000 and would sell for $620,000 after $20,000 of markups. Subtract $40,000 of markdowns and $480,000 of sales: ending inventory at retail is $100,000.

The conventional retail method computes the cost ratio after markups but before markdowns: four hundred thousand over six twenty, sixty-four point five two percent. Ending inventory at cost is about $64,516. Leaving markdowns out of the ratio gives an approximate lower of cost or market.

Including markdowns in the ratio gives sixty-eight point nine seven percent and approximates average cost instead. The conventional method is more common.

Unlike the gross profit method, the retail method is accepted for annual reports and taxes, and it's essential for big retailers with thousands of items.

Quick check. Goods available cost three hundred thousand, retail five hundred thousand; ending inventory at retail eighty thousand. Ending inventory at cost? Pause and pick.

It's B, $48,000: sixty percent of $80,000.

Microteach 11.6 · 1:12Presentation and analysis

Disclose methods and write-downs; inventory turnover shows how fast stock sells.

Reading: Intermediate Accounting, Chapter 9.

Read the script

How fast does Bluestem turn its inventory into sales?

Inventory turnover is cost of goods sold over average inventory: $2.52 million over $720,000, three point five times a year. That's about a hundred and four days of inventory on hand.

Bluestem is seasonal. It builds inventory through winter for spring sales, so a December 31 balance sheet shows inventory near its peak. Analysts should compare with similar companies and look at quarterly data.

Disclosures include the valuation basis, the cost flow method, major classes of inventory, and material write-downs and purchase commitment losses.

Turnover is a balance. Too much inventory ties up cash and risks obsolescence, like the fishing kayaks. Too little risks running out in May.

Quick check. Cost of goods sold one point two million; average inventory three hundred thousand. Days in inventory? Pause and pick.

It's B, about ninety-one days: turnover of four.

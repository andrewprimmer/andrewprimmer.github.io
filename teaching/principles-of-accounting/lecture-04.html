Microteach 4.1 · 1:21Why we adjust

Revenue and expense recognition need entries at period end, even with no new transaction.

Textbook: §4.1, pp. 212–214

Read the script

On June thirtieth, nothing happens at Redwood River Supply. No sale, no payment. And yet the accountant still has entries to make. Why?

Adjusting entries are made at the end of an accounting period so that revenues and expenses are recorded in the period they belong to, not just when cash changed hands.

Three ideas sit behind them. The periodicity assumption chops the life of a business into months, quarters and years. The revenue recognition principle records revenue when it's earned. And the expense recognition principle matches each expense to the revenue it helped earn.

There are four types. Two are deferrals, where cash moved first: prepaid expenses and unearned revenues. Two are accruals, where cash hasn't moved yet: accrued revenues and accrued expenses. Depreciation, a kind of prepaid expense, is the fifth one you'll see.

Here's a rule to remember. An adjusting entry never involves cash. It always changes one balance sheet account and one income statement account.

Quick check. Which account will an adjusting entry never include? Pause and pick.

It's C, cash. Cash transactions were already recorded when the money moved.

Microteach 4.2 · 1:19Prepaid expenses

Supplies and insurance turn into expense as they are used up.

Textbook: §4.2, pp. 215–226

Read the script

Pay for something in advance and you haven't spent it yet. You've bought an asset. The adjustment comes when you use it up.

A prepaid expense is a cost paid in advance, like insurance, rent or supplies. It starts life as an asset and becomes an expense as it's used.

On June first, Redwood River pays twenty-four hundred dollars for twelve months of insurance. Debit prepaid insurance, an asset. Credit cash.

By June thirtieth, one month of cover has been used: twenty-four hundred divided by twelve is two hundred. Debit insurance expense two hundred, credit prepaid insurance two hundred. Twenty-two hundred stays on the balance sheet.

Supplies work the same way. Redwood River bought six hundred dollars of supplies. A count on June thirtieth finds one hundred and fifty left, so four hundred and fifty were used. Debit supplies expense, credit supplies.

Quick check. Three thousand dollars of rent is paid on June first for three months. What's the June thirtieth adjustment? Pause and pick.

It's B. One month is used, so debit rent expense one thousand and credit prepaid rent one thousand.

Microteach 4.3 · 1:08Unearned revenue

Cash received in advance is a liability until the work is done.

Textbook: §4.2, pp. 215–226

Read the script

A customer pays you before you've done the work. Great for cash. But you haven't earned it yet. So what is it?

It's unearned revenue: cash received in advance for goods or services not yet delivered. Until you deliver, you owe the customer, so it's a liability.

On June tenth, Prairie View Dairy pays three thousand dollars up front for six months of equipment servicing. Debit cash, credit unearned revenue, a liability.

By June thirtieth, one month's servicing is done: three thousand over six months is five hundred. Debit unearned revenue five hundred, reducing the liability. Credit service revenue five hundred.

So the liability shrinks and revenue grows as the work gets done. Gift cards, subscriptions and season tickets all work this way.

Quick check. When the dairy prepaid on June tenth, what kind of account was unearned revenue? Pause and pick.

It's C, a liability. Despite the name, it isn't revenue until it's earned.

Microteach 4.4 · 1:18Accrued revenues and expenses

Record what was earned or incurred but not yet billed or paid, such as wages and interest.

Textbook: §4.2, pp. 215–226; §4.3, pp. 227–235

Read the script

Sometimes the work is done or the cost is incurred, but no one has billed anyone yet. Those are accruals.

Accrued revenue is revenue earned but not yet billed or collected. You record a receivable and the revenue.

Redwood River finished seven hundred dollars of repair work on June twenty-ninth but won't send the invoice until July. Debit accounts receivable, credit service revenue.

An accrued expense is a cost incurred but not yet paid. Wages and interest are the classic examples.

Staff worked June twenty-seventh to thirtieth but won't be paid until July third. That's six hundred dollars of wages. Debit wages expense, credit wages payable.

And interest builds up on the five thousand dollar note at six percent a year. One month is five thousand times six percent times one twelfth, twenty-five dollars. Debit interest expense, credit interest payable.

Quick check. Employees have earned twelve hundred dollars that hasn't been paid yet. Which adjusting entry? Pause and pick.

It's B. Debit wages expense, credit wages payable. No cash has moved yet.

Microteach 4.5 · 1:13Depreciation as an adjustment

Accumulated depreciation is a contra asset, and cost less it gives book value.

Textbook: §4.3, pp. 227–235

Read the script

Redwood River's equipment will last years. So charging its whole cost as an expense in June would be wrong. Depreciation fixes that.

Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-term asset, like equipment or a truck, over the periods that benefit from using it. It's not about market value. It's about matching cost to use.

Redwood River's equipment cost eight thousand dollars and should last five years with no salvage value. That's sixteen hundred a year, or about one hundred thirty-three dollars a month.

The adjusting entry debits depreciation expense one hundred thirty-three and credits accumulated depreciation.

Accumulated depreciation is a contra asset. It sits under equipment and reduces it, without touching the original cost. Eight thousand less one thirty-three gives a book value of seven thousand eight hundred sixty-seven.

Quick check. What kind of account is accumulated depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's C, a contra asset. It has a credit balance and reduces the asset it belongs to.

Microteach 4.6 · 1:10The adjusted trial balance

The checked list of balances the statements are built from.

Textbook: §4.4, pp. 236–238

Read the script

Once the adjustments are posted, you run the trial balance again. This one is the version your financial statements are built from.

Start with the unadjusted trial balance. Make the adjusting entries. Post them to the ledger. Then prepare the adjusted trial balance.

At Redwood River, the adjustments added twelve hundred dollars of revenue, from the dairy contract and the unbilled repair, and fourteen hundred and eight of expenses, from insurance, supplies, wages, interest and depreciation. Prepaid accounts shrank and new payables appeared.

Because every adjusting entry had equal debits and credits, the adjusted trial balance must still balance. If it doesn't, an adjustment was posted wrongly.

Now every balance is up to date, and the financial statements can be prepared straight from it. That's the next lecture.

Quick check. Why do we prepare an adjusted trial balance? Pause and pick.

It's B. It checks that the ledger still balances after adjustments, and it's the source for the statements.

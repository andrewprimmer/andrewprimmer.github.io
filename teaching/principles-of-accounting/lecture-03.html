Microteach 3.1 · 1:18The accounting equation

Assets = liabilities + equity, and every transaction keeps it in balance.

Textbook: §3.2, pp. 124–129

Read the script

One equation sits underneath every set of accounts ever prepared. If you remember nothing else from this course, remember this.

Assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity. Everything the business owns was financed either by borrowing, which is liabilities, or by the owner, which is equity.

Equity can be expanded into four parts. Owner's capital goes up with investments. Withdrawals take it down. Revenues push it up, and expenses push it down. So revenue and expenses really live inside equity.

Dana Larson puts twenty thousand dollars into a business bank account. Assets go up twenty thousand. Equity goes up twenty thousand. Twenty thousand equals zero plus twenty thousand.

Next she borrows five thousand from the bank. Cash rises to twenty-five thousand, and a note payable of five thousand appears. Twenty-five thousand equals five thousand plus twenty thousand. Still balanced.

Quick check. Assets are forty thousand dollars and liabilities fifteen thousand. What's equity? Pause and pick.

It's B, twenty-five thousand. Equity is assets minus liabilities.

Microteach 3.2 · 1:20Analyzing transactions

Every transaction changes at least two accounts.

Textbook: §3.4, pp. 135–139

Read the script

Before you write a single journal entry, you need to ask three questions about every transaction. Here's the routine.

First, which accounts are affected? Second, does each one increase or decrease? Third, after the change, does the accounting equation still balance? Every transaction changes at least two accounts.

Redwood River buys six hundred dollars of supplies for cash. Supplies go up six hundred, cash goes down six hundred. Both are assets, so total assets don't change at all.

Next it repairs a farmer's tractor and bills fifteen hundred dollars, to be paid later. Accounts receivable, an asset, goes up fifteen hundred. Service revenue goes up fifteen hundred, which increases equity. Both sides rise together.

Then it pays nine hundred dollars of wages. Cash goes down nine hundred, and wages expense reduces equity by nine hundred. Both sides fall together, so it still balances.

Quick check. Redwood River pays two thousand dollars it owes a supplier. What happens to the equation? Pause and pick.

It's A. Cash, an asset, goes down, and accounts payable, a liability, goes down by the same amount.

Microteach 3.3 · 1:12Debits and credits

Debit is left, credit is right, and each account type has a normal balance.

Textbook: §3.3, pp. 130–134

Read the script

Debit doesn't mean bad and credit doesn't mean good. In accounting they just mean left and right.

A debit is an entry on the left side of an account. A credit is an entry on the right. Every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits, so the books always balance.

Here are the rules. Assets, withdrawals and expenses increase with a debit and decrease with a credit. Liabilities, owner's capital and revenues are the opposite: they increase with a credit and decrease with a debit.

A handy memory trick is DEALER. D, E, A: drawings, expenses and assets increase with debits. L, E, R: liabilities, equity and revenue increase with credits.

An account's normal balance is the side that increases it. Cash normally has a debit balance. Accounts payable normally has a credit balance. If cash ever shows a credit balance, something has gone wrong.

Quick check. Which side increases a revenue account? Pause and pick.

It's credit. Revenue increases equity, and equity increases on the credit side.

Microteach 3.4 · 1:21Journalizing

Date, accounts, amounts and a short explanation in the general journal.

Textbook: §3.5, pp. 140–164

Read the script

The journal is where every transaction is first written down. It's called the book of original entry, and every entry follows the same format.

Each journal entry has the date, the account or accounts debited, listed first, then the accounts credited, indented to the right, the amounts, and a short explanation of what happened.

June first. Dana invests twenty thousand dollars. Debit cash twenty thousand, because an asset increased. Credit Larson, Capital twenty thousand, because equity increased.

June fifth. Redwood River repairs a tractor for Hansen Family Farms and bills fifteen hundred. Debit accounts receivable, credit service revenue.

June eighth. It buys eight thousand dollars of equipment, paying three thousand cash and signing a note for five thousand. Debit equipment eight thousand. Credit cash three thousand and notes payable five thousand. Three accounts, but debits still equal credits. That's called a compound entry.

Quick check. Redwood River pays four hundred dollars cash for advertising. Which account is debited? Pause and pick.

It's B, advertising expense. Expenses increase with a debit, and cash, an asset, is credited.

Microteach 3.5 · 1:14Posting to the ledger

Journal entries move into T-accounts, which show each account's running balance.

Textbook: §3.5, pp. 140–164

Read the script

The journal tells you what happened, in date order. But it can't tell you how much cash you have. For that you need the ledger.

The general ledger holds every account the business uses, each with all of its debits, credits and its current balance. Copying journal amounts into the ledger is called posting.

A quick way to picture a ledger account is a T-account: the account name across the top, debits on the left, credits on the right.

Let's post Redwood River's cash entries. Debit twenty thousand from Dana's investment. Credit three thousand for the equipment. Credit nine hundred for wages. Debit fifteen hundred when Hansen Family Farms pays. Total debits twenty-one five, total credits three nine, leaving a debit balance of seventeen thousand six hundred.

So the journal shows events in order. The ledger sorts them by account so you can see each balance.

Quick check. Cash has debits of eight thousand and credits of fifty-five hundred. What's the balance? Pause and pick.

It's B, a twenty-five hundred dollar debit balance. Debits are bigger, so the balance sits on the debit side.

Microteach 3.6 · 1:10The trial balance

It proves debits equal credits, but not that every entry is right.

Textbook: §3.6, p. 165 onward

Read the script

After posting a month of transactions, how do you know you haven't made a mistake? You prepare a trial balance.

A trial balance lists every ledger account with its balance, debits in one column and credits in another. If the two totals agree, the ledger is in balance.

Here's Redwood River's trial balance. Cash, receivables, equipment and wages expense on the debit side. Notes payable, capital and service revenue on the credit side. Both columns total twenty-eight thousand nine hundred.

But a trial balance that balances isn't proof that everything is right. It won't catch an entry you forgot completely, a debit posted to the wrong account, the same wrong amount on both sides, or an entry recorded twice.

It proves that debits equal credits. Nothing more.

Quick check. Which of these errors would make the trial balance not balance? Pause and pick.

It's C. Posting a five hundred dollar debit as fifty makes one side smaller than the other. The others leave debits and credits equal.

Microteach 2.1 · 1:17The four financial statements

Income statement, statement of owner's equity, balance sheet and statement of cash flows.

Textbook: §2.1, pp. 62–76

Read the script

Four documents tell the whole financial story of a business. Learn what each one answers and you can read any company's accounts.

The four financial statements are the income statement, the statement of owner's equity, the balance sheet, and the statement of cash flows.

Each answers one question. The income statement shows whether the business made a profit over a period. The statement of owner's equity shows how the owner's stake changed. The balance sheet shows what the business owns and owes on one date. And the statement of cash flows shows where cash came from and where it went.

Watch the headings. The income statement covers a period of time, for the month ended June thirtieth. The balance sheet is a snapshot at a single point, as of June thirtieth.

Here's Redwood River Supply's first month. Service revenue of nine thousand dollars, less expenses of six thousand five hundred, gives net income of two thousand five hundred. You'll see that number again in the next statement.

Quick check. Which statement shows what a business owns and owes on a single date? Pause and pick.

It's C, the balance sheet. A snapshot, not a period.

Microteach 2.2 · 1:13Assets, liabilities and equity

What the business owns, what it owes, and what is left for the owner.

Textbook: §2.2, pp. 77–80

Read the script

Every balance sheet is built from just three ingredients. Here they are.

Assets are resources the business owns or controls that it expects to bring future benefit. Cash, money customers owe you, inventory, equipment, buildings, land.

Liabilities are what the business owes to outsiders. Money owed to suppliers, called accounts payable, wages owed to staff, and bank loans.

Owner's equity is the owner's claim on the assets, the part left after every liability is paid. It goes up when the owner invests or the business earns a profit, and down when the owner withdraws money or the business makes a loss.

Assets and liabilities are split into current and noncurrent. Current means they'll be used up, turned into cash, or paid within one year. Cash, receivables and supplies are current. Equipment, buildings and a five-year bank loan are noncurrent.

Quick check. Redwood River Supply owes its supplier twelve hundred dollars. What is that? Pause and pick.

It's B, a liability. Specifically, accounts payable.

Microteach 2.3 · 1:11Revenues, expenses, gains and losses

Revenues and expenses come from main operations; gains and losses from side events.

Textbook: §2.2, pp. 77–80

Read the script

Profit has two ingredients going up and two going down. Mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes in this course.

Revenue is what a business earns from its main activity, selling goods or providing services. For Redwood River Supply, repair and delivery fees are revenue.

Expenses are the costs used up in earning that revenue: wages, rent, utilities, fuel, advertising.

Gains and losses come from events outside the main business. If Redwood River sells an old truck for more than its value in the books, that's a gain. If it sells for less, or is stolen, that's a loss.

On the income statement, revenue and gains increase net income, and expenses and losses reduce it. Nine thousand, less six thousand five hundred, plus a four hundred dollar gain, gives net income of two thousand nine hundred.

Quick check. Redwood River pays eight hundred dollars rent for its workshop. What is it? Pause and pick.

It's B, an expense. Rent is a normal cost of running the business.

Microteach 2.4 · 1:04How the statements link

Net income flows into equity, and equity flows into the balance sheet.

Textbook: §2.1, pp. 62–76; §2.3, p. 81 onward

Read the script

The four statements aren't separate documents. They're one chain, and a number flows from each into the next.

That's why we prepare them in order. First the income statement, then the statement of owner's equity, then the balance sheet, and finally the statement of cash flows.

Dana started with nothing, invested twenty thousand dollars, and the business earned two thousand five hundred from the income statement. She withdrew one thousand for personal use. So ending capital is twenty-one thousand five hundred.

That ending capital, twenty-one thousand five hundred, becomes the owner's equity figure on the June thirtieth balance sheet.

And the cash shown on the balance sheet equals the ending cash on the statement of cash flows. Change one number in the chain and the others must change too.

Quick check. Net income flows first into which statement? Pause and pick.

It's B, the statement of owner's equity, and from there into the balance sheet.

Microteach 2.5 · 1:10Cash basis versus accrual basis

Accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash moves.

Textbook: Looks ahead to §4.1, pp. 212–214

Read the script

Redwood River fixes a tractor in June but gets paid in July. Which month gets the revenue? Your answer depends on the basis of accounting.

Under the cash basis, revenue is recorded when cash comes in and expenses when cash goes out. It's simple, and some small businesses use it for tax.

Under the accrual basis, revenue is recorded when it's earned and expenses when they're incurred, whatever happens to the cash. GAAP requires the accrual basis.

So the tractor repair goes in July under the cash basis, when the money arrives, but in June under the accrual basis, because that's when the work was done.

Two principles drive this. The revenue recognition principle says record revenue when it's earned. The expense recognition principle, often called matching, says record expenses in the same period as the revenue they helped produce.

Quick check. Under the accrual basis, when is revenue recorded? Pause and pick.

It's B, when it's earned. Cash timing doesn't decide it.

Microteach 2.6 · 1:23Reading a classified balance sheet

Current and long-term items, working capital and the current ratio.

Textbook: §2.2, pp. 77–80; §5.3, pp. 300–312

Read the script

A bank looks at your balance sheet with one question: can this business pay what it owes in the next twelve months? A classified balance sheet makes that easy to see.

A classified balance sheet groups assets and liabilities into current and noncurrent sections, so the short-term picture stands out.

Here's Redwood River at June thirtieth. Fourteen thousand of current assets and eighteen thousand noncurrent, thirty-two thousand in total. On the other side, seven thousand current liabilities, three and a half thousand noncurrent, and owner's equity of twenty-one five. That's thirty-two thousand again. It balances.

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Fourteen thousand minus seven thousand gives seven thousand dollars of cushion.

The current ratio divides current assets by current liabilities. Fourteen thousand over seven thousand is two point zero. Redwood River has two dollars of current assets for every dollar it owes in the next year.

Quick check. Current assets nine thousand, current liabilities six thousand. What's the current ratio? Pause and pick.

It's B, one point five. Nine thousand divided by six thousand. Three thousand would be the working capital.

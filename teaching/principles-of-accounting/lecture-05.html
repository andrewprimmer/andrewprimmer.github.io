Microteach 5.1 · 1:09From adjusted trial balance to statements

Prepare the income statement first, then owner's equity, then the balance sheet.

Textbook: §4.5, p. 239 onward

Read the script

You have an adjusted trial balance. Now turn it into three financial statements, in the right order.

First the income statement, because you need net income. Then the statement of owner's equity, because you need ending capital. Then the balance sheet.

Take every revenue and expense from the adjusted trial balance. Revenue of fifty-one hundred, less wages, supplies, insurance, depreciation and interest, leaves net income of two thousand seven hundred ninety-two.

Next, owner's equity. Twenty thousand invested, plus net income of two seven nine two, less one thousand withdrawn, gives ending capital of twenty-one thousand seven hundred ninety-two.

That figure goes onto the balance sheet as owner's equity, alongside the assets and liabilities from the trial balance. If the balance sheet balances, the chain is right.

Quick check. Which statement must be prepared first? Pause and pick.

It's C, the income statement. Every other statement needs its net income.

Microteach 5.2 · 1:11Closing entries

Revenue, expense and drawing accounts go to zero through Income Summary into capital.

Textbook: §5.1, pp. 286–297

Read the script

July first. Redwood River's revenue for July should start at zero, not carry on from June. Closing entries make that happen.

Temporary accounts, revenues, expenses and withdrawals, measure one period and are reset to zero. Permanent accounts, assets, liabilities and capital, carry their balances forward.

Closing takes four steps. First, debit each revenue account to zero it and credit income summary, a temporary holding account.

Second, credit each expense account to zero it and debit income summary for the total, twenty-three hundred and eight.

Third, close income summary into capital. Its credit balance is net income, two seven nine two, so debit income summary and credit capital. Fourth, close withdrawals straight to capital: debit capital one thousand, credit withdrawals.

Quick check. Which of these accounts is not closed at the end of the period? Pause and pick.

It's D, accounts receivable. It's an asset, a permanent account, so its balance carries forward.

Microteach 5.3 · 1:09The post-closing trial balance

Only permanent (balance sheet) accounts carry into the new period.

Textbook: §5.2, pp. 298–299

Read the script

After closing, one last check confirms the books are ready for next month.

The post-closing trial balance is prepared after closing entries are posted. It lists only the permanent accounts: assets, liabilities and the owner's capital.

You should see every asset and liability, capital at its new balance of twenty-one seven nine two, and no revenues, expenses or withdrawals at all, because they're now zero. And debits should still equal credits.

If a revenue or expense account appears on it, a closing entry was missed. When it's right, July begins with a clean slate for revenue and expenses.

So in one cycle you'll prepare three trial balances: unadjusted after posting transactions, adjusted after adjusting entries, and post-closing after closing entries.

Quick check. Which account appears on a post-closing trial balance? Pause and pick.

It's C, wages payable. It's a liability, so it's permanent. The others have all been closed.

Microteach 5.4 · 1:08The accounting cycle in ten steps

From analyzing transactions to the post-closing trial balance.

Textbook: §3.3, pp. 130–134; §5.4, p. 313 onward

Read the script

Every month, every business runs the same loop. Here is the whole accounting cycle in ten steps.

During the period, you identify and analyze each transaction, record it in the journal, post it to the ledger, and prepare an unadjusted trial balance.

At the end of the period, you record and post adjusting entries, prepare the adjusted trial balance, and prepare the financial statements.

Then, to close the period, you record and post closing entries and prepare the post-closing trial balance. Some businesses add a tenth, optional step: reversing entries on the first day of the new period.

Then the cycle starts again. Software like QuickBooks posts entries and prints trial balances automatically, but identifying transactions and making adjustments still takes an accountant's judgment.

Quick check. Which step comes straight after the adjusted trial balance? Pause and pick.

It's B. The financial statements are prepared from the adjusted trial balance.

Microteach 5.5 · 1:13The 10-column worksheet

A spreadsheet that takes the trial balance through adjustments to the statements.

Textbook: §5.4, p. 313 onward

Read the script

Before accounting software, accountants planned the month-end on one big sheet of paper. It's still the clearest way to see how the cycle fits together.

The worksheet is an internal working paper, not a financial statement. It takes the trial balance through adjustments to the statements on a single page.

It has five pairs of debit and credit columns, ten in all: the trial balance, the adjustments, the adjusted trial balance, the income statement, and the balance sheet.

You add the adjustments across each row to get the adjusted balance. Then you move each revenue and expense into the income statement columns, and everything else into the balance sheet columns.

The difference between the income statement columns is net income, for Redwood River two thousand seven hundred ninety-two. Add it to the balance sheet credit column and those columns balance too. That's your proof.

Quick check. On the worksheet, which column does accumulated depreciation go in? Pause and pick.

It's D, the balance sheet credit column. It's a contra asset with a credit balance.

Microteach 5.6 · 1:15Correcting errors

Fix a wrong entry with a correcting entry, never by erasing or overwriting it.

Textbook: §3.6, p. 165 onward

Read the script

Everyone makes recording mistakes. The rule for fixing them is simple: you never erase. You correct.

A correcting entry is a new journal entry that fixes an earlier error. The original stays in the books, so anyone checking later can see exactly what happened and when.

Say Redwood River paid three hundred fifty dollars to repair its truck, but the clerk recorded it as supplies. Debit supplies, credit cash. Cash is right. Supplies is wrong.

The correcting entry moves the amount to where it belongs. Debit repairs expense three fifty, credit supplies three fifty. Cash is untouched because it was right the first time.

Use this three-step method. Write the entry that was made. Write the entry that should have been made. Then record whatever entry turns the first into the second.

Quick check. A five hundred dollar collection from a customer was credited to service revenue instead of accounts receivable. What's the correction? Pause and pick.

It's B. Debit service revenue to remove the wrong credit, and credit accounts receivable, where it should have gone.

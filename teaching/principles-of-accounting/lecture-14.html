Microteach 14.1 · 1:35Sole proprietorships, partnerships and corporations

Each form differs in owner liability, taxation and how long it lasts.

Textbook: §15.1, pp. 926–931

Read the script

Redwood River has grown, and Dana is thinking about taking on a partner, or even incorporating. The choice of business form changes the risk, the tax and the accounting.

A sole proprietorship has one owner. It's easy and cheap to start. But the owner has unlimited personal liability for the business's debts, and the business ends when the owner stops. Its profit is taxed on the owner's personal return.

A partnership has two or more owners. Each partner can bind the business, called mutual agency, and general partners have unlimited liability, even for a partner's mistakes. Profit passes through to the partners' own tax returns.

A corporation is a legal entity separate from its owners, the stockholders. Their liability is limited to what they invested, ownership is easy to transfer, and the business can last indefinitely. But corporate profit is taxed, and dividends are taxed again, which is called double taxation.

In short: unlimited liability and a single layer of tax for proprietors and partners, limited liability and double tax for corporations. Hybrids like the limited liability company, or LLC, try to combine limited liability with pass-through tax.

Quick check. Which business form gives its owners limited liability? Pause and pick.

It's C, the corporation. Stockholders can lose only what they invested.

Microteach 14.2 · 1:07Equity under each form

Owner's capital, partners' capital accounts, or stockholders' equity.

Textbook: §14.4, pp. 890–896

Read the script

Assets and liabilities look the same whatever the business form. The difference shows up in one place: the equity section.

A sole proprietorship has a single capital account in the owner's name. Investments and net income increase it. Withdrawals reduce it. That's what you've used all semester.

A partnership keeps a separate capital account and withdrawals account for each partner. If Dana takes on Sam Nguyen as a partner, the balance sheet shows Larson, Capital and Nguyen, Capital.

A corporation splits equity by source. Contributed capital, common stock and additional paid-in capital, is what stockholders invested. Retained earnings is the profit kept in the business. Payments to owners are called dividends, not withdrawals.

The accounting equation never changes. Only the labels on the equity side do.

Quick check. In a corporation, profits kept in the business are called what? Pause and pick.

It's C, retained earnings.

Microteach 14.3 · 1:17Dividing partnership income

By an agreed ratio, or with salary and interest allowances first.

Textbook: §15.3, pp. 935–937

Read the script

Dana and Sam run Redwood River together. It earns sixty thousand dollars. Who gets what? The partnership agreement decides.

Partners can share income in any agreed way: a fixed ratio, in proportion to capital, or with salary and interest allowances first and the remainder in a ratio. If there's no agreement, the law says to share equally.

Dana and Sam agree on salary allowances of twenty thousand for Dana and fifteen thousand for Sam, ten percent interest on their beginning capital of forty thousand and twenty thousand, and any remainder split equally.

Salaries take thirty-five thousand. Interest is four thousand for Dana and two thousand for Sam. That leaves nineteen thousand, split ninety-five hundred each. Dana gets thirty-three five, Sam twenty-six five. Total, sixty thousand.

Close income summary into each capital account. Remember, salary allowances aren't wages expense. They're just a way of dividing the profit.

Quick check. The partners have no agreement at all. How is income shared? Pause and pick.

It's B, equally.

Microteach 14.4 · 1:19Corporate equity in brief

Common stock and retained earnings, as a preview of ACCT 212.

Textbook: §14.1, pp. 860–870; §14.4, pp. 890–896

Read the script

If Redwood River incorporates, Dana becomes a stockholder. Here's a short preview of corporate equity, which you'll study fully in ACCT 212.

Common stock represents ownership. Each share often has a par value, a small legal amount printed on the share, which has nothing to do with what it's worth.

Redwood River Supply Inc. issues ten thousand one dollar par shares at five dollars each. Debit cash fifty thousand. Credit common stock for par, ten thousand, and paid-in capital in excess of par for the other forty thousand.

Retained earnings grows with net income and shrinks with dividends. Beginning retained earnings, plus net income, minus dividends, gives ending retained earnings. It's the corporate version of the statement of owner's equity.

In ACCT 212 you'll go further: preferred stock, buying back shares, stock dividends and splits, earnings per share, and the statement of cash flows.

Quick check. Beginning retained earnings fifteen thousand, net income eight thousand, dividends three thousand. What's ending retained earnings? Pause and pick.

It's B, twenty thousand.

Microteach 14.5 · 1:35Then and now: a historical annual report

A 1920s–30s annual report set beside a modern 10-K, from the instructor's archival research.

Textbook: Instructor's own material; no textbook reading.

Read the script

Today's annual reports run to hundreds of pages. A century ago, a company's whole report to its shareholders might fit on a few sheets. What changed?

A typical annual report from the nineteen twenties had a short directors' report, a balance sheet and a profit and loss account. There were few explanatory notes and no statement of cash flows. Directors decided for themselves how much to tell shareholders.

In Britain, the Royal Mail case of nineteen thirty-one showed the danger. The Royal Mail Steam Packet Company had used secret reserves built up in good years to report steady profits while it was really losing money. Its chairman went to prison for issuing a misleading prospectus.

In the United States, the crash of nineteen twenty-nine led to the Securities Acts of nineteen thirty-three and thirty-four, which created the SEC and required audited statements from listed companies. Standard-setters developed GAAP. And in two thousand two, SOX added reports on internal control.

So a modern ten-K carries far more disclosure than its nineteen twenties ancestor. But underneath, the journal entries, ledgers and balance sheets are built exactly the way you've learned this semester.

Quick check. What did the Royal Mail case of nineteen thirty-one expose? Pause and pick.

It's B, secret reserves used to hide losses behind steady reported profits.

Microteach 14.6 · 1:19Course recap: the accounting cycle in 2 minutes

Transactions, adjustments, statements and closing, tied together for the final.

Textbook: §3.3, pp. 130–134; §5.4, p. 313 onward

Read the script

Fourteen lectures, one business, one cycle. Here's the whole of ACCT 211 in under two minutes.

Every period, you analyze transactions and journalize them using debits and credits. You post them to the ledger and prepare a trial balance. You make adjusting entries for deferrals, accruals and depreciation. You prepare the statements, and you close the temporary accounts.

You learned to measure assets: cash, protected by internal controls and checked with bank reconciliations. Receivables, at net realizable value. Inventory, using FIFO, LIFO or weighted average. And long-term assets, at cost less depreciation.

And the claims against them: current liabilities from notes, sales tax and unearned revenue, the payroll liabilities every employer carries, and the equity of a proprietor, partners or stockholders.

And through all of it, one equation held: assets equal liabilities plus equity. Good luck on the final.

One last quick check. Which step comes immediately before closing entries? Pause and pick.

It's B, preparing the financial statements. Then you close, and the cycle starts again.

Microteach 6.1 · 1:14Service versus merchandising businesses

A merchandiser has cost of goods sold, and sales less COGS is gross profit.

Textbook: §6.1, pp. 366–377

Read the script

Redwood River Supply has been repairing equipment. Now Dana wants to sell farm supplies too. That one decision changes the accounting.

A service business sells its time and skill, like a repair shop or a law firm. A merchandising business buys goods and resells them, like a hardware store or a wholesale distributor. Merchandisers hold inventory.

Merchandise inventory is goods the business owns and intends to sell. It's a current asset on the balance sheet.

When the goods are sold, their cost moves out of inventory and becomes an expense called cost of goods sold.

Net sales minus cost of goods sold gives gross profit, sometimes called gross margin. It's what's left to pay all the other expenses. If Redwood River sells fifty-eight thousand of supplies that cost thirty-five thousand, gross profit is twenty-three thousand.

Quick check. Where does merchandise inventory appear in the financial statements? Pause and pick.

It's B, a current asset on the balance sheet. It becomes cost of goods sold only when it's sold.

Microteach 6.2 · 1:16Perpetual versus periodic systems

Perpetual updates inventory with every sale; periodic counts it at period end.

Textbook: §6.2, pp. 378–385

Read the script

How does a store know what's on its shelves? There are two ways to keep track, and they give you very different information.

In a perpetual inventory system, inventory and cost of goods sold are updated every time goods are bought or sold. The records always show what should be on hand.

In a periodic system, purchases are recorded but inventory isn't updated with each sale. At the end of the period, staff count what's left, and cost of goods sold is calculated from the count.

Under periodic, cost of goods sold is beginning inventory plus purchases, minus ending inventory.

Perpetual records are always current and make theft or damage easy to spot, but need scanners and software. Periodic is cheap and simple, but losses hide inside cost of goods sold. Today barcodes make perpetual the norm, and it's the system we'll use for Redwood River.

Quick check. Beginning inventory five thousand, purchases twenty thousand, ending inventory four thousand. What's cost of goods sold? Pause and pick.

It's B, twenty-one thousand. Five plus twenty, minus four.

Microteach 6.3 · 1:29Recording purchases

Purchase discounts, returns and freight terms (FOB shipping point or destination).

Textbook: §6.3, pp. 386–391; §6.5, pp. 400–403

Read the script

Redwood River orders ten thousand dollars of fencing and feed from a supplier in Sioux Falls. Here's every entry that purchase can create.

Under a perpetual system, purchases go straight into merchandise inventory. Debit inventory ten thousand, credit accounts payable. The terms are two ten, net thirty: a two percent discount if paid within ten days, otherwise the full amount is due in thirty.

One thousand dollars of feed arrives damaged and is sent back. Debit accounts payable, because Redwood River owes less. Credit merchandise inventory.

Redwood River pays within ten days. It owes nine thousand, and two percent of that is one hundred eighty. Debit accounts payable nine thousand. Credit inventory one hundred eighty, because the goods cost less. Credit cash eight thousand eight hundred twenty.

Freight terms decide who pays shipping. FOB shipping point means ownership passes when the goods leave the seller, and the buyer pays freight, adding it to inventory. FOB destination means the seller owns them until delivery and pays the freight.

Quick check. Goods are bought FOB shipping point with three hundred dollars of freight. What's the buyer's entry? Pause and pick.

It's B. Freight-in is part of the cost of inventory under a perpetual system.

Microteach 6.4 · 1:10Recording sales

Under perpetual, each sale takes two entries: one for revenue, one for COGS.

Textbook: §6.4, pp. 392–399

Read the script

A service business makes one entry for a sale. A merchandiser using a perpetual system makes two. Here's why.

Every merchandise sale has two parts. The selling price is revenue. And the cost of the goods leaving the shelf is an expense. A perpetual system records both at once.

Redwood River sells four thousand dollars of fencing to Hansen Family Farms on account. The first entry debits accounts receivable and credits sales four thousand.

The fencing cost Redwood River two thousand five hundred. So the second entry debits cost of goods sold and credits merchandise inventory twenty-five hundred.

Together, the two entries show the profit on this sale: four thousand of revenue, less twenty-five hundred of cost, a gross profit of fifteen hundred.

Quick check. Under a perpetual system, which account is credited in the second entry for a sale? Pause and pick.

It's C, merchandise inventory. The goods leave the shelf, so inventory goes down.

Microteach 6.5 · 1:21Sales discounts and returns

Contra-revenue accounts, and what terms such as 2/10, n/30 mean.

Textbook: §6.4, pp. 392–399

Read the script

Customers return goods and pay early for a discount. Both reduce what Redwood River really earns, but neither touches the sales account directly.

Instead, they go into contra-revenue accounts: sales returns and allowances, and sales discounts. Both have debit balances and reduce sales. Keeping them separate shows managers how much revenue is lost to returns and discounts.

Hansen returns five hundred dollars of fencing that cost three hundred. Debit sales returns and allowances, credit accounts receivable five hundred. Then put the goods back on the shelf: debit inventory, credit cost of goods sold three hundred.

Hansen now owes thirty-five hundred and pays within ten days, taking a two percent discount of seventy dollars. Debit cash thirty-four thirty and sales discounts seventy. Credit accounts receivable for the full thirty-five hundred.

On the income statement, sales of four thousand, less returns of five hundred and discounts of seventy, gives net sales of thirty-four hundred thirty.

Quick check. What's the normal balance of sales discounts? Pause and pick.

It's debit. It's a contra-revenue account, so it's the opposite of sales.

Microteach 6.6 · 1:34The multi-step income statement

Gross profit, operating income and the gross margin ratio.

Textbook: §6.6, pp. 404–407

Read the script

A merchandiser's income statement doesn't jump straight to net income. It steps down, one subtotal at a time, and each step tells you something.

Here's a year at Redwood River. Net sales fifty-eight thousand, less cost of goods sold thirty-five thousand, gives gross profit of twenty-three thousand. Less operating expenses of fifteen thousand gives income from operations of eight thousand. Less interest of three hundred, a non-operating item, gives net income of seventy-seven hundred.

Each step answers a question. Gross profit: is the markup on goods big enough? Operating income: is the core business profitable? Net income: what's left after everything, including interest and one-off gains or losses.

The gross margin ratio divides gross profit by net sales. Twenty-three thousand over fifty-eight thousand is thirty-nine point seven percent. About forty cents of every sales dollar is left after paying for the goods.

A simple income statement just lists all revenues, then all expenses. Same net income, but you lose the subtotals that make the multi-step version so useful.

Quick check. Net sales eighty thousand, cost of goods sold fifty thousand. What's the gross margin ratio? Pause and pick.

It's B, thirty-seven point five percent. Gross profit of thirty thousand divided by eighty thousand.

Microteach 9.1 · 1:18Accounts receivable and credit sales

Selling on credit wins sales but brings the risk of customers who never pay.

Textbook: §9.1, pp. 578–583

Read the script

Farmers around Marshall often can't pay until after harvest. If Redwood River insists on cash, it loses those customers. So it sells on credit, and takes a risk.

Accounts receivable are amounts customers owe for goods or services bought on credit. They're usually due in thirty to sixty days, with no formal contract and no interest. That makes them a current asset.

Notes receivable are different. They're backed by a signed promissory note, they carry interest, and they can run for months or years. We'll come back to those at the end of this lecture.

Selling on credit wins more customers and more sales, and in farm supply it's expected. The cost is that some customers will never pay. Those uncollectible amounts are called bad debts.

Remember revenue recognition: the revenue was earned when the goods were delivered, even though the cash hasn't arrived. The question is how to account for the part that never will.

Quick check. Which usually carries interest: accounts receivable or notes receivable? Pause and pick.

It's B, notes receivable. They're formal, signed agreements that include an interest rate.

Microteach 9.2 · 1:09The direct write-off method

Simple and used for tax, but it breaks expense recognition, so GAAP allows it only when bad debts are immaterial.

Textbook: §9.2, pp. 584–593

Read the script

The simplest way to handle a bad debt is to wait until you're sure it won't be paid, then write it off. Simple. But it has a problem.

Under the direct write-off method, bad debt expense is recorded only when a specific customer's account is judged uncollectible.

Lakeview Farms has gone out of business owing six hundred dollars. Debit bad debt expense, credit accounts receivable for Lakeview. Done.

But look at the timing. The sale and its revenue were recorded last year. The expense lands this year. That breaks the expense recognition principle, and it leaves receivables overstated in the meantime.

So GAAP allows the direct write-off method only when bad debts are immaterial. It is, however, the method required for federal income tax, which is why many small businesses use it.

Quick check. Why does GAAP generally reject the direct write-off method? Pause and pick.

It's B. The expense often lands in a later period than the revenue it relates to.

Microteach 9.3 · 1:24The allowance method

Estimate bad debts in the period of the sale, using Allowance for Doubtful Accounts.

Textbook: §9.2, pp. 584–593

Read the script

You don't know which customers won't pay. But from experience, you know roughly how much won't be collected. The allowance method uses that.

Under the allowance method, the business estimates its bad debts at the end of each period and records the expense in the same period as the sales. That's what GAAP requires.

Redwood River estimates two thousand dollars of this year's receivables won't be collected. Debit bad debt expense two thousand. Credit allowance for doubtful accounts, a contra asset, because we don't yet know which customers to remove.

On the balance sheet, receivables of fifty thousand, less the allowance of two thousand, show forty-eight thousand: the amount we expect to collect, called the net realizable value.

Next year, Lakeview defaults on six hundred. Now debit the allowance, not an expense, and credit Lakeview's account. The expense was already recorded last year. Receivables and the allowance both fall by six hundred, so net realizable value doesn't change.

Quick check. Under the allowance method, what does writing off a specific customer's account do? Pause and pick.

It's C. Receivables and the allowance fall together, so net realizable value stays the same, and there's no new expense.

Microteach 9.4 · 1:16Income statement versus balance sheet approach

Percentage of credit sales, or percentage of receivables.

Textbook: §9.2, pp. 584–593

Read the script

There are two ways to estimate bad debts. One starts from sales. The other starts from receivables. They can give different numbers.

The income statement approach, also called percentage of sales, estimates the expense directly as a percentage of credit sales. It focuses on matching.

Redwood River's credit sales were two hundred thousand, and experience says one percent goes bad. Bad debt expense is two thousand dollars, regardless of what's already in the allowance.

The balance sheet approach, or percentage of receivables, estimates what the allowance balance should be at year-end. The expense is whatever it takes to get the allowance to that target. It focuses on the balance sheet value.

Receivables are fifty thousand, and five percent is expected to go bad, so the allowance should be twenty-five hundred. It already has a four hundred dollar credit balance left from last year, so the adjustment is twenty-one hundred.

Quick check. Which approach ignores the existing balance in the allowance? Pause and pick.

It's A, the income statement approach. It calculates the expense directly from sales.

Microteach 9.5 · 1:31The aging schedule

Older balances get higher loss rates, and the total sets the year-end adjusting entry.

Textbook: §9.2, pp. 584–593; §9.7, p. 612 onward

Read the script

A bill that's ten days old will almost certainly be paid. A bill that's six months old probably won't. The aging schedule builds that into the estimate.

An aging schedule sorts every customer's balance by how long it's been outstanding, and applies a higher expected loss rate to the older groups. It's a more detailed version of the balance sheet approach.

Here's Redwood River's aging. Thirty thousand current at one percent, three hundred. Ten thousand up to thirty days late at four percent, four hundred. Six thousand at ten percent, six hundred. Three thousand at twenty-five percent, seven fifty. And one thousand over ninety days at fifty percent, five hundred. The allowance should be twenty-five fifty.

Sometimes write-offs during the year exceed the allowance, leaving a debit balance. If it has a two hundred dollar debit balance, the adjustment must be twenty-seven fifty to bring it up to a twenty-five fifty credit.

Debit bad debt expense twenty-seven fifty, credit the allowance. You can practise more of these with the aged debtor report on this site's accounts receivable page.

Quick check. The aging says the allowance should be three thousand. It already has a five hundred dollar credit balance. What's the bad debt expense? Pause and pick.

It's C, twenty-five hundred. Just enough to bring the credit balance up to three thousand.

Microteach 9.6 · 1:25Notes receivable and interest

Interest = principal × rate × time, and the entries at issue and at maturity.

Textbook: §9.6, pp. 606–611

Read the script

When a customer needs longer to pay, Redwood River can ask them to sign a note. Now the debt is formal, and it earns interest.

A promissory note is a written promise to pay a set principal, plus interest, on a set maturity date. To the business holding it, it's a note receivable.

Interest is principal times rate times time. The rate is always annual, so time is the fraction of a year the note runs. Many businesses use a three hundred sixty day year to make the arithmetic easier.

Hansen Family Farms converts six thousand dollars of its account into a ninety-day note at eight percent. Interest is six thousand times eight percent times ninety over three sixty: one hundred twenty dollars.

When the note is signed, debit notes receivable, credit Hansen's account receivable. At maturity Hansen pays sixty-one twenty. Debit cash, credit notes receivable six thousand and interest revenue one twenty. If a year-end falls in between, you accrue the interest earned so far.

Quick check. What's the interest on a ten thousand dollar, six percent, sixty-day note, using a three sixty day year? Pause and pick.

It's B, one hundred dollars. Ten thousand times six percent times sixty over three sixty.

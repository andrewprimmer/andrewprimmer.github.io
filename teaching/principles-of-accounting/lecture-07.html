Microteach 7.1 · 1:16Why cost flow matters

The same goods can give different COGS and profit depending on the method.

Textbook: §10.1, pp. 648–656

Read the script

Redwood River buys the same bag of feed three times in a month, at three different prices. When it sells one, which price was it?

Here's January. One hundred bags on hand at ten dollars each. Two hundred more bought on the tenth at eleven dollars. One hundred more on the twentieth at thirteen. Four hundred bags available, costing forty-five hundred dollars. During January, Redwood River sells two hundred fifty bags.

The bags are identical, so nobody knows which ones left the barn. Accountants use a cost flow assumption to decide which costs go to cost of goods sold and which stay in inventory.

There are four methods: specific identification, first in first out, last in first out, and weighted average. The next two videos work through each one.

Whichever method is chosen, the forty-five hundred dollars must be split between cost of goods sold and ending inventory. The method just changes the split, and so it changes profit.

Quick check. What does a cost flow assumption decide? Pause and pick.

It's B. It's about the flow of costs, not the physical flow of goods.

Microteach 7.2 · 1:19Specific identification and FIFO

First in, first out sends the oldest costs to COGS.

Textbook: §10.1, pp. 648–656

Read the script

If you can tell items apart, you track each one. If you can't, the most common assumption is that the oldest go first.

Specific identification tracks the actual cost of each item sold. It suits expensive, unique items, like cars, jewelry or a used tractor with its own serial number. For bags of feed it isn't practical.

First in, first out, or FIFO, assumes the oldest units are sold first. So the earliest costs go to cost of goods sold, and the newest costs stay in ending inventory.

For Redwood River's two hundred fifty bags, FIFO takes all one hundred at ten dollars, then one hundred fifty at eleven. Cost of goods sold is twenty-six fifty. Ending inventory is the newest bags: fifty at eleven and one hundred at thirteen, eighteen fifty.

Check: twenty-six fifty plus eighteen fifty is forty-five hundred. A strength of FIFO is that the balance sheet shows inventory at recent, realistic costs.

Quick check. Under FIFO, ending inventory is made up of which costs? Pause and pick.

It's B, the most recent. The oldest costs have already gone to cost of goods sold.

Microteach 7.3 · 1:24LIFO and weighted average

LIFO is allowed under US GAAP but not IFRS, and the LIFO conformity rule ties it to tax.

Textbook: §10.1, pp. 648–656

Read the script

Same feed, same sales, two more methods, and two more answers for profit.

Last in, first out, or LIFO, assumes the newest units are sold first. Take the one hundred at thirteen, then one fifty at eleven. Cost of goods sold is twenty-nine fifty. Ending inventory keeps the oldest costs: fifteen fifty.

Weighted average divides total cost by total units: forty-five hundred over four hundred is eleven twenty-five a bag. Cost of goods sold is two hundred fifty times that, twenty-eight twelve fifty. Ending inventory is sixteen eighty-seven fifty.

When prices are rising, FIFO gives the lowest cost of goods sold and the highest profit. LIFO gives the highest cost and the lowest profit, which also means less income tax. Weighted average lands in between.

Two rules about LIFO. It's allowed under US GAAP but banned under international standards. And the IRS's LIFO conformity rule says that if you use LIFO for taxes, you must use it in your financial statements too.

Quick check. Prices are rising. Which method gives the lowest taxable income? Pause and pick.

It's B, LIFO. The newest, highest costs go to cost of goods sold.

Microteach 7.4 · 1:21Perpetual versus periodic costing

The same method can give different answers under each system.

Textbook: §10.2, pp. 657–662; §10.3, pp. 663–671

Read the script

Here's a twist. The same cost flow method can give a different answer depending on whether you use a perpetual or periodic system. It's all about timing.

Under periodic, cost of goods sold is calculated once, at the end of the period, using every purchase made that month. Under perpetual, it's calculated at each sale, using only the purchases made before that sale.

Change one fact. Suppose all two hundred fifty bags were sold on January fifteenth, before the purchase at thirteen dollars arrived.

Periodic LIFO looks back from month-end and takes the thirteen dollar bags first: twenty-nine fifty. Perpetual LIFO, on January fifteenth, couldn't use bags that hadn't arrived. It takes two hundred at eleven and fifty at ten: twenty-seven hundred.

FIFO, though, always gives the same answer under both systems, because the oldest costs are the oldest no matter when you look. Weighted average changes too, because a perpetual system recalculates a moving average after each purchase.

Quick check. Which method always gives the same cost of goods sold under perpetual and periodic systems? Pause and pick.

It's B, FIFO.

Microteach 7.5 · 1:13Lower of cost and net realizable value

Write inventory down when it is worth less than it cost.

Textbook: §10.1, pp. 648–656

Read the script

Inventory goes on the books at cost. But what if the goods are now worth less than you paid?

Net realizable value is what you expect to get when you sell the goods, minus the costs of finishing and selling them.

The rule is conservative. Inventory is reported at the lower of its cost or its net realizable value. You never write inventory up above cost, but you must write it down when its value falls.

Some of Redwood River's ending feed got damp in storage. It cost eighteen fifty, but can now only be sold for sixteen hundred, net of selling costs. That's a two hundred fifty dollar write-down.

Debit cost of goods sold two fifty and credit merchandise inventory two fifty. The loss hits this year's income, when the value fell, not later when the feed is sold.

Quick check. Inventory cost five thousand dollars and its net realizable value is fifty-six hundred. What's it reported at? Pause and pick.

It's B, five thousand. The lower of the two. You never write inventory up above cost.

Microteach 7.6 · 1:31Inventory errors and turnover

An ending-inventory error misstates two years' income, in opposite directions.

Textbook: §10.4, pp. 672–673; §10.5, p. 674 onward

Read the script

Miscount your inventory by a thousand dollars and you'll misstate profit for two years, in opposite directions. Here's why.

Suppose year one's ending inventory is overstated by a thousand dollars. Cost of goods sold is a thousand too low, so net income is a thousand too high. But year one's ending inventory is year two's beginning inventory. So in year two, cost of goods sold is a thousand too high, and net income a thousand too low.

The error counterbalances: the two-year total is correct, but each year's profit is wrong, which can mislead anyone comparing the two years.

To judge how well inventory is managed, use inventory turnover: cost of goods sold divided by average inventory. Redwood River's thirty-five thousand over an average of five thousand is seven. It sold through its stock seven times in the year.

Days' sales in inventory turns that into days: three sixty-five divided by seven is about fifty-two days. Higher turnover and fewer days usually mean less cash tied up on the shelves.

Quick check. Year one's ending inventory is understated. Is year one's net income overstated, understated or correct? Pause and pick.

It's understated. Lower ending inventory means higher cost of goods sold, so lower profit.

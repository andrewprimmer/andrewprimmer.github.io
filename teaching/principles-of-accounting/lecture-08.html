Microteach 8.1 · 1:23The fraud triangle

Pressure, opportunity and rationalization; controls attack opportunity.

Textbook: §8.1, pp. 532–534

Read the script

Most workplace fraud isn't committed by master criminals. It's committed by trusted employees. Why do ordinary people do it?

Fraud is an intentional act of deception for personal gain. In accounting it usually means stealing assets, like cash or inventory, or falsifying the financial statements.

Criminologist Donald Cressey found three conditions are usually present, known as the fraud triangle. Pressure, such as debts or an impossible sales target. Opportunity, a weak control that lets someone get away with it. And rationalization, a story the person tells themselves, like I'm underpaid, or I'll pay it back.

A business can't do much about an employee's pressure or excuses. But it can remove the opportunity. That's the job of internal controls.

Watch for red flags: an employee living beyond their means, someone who never takes a vacation or won't let anyone else do their job, and unexplained cash or inventory shortages.

Quick check. One clerk both opens the mail containing checks and records customer payments. Which side of the triangle does that create? Pause and pick.

It's B, opportunity. That clerk could take a check and hide it in the records.

Microteach 8.2 · 1:26SOX in brief

Section 404 makes public-company managers and auditors report on internal control.

Textbook: §8.7, p. 555 onward

Read the script

In 2001 and 2002, Enron and WorldCom collapsed after billions of dollars of accounting fraud. Congress's answer was the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, known as SOX, set new rules for public companies, their executives and their auditors, to restore trust in financial reporting.

The key requirements: the chief executive and chief financial officer must personally certify the financial statements. Management must report on its internal control, and under section four oh four, the auditor must give an opinion on it too. SOX created the PCAOB to oversee auditors, and it set tough penalties, including prison, for fraud.

Section four oh four is the one accountants talk about most. It makes internal control a yearly, documented, audited responsibility.

Redwood River isn't a public company, so SOX doesn't apply. But the ideas behind it, documented controls and accountable managers, protect any business. You'll study SOX in depth in ACCT 360.

Quick check. Under SOX, who must certify a public company's financial statements? Pause and pick.

It's B, the CEO and CFO. They can't claim they didn't know.

Microteach 8.3 · 1:20COSO's five components

Control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, monitoring.

Textbook: §8.2, pp. 535–541

Read the script

What does good internal control actually look like? The most widely used answer comes from a group called COSO.

Internal control is the set of processes a business uses to safeguard its assets, make its accounting records reliable, run efficiently, and comply with laws.

The COSO framework has five components. The control environment, the tone at the top. Risk assessment, working out what could go wrong. Control activities, the specific procedures. Information and communication, so people know their responsibilities. And monitoring, checking the controls still work.

Control activities are the part you can see. Separate duties so no one person controls a whole transaction. Require authorization. Use prenumbered documents. Physically protect assets. And have someone independent reconcile the records.

Controls give reasonable assurance, not a guarantee. Two employees working together, called collusion, or a manager overriding the rules can still defeat them.

Quick check. Which COSO component is about the tone at the top? Pause and pick.

It's C, the control environment. It's the foundation for everything else.

Microteach 8.4 · 1:16Controlling cash receipts and payments

Separate who handles cash from who records it, deposit daily and approve every payment.

Textbook: §8.3, pp. 542–546

Read the script

Cash is the easiest asset to steal and the hardest to trace. So it gets the tightest controls.

For receipts: have two people open the mail and list every check. Deposit everything daily, without paying anything out of it first. Use registers that print a receipt for every sale. And the person who handles cash never records it in the books.

For payments: pay by check or electronic transfer, not cash. Pay only approved invoices backed by documents. Keep approving a payment separate from signing it. And stamp each invoice paid so it can't be paid twice.

The thread running through all of this is separation of duties. Custody of cash, recording it, and authorizing it should sit with different people.

Small businesses like Redwood River don't have enough staff to separate everything. So the owner fills the gap: Dana opens the bank statement herself and reviews every check and transfer.

Quick check. Which of these is a weak cash control? Pause and pick.

It's B. Paying bills out of the day's takings means the deposit no longer matches the sales, and gaps can hide theft.

Microteach 8.5 · 1:15Petty cash

A fixed imprest fund, replenished with one entry that records the expenses.

Textbook: §8.4, pp. 547–550

Read the script

Writing a check for a three dollar stamp makes no sense. So businesses keep a small, tightly controlled pot of cash called petty cash.

An imprest petty cash fund is a fixed amount, say two hundred dollars, kept by one custodian. Every payment needs a receipt, and the fund is topped back up to exactly two hundred.

To set it up, write a check for two hundred dollars. Debit petty cash, credit cash. Until the fund's size changes, that's the only entry to the petty cash account.

At month-end the box holds receipts for postage forty-five, supplies sixty and delivery seventy, plus twenty-two dollars in cash. That's one ninety-seven. The fund is three dollars short.

To replenish, write a check for one seventy-eight, bringing the box back to two hundred. Debit each expense from the receipts, debit cash over and short for the three dollar shortage, and credit cash. Notice petty cash itself isn't touched.

Quick check. When the fund is replenished, which account is credited? Pause and pick.

It's B, cash. The check to top it up comes from the main bank account.

Microteach 8.6 · 1:32The bank reconciliation

Deposits in transit and outstanding checks fix the bank side; only book-side items get journal entries.

Textbook: §8.6, pp. 552–554

Read the script

Redwood River's books say it has fifteen thousand and fifty dollars in the bank. The bank says fifteen thousand two hundred. Which is right? Probably neither, yet.

Start with the bank side. Fifteen thousand two hundred per the bank statement. Add a deposit in transit, eighteen hundred, made on the last day but not yet processed. Subtract outstanding checks, twenty-one hundred, written but not yet cashed. Adjusted bank balance: fourteen thousand nine hundred.

Now the book side. Fifteen thousand and fifty per the books. Add a note the bank collected for us, two eighty, and interest, twenty. Subtract a customer's check that bounced, called NSF, four twenty, and a service charge, thirty. Adjusted book balance: fourteen thousand nine hundred. They agree.

Here's the rule. Only the book-side items need journal entries, because the books didn't know about them. The bank-side items will clear by themselves.

Debit cash three hundred, credit notes receivable two eighty and interest revenue twenty. Then debit accounts receivable four twenty, because the customer still owes us, and bank service charges thirty, and credit cash four fifty.

Quick check. An outstanding check of six hundred dollars appears on the reconciliation. What journal entry is needed? Pause and pick.

None. It's a bank-side item. The books already recorded the check when it was written.

Microteach 10.1 · 1:14Tangible and intangible long-term assets

Property, plant and equipment versus patents, copyrights and goodwill.

Textbook: §11.1, pp. 700–703

Read the script

Some assets you use up in a month. Others serve the business for years. Those long-term assets come in two kinds.

Long-term assets are used in running the business for more than one year, and aren't held for sale to customers. A truck that Redwood River drives is a long-term asset. A truck on a dealer's lot is inventory.

Tangible assets have physical substance: land, buildings, equipment, vehicles, and natural resources like timber or gravel. Together, the first four are called property, plant and equipment.

Intangible assets have no physical form but give valuable rights: patents, copyrights, trademarks, franchises and licenses, and goodwill.

The cost of each is spread over its useful life, but the name changes. Plant assets are depreciated. Natural resources are depleted. Intangibles are amortized. And land isn't depreciated at all, because it doesn't wear out.

Quick check. Which of these long-term assets is not depreciated? Pause and pick.

It's C, land. It has an unlimited useful life.

Microteach 10.2 · 1:18What goes into cost

The price plus every cost to get the asset ready for use, such as freight and installation.

Textbook: §11.2, pp. 704–708

Read the script

Redwood River buys a delivery truck for forty thousand dollars. But the truck doesn't go on the books at forty thousand. Here's why.

Under the cost principle, a long-term asset is recorded at its price plus every reasonable and necessary cost of getting it ready for its intended use.

So the truck's cost is forty thousand, plus two thousand seven hundred fifty of Minnesota sales tax, plus two fifty to paint the logo on it, forty-three thousand. The first year's insurance is different. It's a cost of running the truck, not of getting it ready, so it's a prepaid expense.

For land, include the price, closing costs, and even the cost of tearing down an old building on it. Paving, fencing and lighting are separate land improvements, because unlike land, they wear out and are depreciated.

Redwood River pays thirteen thousand down and finances thirty thousand. Debit vehicles forty-three thousand, credit cash and notes payable.

Quick check. Which of these is part of a new machine's cost? Pause and pick.

It's B, installation and testing. It's needed to get the machine ready for use. The others are running costs.

Microteach 10.3 · 1:11Straight-line depreciation

(Cost − salvage value) ÷ useful life, the same every year.

Textbook: §11.3, pp. 709–720

Read the script

The simplest and most common depreciation method charges the same amount every year. Here's how it works.

Every method needs three things. Cost, which for the truck is forty-three thousand. Salvage value, what it should be worth at the end, three thousand. And useful life, five years.

Straight-line depreciation is cost minus salvage value, divided by useful life. Forty-three thousand minus three thousand is the depreciable base, forty thousand. Divided by five years, that's eight thousand dollars a year.

Each year, book value drops by eight thousand: thirty-five, twenty-seven, nineteen, eleven, and finally three thousand. That's the salvage value. You never depreciate below it.

Each year-end, debit depreciation expense eight thousand, credit accumulated depreciation.

Quick check. Cost twenty thousand, salvage two thousand, life six years. What's annual straight-line depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's B, three thousand. Eighteen thousand divided by six.

Microteach 10.4 · 1:07Units-of-production depreciation

Depreciation follows how much the asset is actually used.

Textbook: §11.3, pp. 709–720

Read the script

A truck parked all winter wears out less than one driven every day. Units of production ties depreciation to actual use.

The units-of-production method charges depreciation according to use, measured in miles, hours or units produced, instead of time.

First, find the rate per unit. The truck's depreciable base is forty thousand, and it should last one hundred thousand miles. That's forty cents per mile.

Then multiply by actual use each year. Thirty thousand miles in year one, twelve thousand dollars. Fifteen thousand in year two, six thousand. Twenty-five thousand in year three, ten thousand.

It matches expense to wear and tear better than any other method, but you need a good estimate of total use, and careful records of actual use each year.

Quick check. The rate is forty cents a mile, and the truck is driven twenty thousand miles. What's depreciation? Pause and pick.

It's A, eight thousand dollars.

Microteach 10.5 · 1:17Double-declining-balance depreciation

Twice the straight-line rate on book value, front-loading the expense.

Textbook: §11.3, pp. 709–720

Read the script

A new truck loses value fastest in its first years. Double-declining balance front-loads depreciation to reflect that.

Double-declining balance takes twice the straight-line rate and applies it to book value at the start of each year. Five years means a straight-line rate of twenty percent, so the DDB rate is forty percent.

Two traps. You ignore salvage value when you calculate each year's charge. But you must stop when book value reaches salvage value.

Year one, forty-three thousand times forty percent, seventeen thousand two hundred. Year two, forty percent of the remaining twenty-five eight, ten thousand three twenty. Then six thousand one ninety-two, then about thirty-seven fifteen. In year five, you take only enough, about twenty-five seventy-three, to bring book value down to three thousand.

Both methods charge forty thousand in total. DDB just charges more early and less later, which lowers profit in the early years.

Quick check. An asset has a four-year life. What's the double-declining-balance rate? Pause and pick.

It's C, fifty percent. The straight-line rate is twenty-five percent, doubled.

Microteach 10.6 · 1:12Partial years and revised estimates

A change in useful life or salvage value applies from now on, not to past years.

Textbook: §11.3, pp. 709–720; §11.5, p. 723 onward

Read the script

Assets aren't always bought on January first, and estimates aren't always right. Here's how to handle both.

If Redwood River bought the truck on April first, it was used for nine months of the first year. Straight-line depreciation for that year is eight thousand times nine twelfths, six thousand dollars.

Useful life and salvage value are estimates. When new information comes along, say the truck will clearly last longer, you revise the estimate.

A change in estimate is applied prospectively: to the current year and future years only. You never go back and change depreciation already recorded.

After two full years of straight-line, book value is twenty-seven thousand. Dana now expects four more years, not three, with the same salvage. Spread the remaining twenty-four thousand over four years: six thousand a year from now on.

Quick check. A revised estimate of useful life affects depreciation in which years? Pause and pick.

It's B, the current and future years. Past years stay as recorded.

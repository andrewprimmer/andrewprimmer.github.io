Microteach 11.1 · 1:21Capital versus revenue expenditures

Improvements that extend life are capitalized; routine repairs are expensed.

Textbook: §11.2, pp. 704–708

Read the script

Two bills arrive for the delivery truck: one for an oil change, one for a new engine. One is an expense. The other goes on the balance sheet.

A revenue expenditure is an ordinary cost that keeps an asset in normal working order: oil changes, tune-ups, new tires. It benefits only the current period, so it's expensed right away.

A capital expenditure makes the asset better than it was. It extends its life, increases its capacity, or makes it more efficient. It benefits future periods, so it's capitalized: added to the asset and depreciated.

So the hundred and twenty dollar oil change is repairs expense. The six thousand dollar engine adds three years to the truck's life, so it's capitalized and depreciated over the new remaining life.

The choice matters. Capitalizing a cost that should be expensed overstates assets and this year's profit. WorldCom hid billions of dollars of operating costs exactly that way.

Quick check. Replacing a warehouse roof in a way that adds ten years to its life is which kind of expenditure? Pause and pick.

It's B, a capital expenditure. It benefits future periods.

Microteach 11.2 · 1:23Selling or scrapping an asset

Update depreciation, remove cost and accumulated depreciation, then record the gain or loss.

Textbook: §11.5, p. 723 onward

Read the script

Eventually every asset leaves the business. Sold, traded or scrapped, the disposal follows the same three steps.

First, record depreciation up to the date of disposal. Second, remove both the asset's cost and its accumulated depreciation from the books. Third, record any cash received, and the gain or loss.

Redwood River sells equipment that cost eight thousand, with four thousand of accumulated depreciation, so a book value of four thousand. It sells for thirty-five hundred. That's a five hundred dollar loss.

Debit cash thirty-five hundred. Debit accumulated depreciation four thousand to remove it. Debit loss on sale five hundred. Credit equipment eight thousand.

Sell for more than book value and you have a gain. Sell for less and you have a loss. If the asset is scrapped for nothing, the loss equals its whole book value. Gains and losses appear below operating income on the multi-step income statement.

Quick check. Cost ten thousand, accumulated depreciation seven thousand, sold for four thousand. What's the result? Pause and pick.

It's A, a gain of one thousand. Book value is three thousand, and it sold for four.

Microteach 11.3 · 1:12Impairment

Write the asset down when its carrying value cannot be recovered.

Textbook: §11.5, p. 723 onward

Read the script

Depreciation spreads cost gradually. But sometimes an asset loses value suddenly. That's an impairment.

An asset is impaired when its carrying value, cost less accumulated depreciation, can no longer be recovered through using it or selling it.

Warning signs include physical damage, like a flood, a sharp fall in market value, new technology that makes the asset obsolete, or a big change in how it's used.

A spring flood damages Redwood River's grain dryer. Its carrying value is twenty thousand, but the future cash it can generate won't cover that, and its fair value is now twelve thousand. The impairment loss is eight thousand.

Debit impairment loss eight thousand and credit accumulated depreciation. Future depreciation is then based on the new, lower value. Under US GAAP, the loss can't be reversed if the value later recovers.

Quick check. After an impairment, future depreciation is based on what? Pause and pick.

It's B, the new, lower carrying value.

Microteach 11.4 · 1:14Natural resources and depletion

Timber, minerals and oil are expensed as they are extracted.

Textbook: §11.3, pp. 709–720

Read the script

Dana buys a gravel pit outside town. Every truckload taken out uses up part of the asset. That's depletion.

Natural resources, like timber, minerals, oil and gas, are long-term assets that are physically used up as they're extracted. Spreading their cost is called depletion.

It works like units of production. The pit cost five hundred thousand, and the land should be worth fifty thousand once the gravel's gone. Geologists estimate nine hundred thousand tons. Four hundred fifty thousand over nine hundred thousand tons is fifty cents a ton.

In the first year, Redwood River extracts one hundred twenty thousand tons. Depletion is sixty thousand dollars.

Depletion is first added to the cost of the gravel inventory. Debit inventory sixty thousand, credit accumulated depletion. When the gravel is sold, that cost becomes cost of goods sold.

Quick check. Depletion works most like which depreciation method? Pause and pick.

It's C, units of production. Cost is spread by the quantity extracted.

Microteach 11.5 · 1:27Intangibles and amortization

Patents and copyrights are amortized; goodwill is tested for impairment instead.

Textbook: §11.4, pp. 721–722

Read the script

Some of a company's most valuable assets can't be touched: a patent, a brand, a reputation. Here's how accounting handles them.

Patents give exclusive rights to an invention, for twenty years from filing. Copyrights protect creative work, for the author's life plus seventy years. Trademarks protect names and logos and can be renewed indefinitely. Then there are franchises and licenses, and goodwill.

Intangibles with a limited life are amortized, usually straight-line, over the shorter of their legal or useful life. Redwood River buys a patent on a feed mixer for thirty thousand, useful for ten years. Debit amortization expense three thousand a year. Credit the patent directly; no accumulated account is usually needed.

Goodwill arises only when one business buys another and pays more than the fair value of its identifiable net assets. It reflects things like loyal customers and a skilled workforce.

Public companies don't amortize goodwill or other intangibles with indefinite lives. They test them for impairment at least once a year instead. And internally built goodwill is never recorded at all.

Quick check. When can a company record goodwill? Pause and pick.

It's B. Goodwill is recorded only when it's purchased in an acquisition.

Microteach 11.6 · 1:19Reporting long-term assets

Balance sheet presentation and the asset turnover ratio.

Textbook: §11.5, p. 723 onward

Read the script

All those long-term asset entries end up in a few lines of the balance sheet, and one ratio tells you how hard they're working.

Property, plant and equipment is shown at cost, less accumulated depreciation, to give a net figure. Natural resources and intangibles are shown separately, net of depletion and amortization.

The notes to the financial statements disclose the depreciation methods and useful lives used, the major classes of assets, and any impairments, so readers can compare companies that made different choices.

The asset turnover ratio divides net sales by average total assets. It shows how many dollars of sales each dollar of assets generates.

If Redwood River had net sales of fifty-eight thousand and average total assets of forty thousand, its asset turnover is one point four five. Each dollar of assets produced a dollar forty-five of sales. Compare it with similar businesses, because it varies a lot by industry.

Quick check. Net sales ninety thousand, average total assets sixty thousand. What's the asset turnover? Pause and pick.

It's B, one point five.

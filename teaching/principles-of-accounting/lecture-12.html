Microteach 12.1 · 1:12What makes a liability current?

It is due within a year or the operating cycle, and paid from current assets.

Textbook: §12.1, pp. 746–754

Read the script

A lender looking at Redwood River wants to know two things about its debts: how much, and how soon. The how soon is what makes a liability current.

A current liability is an obligation due within one year, or within the operating cycle if that's longer, that will be paid using current assets or by creating another current liability.

The common ones are accounts payable to suppliers, short-term notes payable, accrued wages, interest and taxes, sales tax collected from customers, unearned revenue, and the part of long-term debt due within the next year.

Most current liabilities have a known amount, like an invoice. Some, like warranty repairs, have to be estimated. We'll cover both in this lecture.

Getting the classification right matters because the current ratio and working capital, the measures lenders use to judge short-term safety, depend on it.

Quick check. Which of these is not a current liability? Pause and pick.

It's C. Most of a fifteen-year mortgage is long-term. Only next year's principal would be current.

Microteach 12.2 · 1:19Accounts and short-term notes payable

Accrue interest on notes at period end.

Textbook: §12.1, pp. 746–754; §12.4, pp. 773–776

Read the script

In the fall, Redwood River borrows from the bank to stock up for winter. That loan, a short-term note, brings interest with it.

Accounts payable are amounts owed to suppliers for goods or services bought on credit, with no formal note and usually no interest. You've recorded these since Lecture 3.

On November first, Redwood River signs a six-month note for ten thousand dollars at six percent. Debit cash, credit notes payable.

At year-end, two months of interest have built up: ten thousand times six percent times two twelfths, one hundred dollars. Debit interest expense, credit interest payable.

On May first, Redwood River repays ten thousand plus six months' interest, three hundred. Debit notes payable ten thousand, interest payable one hundred to clear the accrual, and interest expense two hundred for this year's four months. Credit cash ten thousand three hundred.

Quick check. A twelve thousand dollar note at five percent is signed October first. How much interest is accrued at December thirty-first? Pause and pick.

It's B, one hundred fifty. Twelve thousand times five percent times three twelfths.

Microteach 12.3 · 1:24Sales tax payable

Sales tax collected belongs to the state, so it is a liability, not revenue.

Textbook: §12.2, pp. 755–763

Read the script

When a customer pays sales tax at Redwood River's counter, that money doesn't belong to Redwood River. It belongs to the State of Minnesota.

The business collects sales tax on the state's behalf and must pay it over, usually monthly or quarterly. So it's a liability until it's paid, never revenue.

A customer buys one thousand dollars of supplies. At Minnesota's state rate of six point eight seven five percent, the tax is sixty-eight seventy-five. Debit cash ten sixty-eight seventy-five. Credit sales one thousand and sales tax payable sixty-eight seventy-five. Local sales taxes can add more.

At the end of the month, Redwood River files its return and pays everything it collected, say forty-one twenty-five. Debit sales tax payable, credit cash. No expense, because the money was never Redwood River's.

Not every sale is taxable. A customer buying goods to resell, for example, can give an exemption certificate. The accountant needs to know which sales are which.

Quick check. A five hundred dollar sale with six percent sales tax. How much is credited to sales? Pause and pick.

It's B, five hundred. The thirty dollars of tax goes to sales tax payable.

Microteach 12.4 · 1:06Unearned revenue revisited

Gift cards and customer deposits stay liabilities until delivered.

Textbook: §12.1, pp. 746–754

Read the script

In December, Redwood River sells gift cards for the holidays. Cash in, but nothing delivered. You've seen this before, in Lecture 4.

Unearned revenue is cash received for goods or services not yet delivered. It's a liability because the business owes the customer either the goods or a refund.

Redwood River sells two thousand dollars of gift cards. Debit cash, credit unearned revenue.

In January, customers spend five hundred of those cards. Debit unearned revenue, credit sales five hundred, plus the usual cost of goods sold entry. The rest stays a liability.

Other examples are subscriptions, season tickets, customer deposits and prepaid service contracts. Airlines carry billions of dollars of it for tickets sold for future flights.

Quick check. A gift card is redeemed. What happens to the liability? Pause and pick.

It decreases. The promise has been kept, so it becomes revenue.

Microteach 12.5 · 1:08Current portion of long-term debt

Next year's principal moves into current liabilities.

Textbook: §12.1, pp. 746–754

Read the script

Redwood River has a five-year bank loan. It's long-term debt. But part of it is due next year. So which is it?

The current portion of long-term debt is the part of the principal due within the next year. It's reported as a current liability, and the rest stays long-term.

Redwood River owes twenty-five thousand at year-end, repayable at five thousand a year. So five thousand is shown as a current liability, and twenty thousand as long-term.

There's no journal entry for this. It's simply how the balance is split when the balance sheet is prepared. Each year, the next slice moves into current liabilities.

If the five thousand were left in long-term debt, current liabilities would look smaller and the current ratio better than it really is. That could mislead a lender.

Quick check. A sixty thousand dollar loan is repaid at twelve thousand a year. What's the current portion? Pause and pick.

It's C, twelve thousand. The other forty-eight thousand stays long-term.

Microteach 12.6 · 1:21Contingent liabilities and warranties

Probable and estimable: record it; reasonably possible: disclose it.

Textbook: §12.3, pp. 764–772

Read the script

A customer sues Redwood River. Nobody knows yet whether it will have to pay. Should anything go on the balance sheet?

A contingent liability is a possible obligation that depends on how some future event turns out, like a lawsuit or a product warranty.

It depends on two questions: how likely, and can it be estimated? If a loss is probable and can be reasonably estimated, record it. If it's reasonably possible, or probable but can't be estimated, disclose it in the notes. If it's remote, do nothing.

Warranties are the classic probable, estimable contingency. Redwood River sells fifty thousand of equipment with a one-year warranty, and expects repairs to cost two percent. Record warranty expense and a warranty liability of one thousand, in the year of the sale.

When a customer brings in a mixer for a warranty repair that uses two hundred forty dollars of parts, debit the warranty liability, not an expense, and credit parts inventory.

Quick check. A lawsuit loss is reasonably possible, but not probable. How is it treated? Pause and pick.

It's B, disclose it in the notes. Only probable, estimable losses are recorded.

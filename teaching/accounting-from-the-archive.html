    How to use this

    Accounting is often taught as a set of rules. It is better understood as a set of answers to practical problems that real businesses faced: how to tell investors in London what was happening to their money in Mozambique or Colombia, how to decide what counted as profit, and how much of that profit a government could tax.

    Each lesson below introduces one foundational idea, shows it at work in a historical company drawn from my research on British overseas enterprise between about 1890 and 1940, and then asks you to try it yourself. A short check at the end of each lesson tells you whether the idea has landed. Your progress is saved in this browser.

    Figures in the exercises are simplified for teaching and are not taken from the companies' actual accounts unless stated. The historical context is real.

    Lesson 1

    The accounting equation

    Case: the Beira Railway, Portuguese East Africa

    Every business has assets: things it controls that will bring it future benefit, such as cash, buildings, locomotives or stocks of coal. Those assets had to be paid for by somebody. Either the business borrowed the money, which creates liabilities owed to lenders and suppliers, or the owners put it in and left profits in the business, which is equity.

    So at every moment:

    Assets = Liabilities + Equity

    The balance sheet (in IFRS language, the statement of financial position) is simply this equation written out in detail on a particular date. It always balances because it describes the same pool of resources twice: once by what they are, once by who has a claim on them.

    From the archive

    The Beira Railway was built in the 1890s from the port of Beira, in what is now Mozambique, inland towards Rhodesia. It was financed in London through a mixture of shares and fixed-interest debentures. A railway is an extreme case of a business whose assets are almost all fixed: track, bridges and rolling stock that cannot be sold off if traffic disappoints.

    In my research I ask how the balance sheet itself shaped what such a company could do. Once a large slice of the right-hand side is debt carrying a fixed interest charge, the equation tells a prospective investor exactly how little room is left for the shareholders, and that can become a barrier to raising the next round of capital.

    Related work: "Balance Sheet as Barrier" (working paper).

    Lesson 2

    Double entry: debits and credits

    Case: a trading business group in Colombia, 1899–1929

    Because every transaction has two sides, accountants record each one twice: as a debit (Dr) in one account and a credit (Cr) of the same amount in another. Debit simply means the left-hand side of an account; credit means the right. They are not "good" or "bad".

    Type of accountIncrease with aDecrease with a
    AssetsDebitCredit
    ExpensesDebitCredit
    LiabilitiesCreditDebit
    EquityCreditDebit
    Revenue (income)CreditDebit

    A useful memory aid: expenses and assets sit on the debit side, income and the claims of others on the credit side. If total debits equal total credits, the books are arithmetically in balance, which you can test by drawing up a trial balance.

    From the archive

    Double entry is centuries old, but it was the indispensable tool of companies operating across oceans. A business group trading in Colombia had to keep track of goods bought from local producers, freight on the river and by sea, debts owed by merchants abroad, and remittances arriving in London banks, often in different currencies and months apart. Without a disciplined double-entry system, owners at a distance had no way to know whether the enterprise was making money at all.

    Related work: Primmer, "Finding El Dorado: The Rise and Fall of the Jenks Business Group in Colombia, 1899–1929," Business History Review 99 (2025): 121–146.

    Lesson 3

    Profit, accruals and revenue recognition

    Case: a modern warning, Quindell plc, 2013

    The income statement (or profit and loss account) reports what a business earned and what it cost to earn it over a period. Revenue minus expenses equals profit.

    The crucial point is timing. Under the accruals basis, revenue is recorded when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash changes hands. A ship that delivers cargo in December has earned its freight in December, even if the customer pays in February. Wages that staff have earned by 31 December are an expense of that year, even if they are paid in January.

    Modern standards (IFRS 15) say revenue is recognised when the business satisfies its promise to a customer, and only to the extent that it is highly probable the amount will not later have to be reversed. That second test is where judgement, and sometimes trouble, enters.

    Then and now

    Historical companies wrestled with the same question in simpler form: when exactly has a railway, a mine or a trading house earned its income? The problem has not gone away. Quindell, an AIM-listed insurance services and claims business, grew rapidly by recognising revenue on personal injury claims long before the outcome of those claims was known. When its accounts were reviewed, the 2013 results were restated and the reported profits largely disappeared, and both the company and its auditor became the subject of regulatory investigation.

    The lesson for a new accountant: the revenue figure is not a fact you read off a bank statement. It is the product of a judgement about when something has really been earned.

    Related work: a working paper on model-based revenue recognition at Quindell.

    Key idea. Profit depends on timing. Revenue belongs to the period in which it is earned and expenses to the period in which they are incurred, regardless of when cash moves.
    Lesson 4

    Capital, revenue and depreciation

    Case: Victorian railways and the double-account system

    Some spending buys something that will serve the business for many years: a locomotive, a bridge, a new branch line. That is capital expenditure, recorded as an asset on the balance sheet. Other spending is used up in the period: wages, coal, routine repairs. That is revenue expenditure, charged straight to the income statement as an expense.

    Getting this split right matters enormously. Treat a repair as an asset and this year's profit looks higher than it should. To spread the cost of a long-lived asset over the years that benefit from it, accountants charge depreciation: a portion of the asset's cost recognised as an expense each year.

    From the archive

    British railways raised more capital than any industry before them, and the boundary between capital and revenue was fought over from the start. In the 1840s George Hudson, the "Railway King", was found to have paid dividends partly out of capital rather than profits. The Regulation of Railways Act 1868 later required railway companies to report under the double-account system: a capital account showing money raised and how it was spent on the line, kept separate from a revenue account showing each year's receipts and working expenses.

    Under this system the line itself was typically not depreciated. Instead, the cost of renewing worn track and stock was charged to revenue when the work was done. That made profits lumpy, and it gave directors an incentive to describe renewals as improvements so they could be charged to capital instead.

    Related work: a dataset and working paper on Victorian railway capital markets.

    Lesson 5

    Reading the accounts: ratios

    Case: the operating ratio and Victorian railway investors

    Financial statements become useful when you compare figures with each other. A ratio turns raw numbers of very different sizes into something comparable across companies and years.

    Railway investors had their own favourite: the operating ratio, working expenses as a percentage of gross receipts. A ratio of 55% means 55p of every £1 of traffic receipts was swallowed by running the line, leaving 45p of net receipts to pay lenders and then shareholders. Its modern descendants are the operating margin and the cost-to-income ratio used for banks.

    A second classic measure is interest cover: net receipts divided by interest payable. It tells lenders how many times over the business could meet its interest bill.

    From the archive

    Victorian investors could choose between hundreds of railway securities. A coal-export line in South Wales such as the Barry Railway handled dense, profitable traffic; a rural line through mid-Wales such as the Cambrian ran long distances through thinly populated country. My current project uses published railway accounts to test how far investors priced these differences, and whether the operating ratio was the figure that mattered most for the interest rate a railway had to pay.

    Related work: a Victorian railway capital markets project (in progress).

    Lesson 6

    Disclosure: what the accounts tell, and what they hide

    Cases: the Royal Mail case (1931) and Coca-Cola's sales figures (1932)

    The people who own a company rarely run it. Managers know far more than shareholders and lenders, a gap economists call information asymmetry. Published financial statements exist to narrow that gap, and disclosure rules decide what managers must reveal.

    Today UK companies must give a true and fair view, and standards dictate a great deal of detail. In the early twentieth century, the law required far less, and companies chose how much to say. The choices they made, and the stories they told in the chairman's speech and directors' report, are a central theme of my research.

    From the archive

    The Royal Mail Steam Packet Company was one of Britain's great shipping groups. Through the 1920s it was trading at a loss, yet it reported profits and paid dividends by quietly drawing on secret reserves, amounts set aside in earlier, better years and not shown separately. The accounts were technically worded so as not to lie outright. In 1931 its chairman, Lord Kylsant, was convicted over a misleading prospectus; the auditor was acquitted. The case shook confidence in British company reporting and fed into the stricter disclosure of the Companies Act 1948.

    Disclosure could also move in the other direction. In 1932 The Coca-Cola Company stopped publishing its sales figures, a year before the United States passed its Securities Act. Companies weighed what investors wanted against what competitors could learn.

    Related work: "Disclosing Less by Law" (on Coca-Cola), and papers on narrative disclosure in British multinationals and the United Fruit Company's 1930 annual report.

    What would the shareholders have seen?

    A simplified shipping company's results for one year. Switch between the figure published and what was really happening.

    Key idea. Accounts are only as useful as what they disclose. Rules on disclosure, and the principle of a true and fair view, exist because history shows how easily selective reporting can mislead.
    Lesson 7

    Accounting profit, taxable profit and borders

    Case: Rhodesia Railways, 1926–1946

    The profit in the accounts is not automatically the profit that gets taxed. Tax law has its own rules: some expenses are not deductible, and depreciation is usually replaced by the tax system's own capital allowances. So accountants reconcile accounting profit to taxable profit before calculating the tax bill.

    A harder problem arises when one business operates in several countries. Which country gets to tax which part of the profit? Today multinational groups use transfer pricing and allocation rules agreed through bodies such as the OECD. Before those existed, the question had to be settled by accountants, administrators and courts.

    From the archive

    Rhodesia Railways ran a single, physically continuous network across several jurisdictions. A train carrying copper from the north to the coast earned one fare, but crossed several territorial borders on the way. There was no statute saying how the profit should be divided, so it had to be constructed through accounting choices and argued over in the courts, with litigation reaching the Privy Council in 1933.

    Related work: "Constructing a Cross-Border Tax Base Without Statutory Allocation Rules: Accounting and Adjudication in Rhodesia Railways, 1926–1946" (under review).

Microteach 6.1 · 1:20Peanut-butter costing

Spreading overhead evenly over diverse products overcosts simple products and undercosts complex ones.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Spread overhead evenly like peanut butter, and some products look cheaper than they are. Others look more expensive. Here's why.

Broad averaging, or peanut-butter costing, assigns indirect costs uniformly to products that actually use resources in very different ways.

Prairie Gate builds four hundred standard utility trailers a year in long runs. It builds only a hundred livestock trailers, each custom-designed, with frequent setups. Their demands on overhead are very different.

With one rate of thirty-six dollars per labor hour, each utility trailer gets $2,520 of overhead and each livestock trailer $7,200. But labor hours don't capture design time or setups.

Because total overhead is fixed, if one product is undercosted, another must be overcosted. That's product-cost cross-subsidization.

Quick check. Utility trailers rarely win bids, while livestock trailers win easily. What does that suggest? Pause and pick.

It's B. Overcosted utility trailers are bid too high, and undercosted livestock trailers too low.

Microteach 6.2 · 1:13Refining a costing system

Refine costs by tracing more directly, using more cost pools, and using drivers that cause the costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

When product costs look wrong, the fix is to refine the costing system. There are three ways.

First, trace more costs as direct costs, for example by metering power to each machine. Second, split overhead into more pools of similar costs. Third, allocate each pool with a driver that actually causes it.

Signs a system needs refining: products differ a lot in complexity; overhead is a big and growing share of costs; managers can't explain why they win or lose bids; and products that look profitable don't seem to make money.

Activity-based costing is the best-known refinement. It builds cost pools around activities, like designing, setting up and welding, and allocates each with its own driver.

The logic of ABC: products consume activities, and activities consume resources. Cost each activity, then charge products for the activities they use.

Quick check. Which is not a refinement? Pause and pick.

It's D. A single plant-wide rate is the broad averaging we're trying to fix.

Microteach 6.3 · 1:24Activities and the cost hierarchy

Group activity costs by level: unit, batch, product-sustaining and facility-sustaining.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Not every cost rises with the number of trailers. The cost hierarchy sorts activities by what drives them.

Unit-level costs vary with each trailer, like welding. Batch-level costs vary with the number of batches, like setups. Product-sustaining costs support a product line or design, like engineering. Facility-sustaining costs keep the plant running, whatever is made.

Ana groups Prairie Gate's $1,728,000 of overhead into four activity pools: design, $360,000; setups, $240,000; welding, $648,000; and finishing and inspection, $480,000.

Facility-sustaining costs have no causal driver. Some companies allocate them on a rough basis; others leave them unallocated so they don't distort product decisions.

Batch-level thinking explains why small, custom orders are expensive: setting up for one livestock trailer takes as long as setting up for a run of twenty utility trailers.

Quick check. Engineering a new trailer model is which level? Pause and pick.

It's C. It supports a product, regardless of how many units or batches are made.

Microteach 6.4 · 1:34Building the ABC system

Compute a rate for each activity and charge products for the quantity of each driver they use.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Now let's build Prairie Gate's ABC system and recost both trailer lines.

Each pool is divided by its driver. Design: a hundred twenty dollars per design hour. Setups: $1,200 per setup. Welding: sixty dollars per machine hour. Finishing: ten dollars per labor hour.

Utility trailers use six hundred design hours, forty setups, six thousand machine hours and twenty-eight thousand labor hours: $760,000 of overhead, or $1,900 a trailer.

Livestock trailers use twenty-four hundred design hours, a hundred sixty setups, forty-eight hundred machine hours and twenty thousand labor hours: $968,000, or $9,680 a trailer.

Together, the two lines still absorb exactly $1,728,000. ABC doesn't change total overhead. It changes who carries it.

Quick check. A custom job uses thirty design hours, two setups, forty machine hours and a hundred twenty labor hours. What's its overhead under ABC? Pause and pick.

It's A. $3,600 plus $2,400 plus $2,400 plus $1,200 is $9,600.

Microteach 6.5 · 1:19ABC versus traditional costing

ABC shows which products were under- or overcosted, changing prices, bids and product mix.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

So how wrong was the old system? Let's compare.

Under the plant-wide rate, each utility trailer carried $2,520 of overhead. ABC says $1,900. Each livestock trailer carried $7,200; ABC says $9,680.

Utility trailers were overcosted by $620 each; livestock trailers undercosted by $2,480 each. The total, $248,000, shifts from one line to the other.

Now Karen can act. She can bid more aggressively on utility trailers, raise livestock prices or charge separately for custom design, charge for small runs that need extra setups, and offer standard options that cut design hours.

The old system was quietly steering Prairie Gate toward its least profitable work. That's the real cost of broad averaging.

Quick check. Under ABC, what happens to the reported cost of the livestock line? Pause and pick.

It's B. It rises, because the line uses most of the design and setup activity.

Microteach 6.6 · 1:13Is ABC worth it?

ABC pays off when products are diverse and overhead is large; time-driven ABC makes it cheaper.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

ABC costs money to build and maintain. When is it worth it?

ABC pays off when indirect costs are large, when products differ widely in volume and complexity, when much overhead is batch or product-level, and when competition is tight enough that pricing errors hurt.

The challenges: interviewing staff and measuring activities costs money; activity data goes out of date; facility-level costs are still allocated arbitrarily; and managers whose products suddenly look unprofitable may resist.

Time-driven ABC is a cheaper version. It estimates the cost per minute of each department's capacity and how many minutes each activity takes, rather than surveying staff about how they spend their time.

ABC isn't only for factories. Banks use it to cost accounts, hospitals to cost procedures, and retailers to cost customers and delivery.

Quick check. Which company is least likely to benefit from ABC? Pause and pick.

It's B. With one product, all overhead belongs to it anyway.

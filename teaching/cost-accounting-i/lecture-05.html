Microteach 5.1 · 1:21Journal entries under normal costing

Actual overhead is debited to MOH control; applied overhead is credited to MOH allocated and debited to WIP.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 4.

Read the script

Under normal costing, overhead travels on two separate tracks: what's actually spent, and what's applied to jobs.

During 2027, Prairie Gate's actual overhead costs, supervisors, utilities, depreciation and supplies, total $1,800,000. Each is debited to manufacturing overhead control.

Separately, as jobs are worked on, overhead is applied at thirty-six dollars per direct labor hour. Workers logged forty-nine thousand hours, so $1,764,000 is debited to work in process and credited to manufacturing overhead allocated.

At year-end, the two accounts don't match. Control shows $1,800,000 actually spent. Allocated shows $1,764,000 applied to jobs.

Every job, and so work in process, finished goods and cost of goods sold, carries overhead at the budgeted rate. The difference must be dealt with before the books close.

Quick check. Which entry applies overhead to jobs? Pause and pick.

It's B. Debit work in process, credit manufacturing overhead allocated.

Microteach 5.2 · 1:21Under- and overallocated overhead

Underallocated overhead means actual exceeded applied; overallocated means applied exceeded actual.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 4.

Read the script

Applied overhead almost never equals actual overhead. Here's how to measure the gap.

Prairie Gate spent $1,800,000 on overhead but applied only $1,764,000. Overhead is underallocated by $36,000: jobs were charged too little.

Underallocated, or underapplied, means actual exceeds allocated, so job costs were understated. Overallocated means allocated exceeds actual, so jobs were overcharged.

The gap has two possible causes. Overhead spending was different from budget, here $1,800,000 instead of $1,728,000. Or activity was different, here forty-nine thousand hours instead of forty-eight thousand. Lecture 14 separates these with variances.

The two overhead accounts are temporary. At year-end the difference is cleared out using one of three approaches.

Quick check. Actual overhead five twenty thousand, allocated five forty-five. What's the result? Pause and pick.

It's B. More was allocated than spent: overallocated by $25,000.

Microteach 5.3 · 1:11The adjusted allocation-rate approach

Restating every job at the actual rate turns normal costs into actual costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 4.

Read the script

The most accurate fix is to go back and recost every job at the actual overhead rate.

The actual overhead rate for 2027 is $1,800,000 divided by forty-nine thousand hours: $36.73 per hour.

Trailer four-eighteen took eighty hours, so its overhead rises from $2,880 to $2,939, an adjustment of $59. Every job, in work in process, finished goods and cost of goods sold, is restated the same way.

The result is actual costing: each job carries its true share of actual overhead. With computerized job records, this is easy to do.

Companies still keep the budgeted rate during the year for timely pricing and control, then adjust at year-end.

Quick check. Actual overhead six thirty thousand; actual machine hours twenty-one thousand. What's the actual rate? Pause and pick.

It's A, thirty dollars an hour.

Microteach 5.4 · 1:24Proration

Proration spreads the difference over WIP, finished goods and COGS in proportion to overhead allocated in each.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 4.

Read the script

A simpler way to get close to actual costs: spread the difference over the accounts that hold this year's overhead.

At year-end, ten percent of the overhead allocated this year sits in work in process, fifteen percent in finished goods, and seventy-five percent in cost of goods sold.

So the $36,000 underallocation is spread the same way: $3,600 to work in process, $5,400 to finished goods and $27,000 to cost of goods sold.

The entry closes both overhead accounts. Debit allocated for $1,764,000, debit the three accounts for their shares, and credit control for $1,800,000.

Prorating on the overhead component in each account gives the same balances as the adjusted-rate approach. Prorating on total ending balances is a rougher shortcut.

Quick check. Overhead is overallocated by twenty thousand, and cost of goods sold holds eighty percent of allocated overhead. What's the adjustment to cost of goods sold? Pause and pick.

It's B. Jobs were overcharged, so cost of goods sold falls by $16,000.

Microteach 5.5 · 1:26Write-off to cost of goods sold

Writing the whole difference off to COGS is simplest and fine when the amount is immaterial.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 4.

Read the script

The simplest approach of all: put the whole difference into cost of goods sold.

Debit allocated for $1,764,000, debit cost of goods sold for the full $36,000, and credit control for $1,800,000. Both overhead accounts are closed.

The adjusted allocation-rate approach is the most accurate and the most work. Proration comes close. Writing off to cost of goods sold is simplest but leaves inventories at budgeted-rate costs.

Materiality decides. A $36,000 difference is two percent of overhead, and most of it would end up in cost of goods sold anyway, so writing it off is reasonable. A large difference, or large inventories, call for proration.

The choice affects income. Writing off charges all $36,000 against this year's income. Proration leaves $9,000 in inventory, to be expensed when those trailers are sold.

Quick check. Compared with proration, how much lower is this year's income if Prairie Gate writes the whole $36,000 off to cost of goods sold? Pause and pick.

It's C, $9,000: the part proration would have left in inventory.

Microteach 5.6 · 1:27Department overhead rates

Separate rates for departments with different cost drivers give more accurate job costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 4.

Read the script

One plant-wide rate assumes every department's overhead is driven the same way. Often it isn't.

Welding's overhead is mostly robots, power and gas, driven by machine hours. Finishing's overhead is mostly supervision and labor-driven costs. One labor-hour rate can't capture both.

So Ana budgets a rate for each department: sixty dollars per machine hour in welding, and twenty-seven dollars per labor hour in finishing. Together they still total $1,728,000.

Trailer four-eighteen used twenty machine hours in welding and fifty labor hours in finishing. Department rates give it $2,550 of overhead, $330 less than the plant-wide rate. Trailers heavy on robot welding would get more.

More cost pools usually give more accurate job costs, as long as the benefit justifies the cost of the system. Activity-based costing, next lecture, takes this idea further.

Quick check. A job uses ten welding machine hours and thirty finishing labor hours. What's its overhead with department rates? Pause and pick.

It's A. $600 plus $810 is $1,410.

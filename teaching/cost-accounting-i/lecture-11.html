Microteach 11.1 · 1:32Single-rate and dual-rate allocation

Dual-rate allocation charges fixed costs by budgeted usage and variable costs by actual usage.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

Support departments don't make cheese, but cheese can't be made without them. How should their costs be charged to production?

Operating departments make the product: Mixing and Aging. Support departments serve them: Maintenance keeps the vats running and the Quality Lab tests every batch.

Maintenance has $120,000 of fixed costs and forty dollars of variable cost per hour. Mixing budgeted twenty-four hundred hours and Aging sixteen hundred. Actually Mixing used twenty-six hundred and Aging only a thousand.

A single rate lumps fixed and variable together: $280,000 over four thousand budgeted hours is seventy dollars an hour, charged on actual use. A dual rate charges fixed costs by each department's budgeted share, sixty and forty percent, and variable costs at forty dollars per actual hour.

The dual rate reflects how costs behave: Maintenance's fixed capacity was sized for the budgeted demand, so departments pay for what they asked for, while variable costs follow what they actually used.

Quick check. Under a single rate, Aging cuts its hours. What happens to Mixing's charge if fixed costs are spread on actual hours? Pause and pick.

It's B. The same fixed costs are spread over fewer hours, so Mixing pays more, through no fault of its own.

Microteach 11.2 · 1:21Budgeted or actual rates?

Use budgeted rates so user departments aren't charged for the support department's inefficiency.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

Should departments be charged at budgeted rates or actual rates? It matters for fairness and for planning.

A budgeted rate is known in advance, so user departments can plan, and any overspending stays with the support department that caused it. An actual rate passes the support department's inefficiency on to its users.

If Maintenance overspends by $20,000, budgeted rates leave that variance with Maintenance's manager, who controls it. Actual rates would push it onto Mixing and Aging, who don't.

For fixed costs, allocating by budgeted usage charges users for the capacity they requested. Allocating by practical capacity leaves the cost of unused capacity with the support department, where managers can see it. Actual usage is the worst choice.

One catch: if fixed costs are charged on budgeted usage, departments may underestimate their needs to pay less. Comparing budgets with actual use, and rewarding accuracy, keeps estimates honest.

Quick check. Who should bear the cost of a support department's inefficiency? Pause and pick.

It's B, the support department, which controls it. Budgeted rates achieve that.

Microteach 11.3 · 1:23The direct method

The direct method ignores services between support departments and allocates straight to operating departments.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

With two support departments that also serve each other, there are three allocation methods. The simplest is the direct method.

Maintenance costs $300,000; twenty percent of its work is for the Quality Lab, fifty percent for Mixing and thirty for Aging. The Quality Lab costs $200,000; ten percent of its work is for Maintenance, sixty for Mixing and thirty for Aging.

The direct method ignores the services support departments give each other. Maintenance's cost is split between Mixing and Aging in the ratio fifty to thirty. The Lab's in the ratio sixty to thirty. Mixing gets $320,833 and Aging $179,167.

It's simple and widely used, but it ignores the real services the support departments provide each other.

Whatever the method, the whole $500,000 ends up in the operating departments. Only the split changes.

Quick check. Direct method: a support department gives twenty-five percent to another support department, forty-five to A and thirty to B. What share goes to A? Pause and pick.

It's B. Forty-five out of seventy-five is sixty percent.

Microteach 11.4 · 1:28The step-down method

The step-down method allocates support departments in sequence, recognizing some support-to-support services.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

The step-down method recognizes some of the services support departments give each other, one step at a time.

Support departments are allocated in sequence. The first is spread over all the other departments, including the other support department. Once a department has been allocated, nothing is allocated back to it.

A common rule is to start with the department that provides the highest percentage of its services to other support departments. Maintenance gives twenty percent to the Lab; the Lab gives ten percent to Maintenance. So Maintenance goes first.

Maintenance's $300,000 is split twenty, fifty and thirty percent: $60,000 to the Lab, $150,000 to Mixing and $90,000 to Aging. The Lab now has $260,000, split between Mixing and Aging in the ratio sixty to thirty. Mixing gets $323,333 and Aging $176,667.

The order matters: starting with the Lab would give a different answer. That's a weakness of the method.

Quick check. In the step-down method, can a support department receive costs after its own have been allocated? Pause and pick.

It's B, no. That's what makes it step-down.

Microteach 11.5 · 1:27The reciprocal method

The reciprocal method recognizes all services between support departments using simultaneous equations.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

The reciprocal method is the most accurate: it recognizes every service support departments provide each other.

Each support department's complete reciprocated cost is its own cost plus its share of the other's. Maintenance equals $300,000 plus ten percent of the Lab. The Lab equals $200,000 plus twenty percent of Maintenance.

Substitute the Lab's equation into Maintenance's. M equals $320,000 plus point zero two M, so M is $326,531. The Lab is $200,000 plus twenty percent of that: $265,306.

Now allocate each reciprocated cost using the full percentages. Mixing gets $322,449 and Aging $177,551. Together, $500,000.

The three methods give similar totals here, but with bigger reciprocal services the differences grow. The reciprocal method is the most accurate and, with spreadsheets, no longer hard to compute.

Quick check. Which method fully recognizes services between support departments? Pause and pick.

It's C, the reciprocal method.

Microteach 11.6 · 1:23Allocating common costs

Common costs can be shared by the stand-alone or the incremental method.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 16.

Read the script

When two users share one resource, how should they split its cost? There are two main methods.

A common cost is the cost of operating a facility or activity shared by two or more users, where sharing costs less than each going alone.

Redwood Valley shares a refrigerated delivery truck with a sister dairy. On its own, the creamery would pay $48,000 a year; the dairy, $32,000. Sharing costs $60,000.

The stand-alone method splits the cost in proportion to stand-alone costs: sixty and forty percent, $36,000 and $24,000. The incremental method ranks users: the primary user, here the creamery, bears its full stand-alone cost, and the dairy pays only the extra $12,000.

The stand-alone method is usually seen as fairer, because both users share the savings. Under the incremental method, every user wants to be ranked second.

Quick check. Common cost ninety thousand; stand-alone costs sixty thousand each for A and B. Stand-alone allocation to A? Pause and pick.

It's B. Equal stand-alone costs split it fifty-fifty: $45,000.

Microteach 12.1 · 1:18Joint costs and splitoff

A joint process yields several products at once; joint costs must be allocated at the splitoff point.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Some processes make several products at once. You can't make one without the others. Their shared cost is a joint cost.

A joint cost is the cost of a single production process that yields two or more products at the same time.

Redwood Valley's separator spins a hundred thousand gallons of raw milk, costing $400,000 including processing, into twenty-five thousand gallons of cream and seventy-five thousand gallons of skim milk. The point where they become separate is the splitoff point.

Outputs with high sales value are joint products, or a main product if there's only one. Outputs with low value are byproducts. Outputs with minimal value are scrap. The classification can change as markets change: whey used to be waste, and now it's protein powder.

Joint costs must be allocated for inventory and cost of goods sold, cost-reimbursement contracts, insurance settlements and regulated pricing. But, as you'll see, never for deciding whether to process further.

Quick check. What's the splitoff point? Pause and pick.

It's B: where the joint products become separately identifiable.

Microteach 12.2 · 1:22Sales value at splitoff

Allocate joint costs in proportion to each product's sales value at the splitoff point.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

The most common method allocates joint costs by what each product could sell for at splitoff.

At splitoff, cream sells for eight dollars a gallon to other dairies, so the cream is worth $200,000. Skim milk sells for two dollars a gallon: $150,000. Total sales value at splitoff: $350,000.

Cream gets two hundred over three hundred fifty of the joint cost: $228,571. Skim gets $171,429.

This method follows the benefits-received criterion: the product that generates more revenue bears more of the cost. And it uses values at splitoff, before any further processing affects them.

It needs market prices at the splitoff point. When products can't be sold until they're processed further, use the net realizable value method instead.

Quick check. Joint cost sixty thousand; sales values at splitoff ninety thousand for A and thirty thousand for B. What's A's allocation? Pause and pick.

It's B. Three-quarters of $60,000 is $45,000.

Microteach 12.3 · 1:19The physical-measure method

Allocating by physical quantity is simple but ignores value and can make products look unprofitable.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Another approach allocates joint costs by physical quantity: gallons, pounds or tons. Simple, but risky.

By gallons, cream gets a quarter of the joint cost, $100,000, and skim gets three-quarters, $300,000.

Look at the margins. Under the sales value method, both products show the same negative margin percentage. Under the physical method, cream looks very profitable and skim shows a $150,000 loss, because skim is bulky but cheap.

But skim isn't really unprofitable: Redwood Valley can't make cream without making skim. A product's allocated margin depends on the method, not on its real contribution.

The physical method is used when market prices are volatile or unavailable, or when physical quantities clearly drive the joint costs. But it ignores revenue-generating power.

Quick check. Joint cost sixty thousand; output a thousand kilograms of A and three thousand of B. Physical-measure allocation to A? Pause and pick.

It's A. A quarter of $60,000: $15,000.

Microteach 12.4 · 1:38NRV and constant gross-margin methods

NRV allocates by final value less separable costs; constant gross margin gives every product the same margin %.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

When products are processed further before sale, allocation can use their final values. There are two ways.

Redwood Valley churns its cream into butter worth $350,000, after $90,000 of separable costs, and bottles its skim, worth $240,000 after $60,000 of bottling. Net realizable value is final sales value minus separable costs: $260,000 and $180,000.

The NRV method allocates joint costs by NRV: butter's cream gets two-sixty over four-forty, $236,364, and skim $163,636.

The constant gross-margin method gives every product the same gross margin percentage as the whole operation, here six point seven eight percent. Each product's total cost is ninety-three point two two percent of its revenue; subtract its separable costs to get its joint cost share.

When market prices at splitoff exist, the sales value at splitoff method is usually preferred: it's simple and isn't affected by later processing decisions. NRV is the fallback. Constant gross margin is the only method that can allocate negative joint costs.

Quick check. Final sales value eighty thousand; separable costs thirty thousand. What's the NRV? Pause and pick.

It's B, $50,000.

Microteach 12.5 · 1:21Sell or process further?

Process further if incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost; joint costs are irrelevant.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Should Redwood Valley sell its cream at splitoff or churn it into butter? Joint costs play no part in the answer.

Churning cream into butter raises revenue from $200,000 to $350,000, an extra $150,000, for an extra $90,000 of cost. Processing further adds $60,000.

Bottling skim adds $90,000 of revenue for $60,000 of cost: a gain of $30,000. Process both further.

The $400,000 joint cost, and how it was allocated, is irrelevant. It's incurred whether the cream is sold at splitoff or churned. Using allocated joint costs here can lead to the wrong choice.

Check the practical side too: can the market absorb more butter at that price, and is the churning capacity available?

Quick check. Sales value at splitoff fifty thousand; after processing seventy-two thousand; extra cost twenty-five thousand. Process further? Pause and pick.

It's B. Incremental revenue $22,000 is less than the $25,000 cost: sell at splitoff.

Microteach 12.6 · 1:25Accounting for byproducts

Byproducts can be recognized at production, reducing main product cost, or at sale, as revenue.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 17.

Read the script

Cheese making produces whey: lots of it, worth little. That makes it a byproduct. There are two ways to account for it.

In May, a cheese batch costing $120,000 also yields twenty thousand gallons of whey, which a local hog farmer buys at twenty-five cents a gallon: $5,000 of value. Sixteen thousand gallons are sold in May.

Under the production method, whey is recognized when produced: its $5,000 value reduces the joint cost assigned to cheese, to $115,000, and unsold whey is inventory at $1,000. Under the sales method, nothing happens until whey is sold; then the $4,000 of sales is reported as revenue or as a reduction of cost of goods sold.

The production method recognizes the byproduct's value when it's created, which is conceptually better. The sales method is simpler, but managers could time byproduct sales to manage income.

And byproducts can grow up. Whey protein is now valuable enough that some creameries treat whey as a joint product.

Quick check. Under the production method, eight thousand dollars of byproduct value does what to the main product's cost? Pause and pick.

It's B: it lowers it by $8,000.

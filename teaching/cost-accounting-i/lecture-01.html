Microteach 1.1 · 1:26Financial, management and cost accounting

Management accounting serves managers inside the firm; cost accounting measures the costs they need.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Financial accounting tells outsiders how a company did. Management accounting helps the people inside run it. Here's the difference.

Financial accounting reports to investors and lenders, follows GAAP and looks back at the whole company. Management accounting serves managers, follows no required rules, looks forward, and drills down into products, jobs and departments.

Cost accounting sits between the two. It measures, analyzes and reports the costs of acquiring and using resources. Inventory and cost of goods sold for the financial statements come from it, and so do the product costs managers use to price and plan.

Meet the company we'll follow this semester: Prairie Gate Trailers, which builds custom livestock and utility trailers to order in Marshall, Minnesota. Its owner, Karen Olsen, needs to know what each trailer really costs.

Karen's questions are management accounting questions. What did a particular trailer cost? What price should she bid? Why was a department over budget? None of these appear in the annual report.

Quick check. Which is a management accounting question? Pause and pick.

It's B. Product profitability is an internal decision question. The others are financial reporting.

Microteach 1.2 · 1:23Strategy and the value chain

Cost information supports strategy across every business function in the value chain.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

A company competes either by being cheaper or by being different. Cost accounting supports both strategies. Here's how.

A cost leader wins by having the lowest costs, like a maker of standard utility trailers. A differentiator wins by offering something customers value and will pay more for, like a custom trailer for show cattle. Prairie Gate does a bit of both.

The value chain is the sequence of business functions that make a product valuable to customers: research and development, design, production, marketing, distribution and customer service. Costs arise in every one of them, not only in the factory.

Customers judge a company on a handful of key success factors: cost, quality, time, innovation and, increasingly, sustainability. Management accountants track all five.

The supply chain stretches outside the company: from steel and axle suppliers, through Prairie Gate, to dealers and the farmers who buy the trailers. Cutting costs often means working with partners along it.

Quick check. Prairie Gate's warranty repairs belong to which value chain function? Pause and pick.

It's D, customer service. Costs after the sale are part of the value chain too.

Microteach 1.3 · 1:19The five-step decision process

Managers identify, gather, predict, decide, then implement and evaluate; planning and control link them.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Good decisions follow a pattern. Management accountants help at every step. Here's the five-step process.

First, identify the problem and the uncertainties. Second, obtain information. Third, make predictions about the future. Fourth, choose among alternatives. Fifth, implement the decision, evaluate performance and learn.

Karen wonders whether to add a line of standard stock trailers. Sales expects a hundred and twenty a year at $9,500 each. Cost accounting predicts $7,800 of cost per trailer. That's $204,000 of margin a year, before any new fixed costs.

Steps one to four are planning: choosing goals and how to reach them, summed up in a budget. Step five is control: comparing what happened with the plan in performance reports, then learning.

Feedback closes the loop. If actual costs come in higher than predicted, next year's predictions improve.

Quick check. Comparing June's actual welding costs with the budget is part of which step? Pause and pick.

It's D. Evaluating performance against the plan is control, step five.

Microteach 1.4 · 1:27Guidelines and the controller

Use cost–benefit thinking, consider behavior, and remember different costs for different purposes.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Three guidelines run through this whole course. And the person who usually applies them is the controller.

First, cost–benefit: better information is worth having only if its benefits exceed its cost. Second, systems shape behavior: how you measure people changes what they do. Third, different costs for different purposes: there's no single right cost for a product.

For the balance sheet, trailer four-eighteen's cost includes only factory costs. To price a repeat order, Karen needs all value chain costs, including design and delivery. To decide on a one-off special order, only the extra costs matter.

In most companies, the controller is the chief management accountant, responsible for cost systems, budgets and reporting. The treasurer handles cash and financing. Both report to the chief financial officer.

Accountants are usually staff, not line managers. Production managers make the decisions; accountants supply the analysis that helps them decide well.

Quick check. Karen considers a forty thousand dollar costing system that saves ten thousand a year for two years. Which guideline applies? Pause and pick.

It's B. Benefits of $20,000 don't justify $40,000 of cost.

Microteach 1.5 · 1:31Where cost accounting came from

Cost accounting grew from factory owners, railroads and engineers, and was reinvented in the 1980s.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 1.

Read the script

Cost accounting wasn't invented by a standard-setter. It grew out of factories, railroads and arguments about profit. Here's the short history.

In the 1770s, the potter Josiah Wedgwood analyzed his costs to survive a slump. In the 1850s, American railroads tracked cost per ton-mile. Engineers inspired by Frederick Taylor developed standard costs in the early twentieth century. DuPont formalized return on investment around 1914.

Wedgwood's costing in 1772 showed him which wares lost money and that unit costs fell as volume rose. Those are still core ideas: product costing and fixed costs spread over output.

By the 1980s, critics said cost systems were built for financial reporting, not decisions. Johnson and Kaplan's 1987 book, Relevance Lost, argued management accounting had stalled. Its answer, activity-based costing, is Lecture 6.

The lesson: costing systems reflect the businesses of their time. Labor-heavy factories allocated overhead by labor hours. Automated factories needed something better.

Quick check. What problem did Relevance Lost highlight? Pause and pick.

It's B. Product costs built for external reporting misled managers making decisions.

Microteach 1.6 · 1:21Professional ethics

The IMA's standards are competence, confidentiality, integrity and credibility.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 1. Also: IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice.

Read the script

Management accountants are often under pressure to make the numbers look better. Professional ethics tells them what to do.

The Institute of Management Accountants, which awards the C-M-A credential, sets out four overarching principles: honesty, fairness, objectivity and responsibility.

And four standards: competence, keeping your skills up; confidentiality, protecting information; integrity, avoiding conflicts of interest; and credibility, reporting information fairly and completely, including bad news.

Suppose Prairie Gate's plant manager, whose bonus depends on profit, asks the accountant to delay writing off scrapped steel until next year. That would misstate results: a credibility and integrity problem.

The IMA's advice: follow the organization's policies; discuss the issue with your supervisor, or the next level up if the supervisor is involved; get confidential advice, for example from an IMA ethics counselor; and consult a lawyer about your rights and obligations.

Quick check. An accountant shares Prairie Gate's bid prices with a friend at a rival. Which standard is broken? Pause and pick.

It's B, confidentiality.

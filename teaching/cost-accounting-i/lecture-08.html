Microteach 8.1 · 1:19Process costing basics

Process costing averages a department's costs over the identical units it produces.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

When every unit is identical, you don't need a job record for each one. You average. That's process costing.

Meet Redwood Valley Creamery, a Marshall cheese maker. Milk goes through two departments: Mixing, where it's cultured, curdled and pressed into blocks, and Aging, where blocks mature before sale. Every block is the same, so it uses process costing.

Job costing collects costs by job. Process costing collects them by department for a period, then divides by the units produced. Each department has its own work-in-process account.

In the simplest case, all work started in the month is finished. In February, Mixing spent $540,000 on milk and cultures and $324,000 on conversion costs, and finished eighteen thousand blocks: forty-eight dollars a block.

The complication comes when some blocks are only partly finished at the end of the month. That's where equivalent units come in.

Quick check. Costs two hundred fifty thousand; ten thousand units started and completed; no work in process. Cost per unit? Pause and pick.

It's B, twenty-five dollars.

Microteach 8.2 · 1:15Equivalent units

Equivalent units convert partly finished units into the number of whole units that work would make.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Four thousand blocks are only a quarter done. How many finished blocks is that worth? Equivalent units answer that.

Equivalent units are the number of complete units that could have been made with the work actually done. Multiply physical units by their percentage of completion, separately for each cost category.

In Mixing, all the milk is added at the start, so four thousand blocks in process are a hundred percent complete for materials: four thousand equivalent units. But they're only twenty-five percent through conversion: a thousand equivalent units.

That's why we compute equivalent units separately. Materials are often added at a particular point, while conversion costs are incurred evenly through the process.

Who decides a block is twenty-five percent complete? Supervisors estimate it, helped by process timings and sensors. The estimates matter, because they split costs between finished and unfinished units.

Quick check. Two thousand units are sixty percent complete for conversion. How many conversion equivalent units? Pause and pick.

It's B, twelve hundred.

Microteach 8.3 · 1:24The five-step approach

Summarize physical units, compute equivalent units, total the costs, compute cost per EU, assign costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Every process costing problem follows the same five steps. Learn them, and the method doesn't matter.

Step one: summarize the flow of physical units. Step two: compute equivalent units for each cost category. Step three: summarize the total costs to account for. Step four: compute the cost per equivalent unit. Step five: assign costs to units completed and to ending work in process.

Here's Mixing in March. It began with two thousand blocks, forty percent converted, carrying $56,000 of materials and $16,000 of conversion cost. It started eighteen thousand blocks, finished sixteen thousand and ended with four thousand, a quarter converted. March added $540,000 of materials and $324,000 of conversion cost.

There are two methods. Weighted average blends the work done last month on beginning inventory with this month's work. FIFO keeps them separate. This lecture covers weighted average; Lecture 9 covers FIFO.

One fact to remember for Mixing: all materials go in at the start, so every block in process is complete for materials.

Quick check. Which step comes after computing equivalent units? Pause and pick.

It's B: summarize the total costs to account for.

Microteach 8.4 · 1:22Weighted average: units

Weighted-average equivalent units = units completed + equivalent units in ending WIP.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Steps one and two under weighted average. The rule is simple: completed units, plus the equivalent units still in process.

Step one. Two thousand blocks in beginning inventory plus eighteen thousand started gives twenty thousand to account for. Sixteen thousand were completed and four thousand remain in process: twenty thousand accounted for.

Weighted-average equivalent units are units completed, which are a hundred percent done, plus the equivalent units in ending work in process. It doesn't matter whether the completed units started this month or last.

For materials: sixteen thousand completed plus four thousand in ending inventory, twenty thousand equivalent units. For conversion: sixteen thousand plus a quarter of four thousand, seventeen thousand.

Notice that the forty percent completion of beginning inventory never entered the calculation. Weighted average treats last month's work as if it were done this month.

Quick check. Nine thousand units completed; fifteen hundred in ending inventory, forty percent converted. Weighted-average conversion equivalent units? Pause and pick.

It's B. Nine thousand plus six hundred is ninety-six hundred.

Microteach 8.5 · 1:27Weighted average: costs

WA cost per EU = (beginning WIP cost + current cost) ÷ WA equivalent units.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Steps three to five. Add up the costs, divide by equivalent units, and assign.

Materials: $56,000 in beginning inventory plus $540,000 added, $596,000, divided by twenty thousand equivalent units: $29.80. Conversion: $16,000 plus $324,000, $340,000, divided by seventeen thousand: twenty dollars.

Each completed block costs $49.80: sixteen thousand of them is $796,800, transferred to Aging. Ending inventory gets four thousand materials units at $29.80 and a thousand conversion units at twenty dollars: $139,200. Together, $936,000, exactly the costs to account for.

Always check: the costs assigned must equal the total costs to account for, here $936,000.

Weighted average is simple, but it blends last month's costs with this month's, which can blur changes in efficiency or prices.

Quick check. Total conversion costs ninety thousand; weighted-average conversion equivalent units eighteen thousand. Cost per equivalent unit? Pause and pick.

It's A, five dollars.

Microteach 8.6 · 1:18Journal entries in process costing

Each department has its own WIP account; completed units transfer cost to the next department.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

In process costing, costs move from department to department, each with its own work-in-process account.

Materials put into Mixing debit work in process Mixing and credit materials: $540,000. Conversion costs, labor and applied overhead, debit work in process Mixing for $324,000.

Completed blocks move to Aging. Debit work in process Aging, credit work in process Mixing: $796,800. In Aging, these become transferred-in costs.

The Mixing T-account: $72,000 beginning balance, plus $540,000 and $324,000 added, less $796,800 transferred out, leaves $139,200, the ending inventory we computed.

Aging adds its own conversion costs, then transfers finished blocks to finished goods. That's Lecture 9.

Quick check. Which entry moves completed blocks from Mixing to Aging? Pause and pick.

It's B. Debit Aging, credit Mixing.

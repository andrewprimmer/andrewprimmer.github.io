Microteach 2.1 · 1:10Cost objects: direct and indirect

Direct costs are traced to a cost object; indirect costs must be allocated.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 2.

Read the script

Every cost question starts with: the cost of what? That 'what' is the cost object.

A cost object is anything you want to measure the cost of: a trailer, a product line, a customer, a department, even a project like a new paint booth.

Direct costs can be traced to the cost object in an economically feasible way, like the steel and axles in trailer four-eighteen. Indirect costs can't be traced, so they're allocated, like plant rent and the floor supervisor's salary.

Whether a cost is direct depends on materiality, since tracing a few cents of welding wire isn't worth it; on technology, since barcodes make tracing cheaper; and on how operations are designed.

And it depends on the cost object. The welding supervisor's salary is indirect to any trailer, but direct to the welding department.

Quick check. The cost object is one trailer. Which cost is direct? Pause and pick.

It's B. The panels can be traced to that trailer. The others are shared and must be allocated.

Microteach 2.2 · 1:23Variable and fixed costs

Variable costs change in total with activity; fixed costs don't, so fixed cost per unit changes.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 2.

Read the script

Costs can also be classified by how they behave when activity changes. And unit costs can mislead.

A variable cost changes in total in proportion to activity, but stays constant per unit. A fixed cost stays constant in total over a period, so its cost per unit falls as volume rises.

Prairie Gate leases a robotic welder for $12,000 a month. At forty trailers, that's $300 each. At sixty, $200. At eighty, $150. The total never changes.

The trap: treating a fixed unit cost as if it were variable. Building a forty-first trailer adds no lease cost at all. Use totals, not unit averages, for decisions.

A cost driver is what causes a cost to change: trailers built for steel, labor hours for wages, number of setups for setup costs. Fixed costs have drivers too, but only over the long run.

Quick check. Steel costs three thousand dollars per trailer, and production rises from fifty to sixty. What happens? Pause and pick.

It's C. Steel is variable: ten more trailers add $30,000. The cost per trailer stays the same.

Microteach 2.3 · 1:17Three sectors, three inventories

Manufacturers hold direct materials, work-in-process and finished goods inventories.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 2.

Read the script

A bank, a hardware store and a trailer factory account for costs differently. The difference is inventory.

Manufacturers turn materials into products. Merchandisers, like a hardware store, buy and resell goods. Service firms, like banks and law firms, provide services and hold no inventory of products.

A manufacturer has three inventories. Direct materials waiting to be used: steel, axles and tires. Work-in-process: trailers that are partly built. And finished goods: completed trailers not yet sold.

At the end of December, Prairie Gate has $84,000 of materials, $126,000 of work in process and $58,000 of finished trailers: $268,000 of inventory on its balance sheet.

Service firms still need cost accounting: a bank costs each type of account, and a hospital each procedure. They just have no product inventory.

Quick check. A trailer with a welded frame but no floor or wiring sits in which inventory? Pause and pick.

It's B, work in process.

Microteach 2.4 · 1:17Inventoriable and period costs

Manufacturing costs are inventoriable and expensed when sold; other costs are period costs, expensed when incurred.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 2.

Read the script

When does a cost hit the income statement? It depends on whether it's an inventoriable cost or a period cost.

Inventoriable costs are all the costs of making a product: materials, labor and factory overhead. They sit on the balance sheet as inventory until the product is sold, then become cost of goods sold.

Period costs are all the other costs: research, design, marketing, distribution, customer service and administration. Under GAAP they're expensed in the period incurred.

Steel and the factory supervisor's salary are inventoriable: they're expensed as cost of goods sold when the trailer is sold. Dealer commissions and office salaries are period costs, expensed right away.

So factory rent is inventoriable, while rent for the sales office is a period cost. If a trailer built in December is still unsold, its share of factory rent waits in finished goods.

Quick check. Which cost is inventoriable at Prairie Gate? Pause and pick.

It's B. The welding robot is used to manufacture trailers, so its depreciation is factory overhead.

Microteach 2.5 · 1:25Prime and conversion costs

Prime cost is direct materials plus direct labor; conversion cost is direct labor plus overhead.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 2.

Read the script

Manufacturing costs come in three pieces, and two useful combinations.

Direct materials are materials traced to the product. Direct manufacturing labor is the pay of workers traced to the product, like welders. Manufacturing overhead is every other factory cost: supervisors, utilities, depreciation, supplies.

Trailer four-eighteen used $6,200 of direct materials, $2,400 of direct labor and $2,880 of overhead: $11,480 in total.

Prime cost is direct materials plus direct labor: $8,600. Conversion cost is direct labor plus overhead, the cost of converting materials into a product: $5,280.

As factories automate, direct labor shrinks and overhead grows, so many companies now track just two categories: direct materials and conversion costs. That's how process costing works in Lecture 8.

Quick check. Direct materials five thousand, direct labor eighteen hundred, overhead twenty-two hundred. What's conversion cost? Pause and pick.

It's B. Labor plus overhead: $4,000.

Microteach 2.6 · 1:22Overtime premium and idle time

Overtime premium and idle time are usually overhead, not direct labor.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 2.

Read the script

A welder works overtime and waits for a broken machine. Which part of the paycheck is direct labor?

Tom, a welder, earns thirty dollars an hour, time-and-a-half for overtime. He works forty-four hours, four of them overtime, so he's paid $1,380. Two of his hours were idle while the welding robot was down.

Direct labor is the forty-two productive hours at the regular rate: $1,260. The overtime premium, four hours at fifteen dollars, is $60 of overhead. The idle time, two hours at thirty, is another $60 of overhead.

Why? Overtime usually results from the overall workload, not the trailer that happened to be on the line late in the week. Spreading the premium through overhead keeps job costs fair.

The exception: if a customer's rush order caused the overtime, the premium is charged directly to that job.

Quick check. A worker paid twenty dollars an hour works forty-five hours, five of them overtime at time-and-a-half. How much is overtime premium? Pause and pick.

It's A. The premium is half the rate, ten dollars, times five hours: $50.

Microteach 7.1 · 1:29Using ABC for pricing and mix

Activity-based management uses ABC information to make better pricing, mix and process decisions.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

ABC is only worth building if managers use it. Using activity information to run the business is called activity-based management.

Activity-based management, or A-B-M, is the use of activity cost information to make decisions: about prices, product mix, cost reduction, process improvement and design.

With ABC costs, utility trailers sell for $9,200 and cost $8,700, a $500 margin. Livestock trailers sell for $16,400 but cost $18,280: each one loses $1,880. The old system showed them as Prairie Gate's most profitable line.

Karen has options: raise livestock prices or charge separately for custom design; cut the cost of the activities the line uses; or, at the extreme, drop it.

Dropping a line is rarely the first answer. Some of the overhead allocated to it, like facility costs, wouldn't disappear. Relevant cost analysis for such decisions is in Cost Accounting II.

Quick check. Under ABC, a product's margin turns negative. What's the best first step? Pause and pick.

It's B. Understand the activities driving the cost, then price them or reduce them.

Microteach 7.2 · 1:21Value-added and non-value-added activities

Eliminate or reduce non-value-added activities that customers don't pay for.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Some activities create value customers will pay for. Others just cost money. ABM helps tell them apart.

A value-added activity is one customers see as worth paying for, like welding a frame or designing a custom layout. A non-value-added activity adds cost but no value, like moving parts around the plant, reworking defects or waiting for setups.

Prairie Gate's two hundred setups take six hours each. With quick-change fixtures, the team thinks they can take three. If the crew and equipment devoted to setups can be cut in half, the setup pool falls from $240,000 to $120,000.

But the savings are real only if spending actually falls, for example by redeploying setup workers, or if the freed capacity is used to build more trailers. Otherwise you have unused capacity, not lower costs.

Cost drivers point straight to the causes of cost, so managers know where to look: fewer setups, fewer defects, fewer engineering changes.

Quick check. Which activity is non-value-added? Pause and pick.

It's C. Moving steel around adds cost but no value the customer pays for.

Microteach 7.3 · 1:26Design decisions

Most costs are locked in at the design stage; ABC shows designers what complexity costs.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Most of a product's cost is decided before production starts, when it's designed. ABC tells designers what complexity costs.

Locked-in, or designed-in, costs are costs that haven't been incurred yet but will be, because of decisions already made. Choose a frame with forty unique parts, and the cost of buying, storing and welding them is locked in.

Today each livestock trailer needs twenty-four design hours. Offering three standard designs with options could cut that to eight, saving sixteen hours at a hundred twenty dollars: $1,920. If setups halve too, that's another $960 a trailer, enough to make the line profitable.

Common parts across models, like the same axles, lights and hitches, cut purchasing, storage and setup activity.

The trade-off: some customers buy from Prairie Gate precisely because of custom design. The goal is to keep the variety customers value and pay for, and drop complexity they don't.

Quick check. Design hours fall by ten per trailer, at a hundred twenty dollars an hour, on a hundred trailers. What's the potential saving? Pause and pick.

It's C, $120,000, if the design resources are actually reduced.

Microteach 7.4 · 1:29ABC in merchandising and services

ABC reveals the cost of serving different customers and services, not just products.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

A farm supply distributor doesn't make anything. But ABC can still reveal which customers make money.

Marshall Farm Supply distributes feed, fencing and parts. Its activity rates: forty dollars to process an order, ninety per delivery, and a hundred fifty extra for each rush order.

Hansen Farms and Berg Dairy each buy $60,000 a year, with the same $12,000 gross margin. Hansen orders monthly: twelve orders and deliveries, no rushes, $1,560 of activity cost. Berg orders small amounts a hundred fifty times, forty of them rushed: $25,500. Berg loses Marshall Farm Supply $13,500 a year.

The answer isn't necessarily to drop Berg. Marshall Farm Supply could set minimum order sizes, charge for deliveries and rush orders, or move Berg to cheaper online ordering.

The same method works in banks, which cost each type of account, hospitals, which cost procedures, and airlines, which cost each fare class.

Quick check. A customer places fifty orders with fifty deliveries and ten rush orders. What's the activity cost? Pause and pick.

It's B. $2,000 plus $4,500 plus $1,500 is $8,000.

Microteach 7.5 · 1:16Time-driven ABC

Time-driven ABC uses cost per minute of capacity and minutes per activity.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

Traditional ABC asks staff how they spend their time. Time-driven ABC replaces the surveys with two simple estimates.

Time-driven ABC needs two estimates: the cost per minute of the capacity a department supplies, and the number of minutes each activity takes.

Prairie Gate's customer service team costs $560,000 a year. Eight staff each work about seventeen fifty productive hours: eight hundred forty thousand minutes of practical capacity. That's about sixty-seven cents a minute.

A warranty claim takes thirty minutes: twenty dollars. An order change, fifteen minutes: ten dollars. Setting up a new dealer, four hours: a hundred sixty dollars.

If this year's claims, changes and setups use only seven hundred thousand minutes, a hundred forty thousand minutes, about $93,000 of capacity, is unused. Time-driven ABC makes that visible.

Quick check. Cost per minute is eighty cents. An activity takes forty-five minutes. What does it cost? Pause and pick.

It's B, $36.

Microteach 7.6 · 1:09Activity-based budgeting

Budget resources from the activities needed to meet planned output.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 5.

Read the script

ABC can also run in reverse: start from planned output, work out the activities needed, then budget the resources.

Activity-based budgeting starts with the production and sales forecast, estimates the activities needed to deliver it, then the resources those activities require, and finally their cost.

For 2028, Karen forecasts a hundred fifty setups, thanks to longer utility runs, each taking three hours with the new fixtures. That's four hundred fifty crew hours, so the setup crew can shrink from two people to one, a saving of about $60,000.

Because each budget line is tied to an activity and its driver, managers can see why a cost is there and what would change it.

That ends our work on job costing and ABC. After the midterm, we move to process costing at Redwood Valley Creamery.

Quick check. Three hundred inspections are planned, at twenty minutes each. How many inspector hours? Pause and pick.

It's B, a hundred hours.

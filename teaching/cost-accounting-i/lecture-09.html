Microteach 9.1 · 1:05The FIFO method

FIFO keeps beginning inventory separate and costs this period's work at this period's prices.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Weighted average blends last month with this month. FIFO keeps them apart. Here's the idea.

Under FIFO, the first units in are the first out. Beginning work in process is assumed to be completed first. This period's costs are spread only over the work done this period.

So Mixing's March output splits into three groups: two thousand beginning blocks that were finished, fourteen thousand started and completed in March, and four thousand started but not finished.

Started and completed is units completed minus beginning inventory: sixteen thousand minus two thousand, or fourteen thousand.

Why use FIFO? Because the cost per equivalent unit reflects only this month's prices and efficiency, which makes it better for evaluating performance.

Quick check. Beginning work in process three thousand; twenty thousand completed. How many started and completed? Pause and pick.

It's B, seventeen thousand.

Microteach 9.2 · 1:18FIFO equivalent units

FIFO equivalent units = work to finish beginning WIP + started and completed + ending WIP work.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

FIFO equivalent units measure only the work done this period. Three pieces.

Beginning inventory already had all its materials, so finishing it needs no materials, but it was forty percent converted, so sixty percent of the work, twelve hundred units, was done in March. Fourteen thousand blocks were started and completed. Ending inventory got four thousand units of materials and a thousand of conversion. March work: eighteen thousand materials units and sixteen thousand two hundred conversion units.

A shortcut: FIFO equivalent units equal weighted-average equivalent units minus the work already in beginning inventory. Materials: twenty thousand minus two thousand. Conversion: seventeen thousand minus eight hundred.

Notice that under FIFO, the percentage of completion of beginning inventory matters: it tells you how much work was left to do this month.

A useful check: materials go in at the start, so FIFO materials equivalent units equal the eighteen thousand blocks started this month.

Quick check. Beginning inventory is a thousand units, thirty percent converted. How many conversion equivalent units to finish them? Pause and pick.

It's B, seven hundred.

Microteach 9.3 · 1:31FIFO costs and assignment

FIFO cost per EU uses current costs only; completed units carry beginning WIP cost plus the cost to finish.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Now the costs. Under FIFO, beginning inventory's cost stays separate.

Divide only March's costs by March's work. Materials: $540,000 over eighteen thousand units, thirty dollars. Conversion: $324,000 over sixteen thousand two hundred, twenty dollars.

Blocks transferred out carry three pieces: the $72,000 already in beginning inventory, $24,000 to finish them, and fourteen thousand started-and-completed blocks at fifty dollars each, $700,000. Total: $796,000.

Ending inventory gets four thousand materials units at thirty dollars and a thousand conversion units at twenty: $140,000. Together with $796,000 transferred out, that's $936,000, all the costs to account for.

Under FIFO, finished blocks come in two batches with different unit costs: the beginning batch, and those started this month. In practice, transfers to the next department are usually recorded at their combined average.

Quick check. Current conversion costs eighty-one thousand; FIFO conversion equivalent units nine thousand. Cost per equivalent unit? Pause and pick.

It's A, nine dollars.

Microteach 9.4 · 1:21FIFO vs weighted average

The methods differ only in how beginning WIP is treated; FIFO is better for performance evaluation.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

We've costed March both ways. How different are the answers, and which should Redwood Valley use?

Weighted average transferred out $796,800 and left $139,200 in ending inventory. FIFO transferred out $796,000 and left $140,000. The totals are the same.

The difference comes from beginning inventory. Its materials cost twenty-eight dollars a block, while March's cost thirty. Weighted average blends the cheaper materials into all units; FIFO sends them out first.

Weighted average is simpler. FIFO isolates this month's costs, making it better for judging current performance. When prices and efficiency are stable, or inventories small, the two give almost the same answer.

Don't confuse this with FIFO for finished goods inventory. Process-costing FIFO is about equivalent units within a department.

Quick check. When prices are rising, FIFO's cost of units transferred out is usually what, compared with weighted average? Pause and pick.

It's B, lower: the older, cheaper beginning inventory goes out first.

Microteach 9.5 · 1:32Transferred-in costs

Costs from a prior department are treated as a separate cost category that is always 100% complete.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

When blocks arrive in Aging, their Mixing costs come with them. Those are transferred-in costs.

Transferred-in costs are costs incurred in earlier departments, carried forward with the units. In the later department they're treated like materials added at the start: always one hundred percent complete.

Aging began March with three thousand blocks, received sixteen thousand from Mixing, completed seventeen thousand, and ended with two thousand, forty percent through aging. Weighted-average equivalent units: nineteen thousand for transferred-in costs and seventeen thousand eight hundred for conversion.

Transferred-in cost per unit is the beginning balance plus the $796,800 received, divided by nineteen thousand: fifty dollars. Conversion is $7.50. Completed blocks cost $57.50 each, $977,500 in total, moved to finished goods. Ending inventory is $106,000.

The most common mistake in multi-department problems is forgetting transferred-in costs. They're usually the biggest cost category in later departments.

Quick check. In a second department, ending inventory is thirty percent converted. How complete is it for transferred-in costs? Pause and pick.

It's D, a hundred percent. Transferred-in costs arrive with the units.

Microteach 9.6 · 1:20Hybrid systems: operation costing

Operation costing traces materials to batches and applies conversion costs by operation.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 18.

Read the script

Some products are partly identical and partly custom. They need a hybrid of job and process costing.

Operation costing is a hybrid. Each batch is a job for direct materials, because batches use different materials. But every batch goes through the same operations, so conversion costs are applied at an average rate per operation.

Redwood Valley also packs cheese for retail. Holiday gift boxes use $9 of materials each, and go through cutting, wrapping and packing. Bulk packs use $3 and skip wrapping.

With conversion rates of $1.20 for cutting, two dollars for wrapping and eighty cents for packing, the holiday batch costs $6,500: thirteen dollars a box.

Hybrid systems are common in clothing, shoes, food and electronics: the same steps, different materials. Real systems are rarely pure job or pure process costing.

Quick check. A bulk batch of two thousand boxes: materials three dollars, cutting a dollar twenty, packing eighty cents each. Batch cost? Pause and pick.

It's B. Five dollars a box: $10,000.

Microteach 14.1 · 1:27Planning overhead and standard rates

Budgeted overhead rates turn planned overhead into a standard cost per unit.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Last time we analyzed steel and labor. Today: overhead, the cost that's hardest to control, at Prairie Gate's standard utility trailer.

Variable and fixed overhead are planned differently. Variable overhead, like welding gas and power, changes with labor hours and is controlled day to day. Fixed overhead, like the plant lease and supervision, is decided when capacity is planned.

Each utility trailer takes twenty-four standard labor hours. Variable overhead is ten dollars a labor hour, so $240 a trailer. Fixed overhead is budgeted at $40,000 for March, based on forty trailers, so the fixed rate is a thousand dollars a trailer.

The output used to compute the fixed rate is called the denominator level. Here it's forty trailers, or nine hundred sixty labor hours. It's a choice, and it matters.

In March, Prairie Gate built thirty-six trailers using nine hundred labor hours. Actual variable overhead was $9,300 and actual fixed overhead $41,000.

Quick check. Fixed overhead budget sixty thousand dollars, denominator level fifty trailers. Fixed rate per trailer? Pause and pick.

It's B, $1,200.

Microteach 14.2 · 1:25Variable overhead variances

VMOH spending = (actual rate − budgeted rate) × actual hours; efficiency = (actual − standard hours) × rate.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Variable overhead splits into two variances, just like labor.

The flexible budget allows eight hundred sixty-four hours for thirty-six trailers, so variable overhead should have been $8,640. Actual was $9,300: a flexible-budget variance of $660 unfavorable.

The efficiency variance asks how many hours were used. Prairie Gate used thirty-six hours more than standard, at ten dollars each: $360 unfavorable. It's caused by whatever caused the labor efficiency variance.

The spending variance compares actual variable overhead with what nine hundred hours should have cost: $9,300 versus $9,000, so $300 unfavorable. Maybe gas prices rose, or more power was used per hour.

Watch out: the variable overhead efficiency variance doesn't measure overhead use directly. It measures the efficiency of the hours used as the cost driver.

Quick check. Actual hours five hundred; standard hours allowed five twenty; variable overhead rate eight dollars. Efficiency variance? Pause and pick.

It's A, $160 favorable.

Microteach 14.3 · 1:30Fixed overhead variances

Fixed MOH spending = actual − budget; production-volume = budget − allocated.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Fixed overhead doesn't change with output, so its variances work differently.

The fixed overhead spending variance, also called the flexible-budget variance, is actual fixed overhead minus the budget: $41,000 minus $40,000, or $1,000 unfavorable. Perhaps the supervisor's salary rose. There's no efficiency variance for fixed overhead.

Fixed overhead allocated to the trailers is thirty-six times a thousand dollars, $36,000. The budget was $40,000. The $4,000 gap is the production-volume variance: unfavorable, because output fell short of the forty-trailer denominator.

The production-volume variance doesn't mean anyone spent too much. It exists only because a fixed cost is allocated per unit, as if it were variable. It measures unused capacity, valued at the allocation rate.

Managers should ask why output was low: weaker demand, a lost dealer, a supply delay, or capacity that was too large to begin with.

Quick check. Fixed overhead budget sixty thousand, twelve hundred dollars a trailer; fifty-five trailers built. Production-volume variance? Pause and pick.

It's B. Allocated $66,000 exceeds the budget: $6,000 favorable.

Microteach 14.4 · 1:30The four variances and journal entries

Underallocated overhead equals the sum of the four overhead variances.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Put the four overhead variances together, and they explain all of March's underallocated overhead.

Variable spending, $300 unfavorable. Variable efficiency, $360 unfavorable. Fixed spending, $1,000 unfavorable. Production volume, $4,000 unfavorable. Total: $5,660.

Check it: actual overhead was $50,300 and allocated overhead $44,640. Underallocated: $5,660. The variances explain all of it.

To close variable overhead, debit the allocated account for $8,640 and the two unfavorable variances, and credit the control account for the actual $9,300.

Fixed overhead closes the same way: debit allocated for $36,000 and the two variances, and credit control for $41,000. At year-end, variances are written off to cost of goods sold or prorated, as in Lecture 5.

Quick check. Actual overhead eighty thousand; allocated seventy-six thousand. The four variances must total what? Pause and pick.

It's B, $4,000 unfavorable: underallocated.

Microteach 14.5 · 1:21Interpreting overhead variances

Read the variances together, and remember the sales-volume variance measures lost profit, not the PVV.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Numbers alone don't manage a plant. How should Dale Kirchner read March?

The extra thirty-six hours drove both the labor and variable overhead efficiency variances, so they share one cause. Variable spending needs a look at gas and power prices. Fixed spending came from a supervisor's raise. And the big one, volume, came from building four fewer trailers.

Don't confuse the production-volume variance with the sales-volume variance from Lecture 13. The sales-volume variance, $6,160, is the contribution margin lost on four fewer trailers: real lost profit. The production-volume variance is an accounting effect of allocating fixed cost.

The same analysis works beyond manufacturing overhead and with activity-based cost pools: setups, design hours or shipping, using each activity's own cost driver.

And favorable isn't always good. A manager could make the production-volume variance favorable just by building trailers for inventory nobody ordered.

Quick check. Which variance measures real profit lost because fewer units were sold? Pause and pick.

It's B, the sales-volume variance.

Microteach 14.6 · 1:21Course recap: Cost Accounting I

Accumulate costs, assign them to products, and compare against standards: the foundations of cost accounting.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 8.

Read the script

Fourteen lectures in two minutes: how Prairie Gate and Redwood Valley know what their products cost.

First, foundations: cost terms and behavior, and how costs flow from materials to work in process to finished goods. Then job costing at Prairie Gate, with a budgeted overhead rate and year-end adjustments, and activity-based costing to sharpen product costs.

Next, process costing at Redwood Valley Creamery, with equivalent units under weighted average and FIFO, then spoilage, rework and scrap. Then allocation: support departments, and joint costs split between cream and skim, with whey as a byproduct.

Finally, standards and variances: static and flexible budgets, materials and labor variances, and today's overhead variances.

In ACCT 341, Cost Accounting II, you'll use these costs to plan, decide and evaluate performance: CVP, budgets, relevant costs, pricing, capital budgeting and transfer pricing.

Last quick check. Which costing system suits cheddar made in continuous batches? Pause and pick.

It's B, process costing. Good luck on the final, and thanks for building with Prairie Gate.

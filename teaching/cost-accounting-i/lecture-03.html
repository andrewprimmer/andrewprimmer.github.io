Microteach 3.1 · 1:31How costs flow through a factory

Manufacturing costs flow from materials to work in process, finished goods and cost of goods sold.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 2 and 4.

Read the script

Manufacturing costs move through the accounts the same way trailers move through the factory. Follow the flow.

Materials are bought into materials inventory. When they're used, their cost moves into work in process, joined by direct labor and manufacturing overhead. When trailers are finished, their cost moves to finished goods. When they're sold, it becomes cost of goods sold.

Every inventory account follows the same equation: beginning balance, plus what comes in, minus what goes out, equals the ending balance. Know three numbers and you can find the fourth.

Prairie Gate's work in process started October with $110,000. It received $196,000 of materials, $88,000 of direct labor and $132,000 of overhead. $400,000 of trailers were finished and moved out, leaving $126,000.

The key idea: inventoriable costs follow the product. They only reach the income statement when the trailer is sold.

Quick check. Finished goods began at sixty-four thousand, four hundred thousand was completed, and the ending balance is fifty-eight thousand. What's cost of goods sold? Pause and pick.

It's B. 64,000 plus 400,000 minus 58,000 is $406,000.

Microteach 3.2 · 1:15Direct materials used

Direct materials used equals beginning materials plus purchases minus ending materials.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 2 and 4.

Read the script

Purchases aren't the same as materials used. The difference sits in the storeroom.

Prairie Gate began October with $70,000 of steel, axles and tires. It bought $210,000 more, so $280,000 was available. $84,000 was still in the storeroom at month-end, so $196,000 was used in production.

If Prairie Gate stocks up on steel before a price increase, purchases jump, but materials used, and product cost, don't.

Some materials are too small to trace, like welding wire and grease. These indirect materials go to manufacturing overhead, not direct materials.

In practice, a materials requisition records each issue from the storeroom to a specific trailer, which is how job costing will trace materials in Lecture 4.

Quick check. Beginning materials thirty thousand, purchases ninety-five thousand, ending twenty-five thousand. What was used? Pause and pick.

It's B. 30,000 plus 95,000 minus 25,000 is $100,000.

Microteach 3.3 · 1:26Cost of goods manufactured

COGM adds this period's manufacturing costs to beginning WIP and subtracts ending WIP.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 2 and 4.

Read the script

The schedule of cost of goods manufactured shows the cost of everything finished this period. Let's build it.

In October, Prairie Gate used $196,000 of direct materials and incurred $88,000 of direct labor and $132,000 of overhead: $416,000 of manufacturing costs.

Add the $110,000 of trailers that were partly built at the start, for $526,000 to account for. Subtract the $126,000 still in process at month-end. Cost of goods manufactured, the cost of trailers finished in October, is $400,000.

Notice the difference. Prairie Gate spent $416,000 this month but finished $400,000 of trailers, because work in process grew.

The schedule is just the work-in-process T-account, written out as a report.

Quick check. Costs incurred three hundred thousand, beginning work in process forty thousand, ending fifty-five thousand. What's cost of goods manufactured? Pause and pick.

It's B. 40,000 plus 300,000 minus 55,000 is $285,000.

Microteach 3.4 · 1:25The manufacturer's income statement

COGS equals beginning finished goods plus COGM minus ending finished goods.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 2 and 4.

Read the script

Cost of goods manufactured feeds the income statement through finished goods. Here's the last step.

Prairie Gate began with $64,000 of finished trailers on the lot. Add the $400,000 manufactured, for $464,000 available for sale. $58,000 of trailers remain unsold, so cost of goods sold is $406,000.

Revenues were $560,000. Less cost of goods sold, gross margin is $154,000. Less $96,000 of period costs for marketing, distribution and administration, operating income is $58,000.

A merchandiser's statement looks the same, except purchases take the place of cost of goods manufactured, and it has one inventory instead of three.

Period costs skip the inventory accounts entirely and go straight to the income statement.

Quick check. Revenue eight hundred thousand, cost of goods sold five twenty, period costs one ninety. Operating income? Pause and pick.

It's B. Gross margin $280,000, less $190,000: $90,000.

Microteach 3.5 · 1:16Journal entries for cost flows

Each step of the flow is a journal entry moving cost from one account to the next.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 2 and 4.

Read the script

Every arrow in the cost flow is a journal entry. Here are the main ones for October.

Issuing direct materials debits work in process and credits materials: $196,000. Direct labor debits work in process and credits wages payable: $88,000.

Actual overhead costs, like utilities and depreciation, are first collected in manufacturing overhead control. Then they're moved into work in process. In Lecture 5 you'll see that companies usually apply overhead using a predetermined rate instead.

Completed trailers move from work in process to finished goods: $400,000. Trailers sold move from finished goods to cost of goods sold: $406,000. A separate entry records the revenue.

The word control means a general ledger account backed by a subsidiary ledger: individual job cost records behind work in process, for example.

Quick check. Which entry records trailers completed? Pause and pick.

It's B. Cost moves out of work in process into finished goods.

Microteach 3.6 · 1:21Three meanings of product cost

Product cost differs for pricing, government contracting and external reporting.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapters 2 and 4.

Read the script

What did a trailer cost? The answer depends on why you're asking. Here are three definitions of product cost.

For pricing and product-mix decisions, product cost includes the whole value chain, from design to customer service. For a government cost-plus contract, it includes only the costs the contract allows. For the financial statements, it includes only manufacturing costs.

Trailer four-eighteen's GAAP inventory cost is $11,480. Add design, marketing, delivery and expected warranty costs, and its full value-chain cost is $13,580. Pricing it at $13,000 would look profitable on the GAAP number, but it isn't.

Suppose Lyon County buys six utility trailers on a cost-plus-eight-percent contract. The contract decides which costs count, so the accounting must follow it, not GAAP.

It's the theme of the course again: different costs for different purposes. Always ask what the cost is for.

Quick check. Which product cost should Karen use for a long-run price? Pause and pick.

It's B. In the long run, prices must cover every cost in the value chain.

Microteach 10.1 · 1:10Spoilage, rework and scrap

Spoiled units are discarded or sold cheaply, reworked units are fixed, scrap is leftover material.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 19.

Read the script

No process is perfect. Some units fail inspection, some get fixed, and some material is left over. Each is accounted for differently.

Spoilage is units that don't meet standards and are discarded or sold at a reduced price, like cheese blocks with bad texture. Rework is units that are repaired and sold as good, like a trailer with a weld redone. Scrap is leftover material with little value, like steel offcuts.

Waste is expensive, and you can't reduce what you don't measure. Accounting for it properly shows managers where quality problems cost the most.

The key question for each kind of waste: is it a normal part of an efficient process, or is it abnormal and avoidable? The answer decides where the cost goes.

We'll look at waste in both settings: process costing at Redwood Valley Creamery, and job costing at Prairie Gate Trailers.

Quick check. A cracked weld is ground out and redone, and the trailer is sold as good. What's that? Pause and pick.

It's B, rework.

Microteach 10.2 · 1:16Normal and abnormal spoilage

Normal spoilage is a cost of good units; abnormal spoilage is a loss for the period.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 19.

Read the script

Some spoilage is the price of making a product at all. Some is a mistake. The accounting treats them very differently.

Normal spoilage is inherent in a process operating efficiently: cheese making always loses a few blocks. Its cost is added to the cost of the good units. Abnormal spoilage is avoidable, caused by errors or breakdowns, and is written off as a loss in the period, so managers see it.

Management sets a normal spoilage rate, usually as a percentage of the good units that pass inspection. Redwood Valley's is two percent.

Spoilage is identified at an inspection point. Spoiled units carry all the costs added up to that point, and only units that have passed the inspection point bear the normal spoilage cost.

Many companies now set the normal rate at zero, treating all spoilage as abnormal so it's always visible and always targeted.

Quick check. Ninety-two hundred good units pass inspection, and normal spoilage is two percent of good units. How many normal spoiled units? Pause and pick.

It's A, a hundred eighty-four.

Microteach 10.3 · 1:23Spoilage in process costing

Count spoiled units in equivalent units; add normal spoilage cost to good units and write off abnormal.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 19.

Read the script

Let's account for spoilage in Redwood Valley's Mixing department, using weighted average.

Mixing started ten thousand blocks in May. Ninety-two hundred passed inspection, four hundred failed and four hundred are still in process, half converted. Normal spoilage is two percent of good units, a hundred eighty-four. The other two hundred sixteen are abnormal.

Spoiled units were inspected at the end, so they're complete. Equivalent units: ten thousand for materials and ninety-eight hundred for conversion. Costs per equivalent unit: thirty and twenty dollars, fifty dollars a block.

Good blocks cost $460,000, plus $9,200 of normal spoilage: $469,200, or fifty-one dollars a block, transferred to Aging. Abnormal spoilage, $10,800, goes to a loss account. Ending inventory, $16,000. Total: $496,000.

Ending inventory gets no spoilage cost, because it hasn't reached the inspection point yet.

Quick check. Each spoiled unit cost fifty dollars, and three hundred are abnormal. Loss from abnormal spoilage? Pause and pick.

It's B, $15,000.

Microteach 10.4 · 1:17Spoilage in job costing

Normal spoilage for a specific job stays with the job; normal spoilage common to all jobs goes into overhead.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 19.

Read the script

In job costing, spoilage has three possible homes. It depends on who caused it.

Normal spoilage caused by a specific job's requirements, like a customer's very tight tolerances, stays with that job. Normal spoilage common to all jobs is built into the overhead rate. Abnormal spoilage goes to a loss account.

On job four-thirty-one, two aluminum side panels are spoiled. They cost $900, and can be sold as scrap for $100. Net spoilage cost: $800.

If the customer's unusual design caused it, only the scrap value is removed from the job. The $800 net cost stays in job four-thirty-one, and the customer should be priced for it.

If panels are routinely spoiled across all jobs, the $800 goes to manufacturing overhead control, spread through the rate. If it was an avoidable mistake, it goes to a loss from abnormal spoilage.

Quick check. Spoilage is normal and common to all jobs. Where does its net cost go? Pause and pick.

It's B, manufacturing overhead, spread through the rate.

Microteach 10.5 · 1:10Rework

Rework follows the same three cases: the job, overhead, or a loss.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 19.

Read the script

Reworked units are repaired and sold as good. The cost of fixing them follows the same three cases.

On job four-thirty-three a weld cracks in testing and is redone: $120 of materials, eight hours of labor and the overhead those hours carry. Rework cost: $648.

If the customer's requirements caused it, charge job four-thirty-three. If it's normal across all jobs, charge overhead. If it was avoidable, say a welder skipped preheating, charge a loss from abnormal rework.

Rework is often hidden: if welders just fix problems and log the hours to the job, nobody sees the cost. Separate rework codes on time records make it visible.

Once rework is tracked by cause, managers can attack the biggest causes, such as a batch of poor steel, a training gap or a badly maintained welder.

Quick check. Rework caused by a welder's avoidable error. What's debited? Pause and pick.

It's C, loss from abnormal rework.

Microteach 10.6 · 1:13Accounting for scrap

Scrap revenue reduces the cost of the job or overhead, recognized at sale or at production.

Reading: Horngren's Cost Accounting, Chapter 19.

Read the script

Scrap has some value, so it needs accounting too. The questions are when to recognize it, and what it reduces.

Scrap can be recognized when it's sold, which is simplest when amounts are small, or when it's produced, if it's valuable and should be controlled as inventory. And its value can reduce a specific job's cost, or overhead if it's common to all jobs.

Prairie Gate sells a month's steel offcuts for $1,200. They come from all jobs, so the cash reduces manufacturing overhead control.

If scrap is valuable, like aluminum offcuts, it's recorded as inventory when produced, at its expected sale value, and controlled like any other material.

Scrap is also a signal. Rising offcuts may mean poor cutting layouts or worn blades. Better nesting software at Prairie Gate could cut steel use as well as scrap.

Quick check. Scrap traceable to a single job is sold for three hundred dollars. What does it reduce? Pause and pick.

It's B, that job's cost in work in process.

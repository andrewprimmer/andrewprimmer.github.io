Revenue Recognition in Six Videos

Six two-minute micro-lessons on ASC 606, made for going back over the lecture at home. Each one fits a phone screen and ends with a worked example.

Watch them in order the first time. After that, jump straight to the step you are stuck on. When a video says pause, stop it and try the question before you see the answer.

Under each video you can open the full script if you would rather read along, or are watching somewhere you cannot play sound.

Video one · 2:08The five-step model

The ASC 606 framework end to end, with a worked laptop-and-support allocation.

Read the script

Revenue recognition in two minutes. Every revenue question on your exam hangs on ASC 606 and its five steps.

The core principle: revenue is recognised when control passes to the customer, at the amount the company expects to be entitled to. Not when the cash arrives.

Step one: identify the contract.

Step two: identify the performance obligations, the separate promises.

Step three: determine the transaction price.

Step four: allocate it to the obligations.

Step five: recognise revenue as each one is satisfied.

Let's try one. TechCo sells a laptop with two years of support for three thousand dollars. Sold separately, the laptop is twenty-seven hundred and the support nine hundred.

Pause here and work out how much revenue TechCo records on day one.

Here's the working. Two obligations, so first add up the standalone prices: twenty-seven hundred plus nine hundred is thirty-six hundred.

Laptop. Divide its standalone price by the total: twenty-seven hundred divided by thirty-six hundred is seventy-five percent. Times that by the three thousand contract price: twenty-two fifty.

Support. Nine hundred divided by thirty-six hundred is twenty-five percent. Times three thousand: seven fifty. And check: twenty-two fifty plus seven fifty is three thousand.

On delivery: debit cash three thousand, credit sales revenue twenty-two fifty, and credit unearned service revenue seven fifty.

The support is earned over twenty-four months. Seven fifty divided by twenty-four is thirty-one twenty-five a month.

Remember: control, not cash. Next video: steps one and two.

Video two · 1:48Contracts and performance obligations

Steps 1 and 2: when a contract exists, and when promises are distinct.

Read the script

Steps one and two. Is there a contract, and what exactly has the company promised?

Under ASC 606 a contract exists only if five criteria are all met. First, both parties have approved it and are committed to it. Second, each party's rights are identifiable.

Third, the payment terms are identifiable. Fourth, the contract has commercial substance.

And fifth, it's probable the company will collect what it's entitled to.

If the criteria aren't met, there's no contract yet. Any cash received goes on the balance sheet as a liability, not revenue.

Step two. A performance obligation is a promise to transfer a distinct good or service.

Distinct means passing both of two tests. First, the customer could benefit from it on its own, or with resources that are readily available.

Second, it's separately identifiable in the contract. It isn't heavily integrated with, or modifying, the other promises.

Compare two cases. A gym buys a treadmill plus standard installation that any technician could do. That's two performance obligations.

Now a seller builds a custom production line, integrating and modifying every component. Those promises combine into one performance obligation.

Exam tips. Both tests must be met. A series of identical services, like monthly cleaning, can be one obligation. Next video: step three, the transaction price.

Video three · 1:50The transaction price

Step 3: variable consideration and the constraint, financing, noncash consideration and payments to customers.

Read the script

Step three: how much revenue? That's the transaction price.

The transaction price is the amount the company expects to be entitled to in exchange for its goods or services.

It excludes amounts collected for third parties. Sales tax, for example, is a liability, not revenue.

Watch for four complications. Variable consideration, and a significant financing component.

Then noncash consideration, and consideration payable to the customer.

Bonuses, rebates, penalties and refunds all make the price variable. Estimate it using the expected value when there are many possible outcomes, or the most likely amount when there are only two.

Try this. A builder earns a fifty thousand dollar bonus for finishing by June thirtieth, and there's an eighty percent chance it will.

Pause the video and decide how much of the bonus to include.

It's all or nothing, so use the most likely amount: the full fifty thousand.

But always apply the constraint. Include variable consideration only if it's probable there won't be a significant reversal of revenue later.

Quickly, the other three. If payment and delivery are more than a year apart, adjust for the time value of money.

Measure noncash consideration at fair value. And payments to the customer, like slotting fees, reduce revenue. Next: allocating the price.

Video four · 1:40Allocating the price

Step 4: relative standalone selling prices, and how to estimate them.

Read the script

Step four. One price, several promises. How do we split it?

We allocate the transaction price based on relative standalone selling prices.

The standalone selling price is what the company would charge for that item on its own.

If there's no observable price, estimate one. Use an adjusted market assessment, or the expected cost plus a margin.

The residual approach, total price less the other standalone prices, is only allowed when the price is highly variable or uncertain.

Try it. A ten thousand dollar contract covers equipment, installation and training. Their standalone prices are nine thousand, fifteen hundred and fifteen hundred: twelve thousand in total.

Pause and allocate the ten thousand.

Each item gets its share of the total standalone price. Equipment is nine twelfths of ten thousand: seventy-five hundred. Installation and training get twelve fifty each.

Notice that the two thousand dollar discount is spread proportionally across all three, unless there's clear evidence it belongs to just one.

One more. Training costs eight hundred dollars to provide, and the normal margin is twenty-five percent on cost. So the estimated standalone price is a thousand dollars. Next: step five, when to recognise.

Video five · 1:46Point in time or over time

Step 5: the over-time criteria, control indicators and a cost-to-cost long-term contract.

Read the script

Step five. When does revenue hit the income statement? At a point in time, or over time?

Revenue is recognised over time if any one of three criteria is met. One: the customer consumes the benefit as you perform, like a cleaning service.

Two: you're building an asset the customer already controls, like a building on its land.

Three: the asset has no alternative use to you, and you have an enforceable right to payment for work done so far.

If none apply, recognise revenue at the point control transfers. Look for the indicators: a right to payment, legal title, physical possession, the risks and rewards of ownership, and customer acceptance.

Over-time contracts need a measure of progress. Here's a five million dollar contract with estimated total costs of four million. Costs to date are one million in year one, and two point four million by the end of year two.

Pause and calculate each year's revenue.

Year one is twenty-five percent complete, so revenue is one point two five million. By year two it's sixty percent: three million cumulative, less what's already recognised, gives one point seven five million.

Watch out. If the contract is expected to make a loss, recognise the whole loss immediately. Next: the special issues.

Video six · 1:58Special issues

Principal or agent, warranties, right of return and consignment.

Read the script

Four special issues that turn up again and again in exams: principal versus agent, warranties, returns, and consignment.

Principal or agent? Ask whether the company controls the good or service before it's transferred. A principal reports revenue gross. An agent reports only its commission.

A travel website sells a five hundred dollar flight and keeps fifty. The airline provides the flight, so the website is an agent. Its revenue is fifty dollars.

Warranties. An assurance-type warranty just promises the product works as specified. It's not a performance obligation: accrue a warranty expense and liability.

A service-type warranty, like extended cover, is a separate obligation. Defer that revenue and recognise it over the coverage period.

Right of return. A company sells a thousand units at a hundred dollars each. Each cost sixty, and it expects five percent to come back.

Pause. How much revenue?

Revenue is only ninety-five thousand. The five thousand expected to be refunded is a refund liability.

And the three thousand of inventory you expect back is an asset, so cost of goods sold is fifty-seven thousand.

Finally, consignment. The consignor ships goods to a dealer but keeps control until they're sold. So revenue comes when the dealer sells to the end customer, not when the goods ship.

That's the revenue block. Five steps, and control, not cash. Rewatch any part you need, then try the homework.

Microteach 11.1 · 1:30Amount realized and gain

Gain realized is amount realized minus adjusted basis; amount realized includes debt the buyer takes over.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 11. Free reading: IRS Pub. 544.

Read the script

Every sale of property starts with the same calculation. What you received, minus what you have invested, is your gain or loss.

The amount realized is the cash plus the fair value of any property you receive, plus any of your debt the buyer takes over, less selling costs like commissions.

Adjusted basis is your original cost, plus capital improvements, minus depreciation you've taken or could have taken.

Nora sells her cameras in 2027 for $6,000. They cost $10,000, and she's taken $6,160 of depreciation: $2,000, $3,200 and a half-year $960. Adjusted basis is $3,840, so her gain is $2,160.

A realized gain is usually recognized, meaning taxed, now. But some provisions, like like-kind exchanges and involuntary conversions, let you defer it.

Quick check. Erik sells land with a forty thousand dollar mortgage. The buyer pays sixty thousand cash and takes over the mortgage, and the commission is five thousand. What's the amount realized? Pause and pick.

It's B. $60,000 plus $40,000 of debt relief, less $5,000 of commission: $95,000.

Microteach 11.2 · 1:27The character of gains and losses

Assets are ordinary, capital or Section 1231, and the character decides the rate.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 11. Free reading: IRS Pub. 544.

Read the script

A gain can be ordinary, capital, or something in between called Section 1231. The type of asset decides.

Ordinary assets, like inventory and receivables, produce ordinary income. Capital assets, like investments and personal-use property, produce capital gains. Section 1231 assets, business property held more than a year, get the best of both worlds.

Section 1231 is generous. If your 1231 transactions net to a gain, it's taxed as a long-term capital gain. If they net to a loss, it's an ordinary loss, fully deductible.

Personal-use property is a capital asset, but losses on it aren't deductible. If you sell your family car for less than you paid, there's no tax benefit. Gains, though, are taxable.

Nora's business camera, held three years, is Section 1231 property. Jake's personal pickup is a capital asset. A photo print Nora holds for sale to customers is inventory, an ordinary asset.

Quick check. Nora sells business equipment she's held for eight months, at a loss. What's the character? Pause and pick.

It's B. Business property held a year or less isn't 1231 property; it's an ordinary asset, so the loss is ordinary.

Microteach 11.3 · 1:34Section 1245 recapture

Gain on business personal property is ordinary income up to the depreciation taken.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 11. Free reading: Form 4797 instructions.

Read the script

Depreciation saves tax at ordinary rates. When you sell, the Code takes some of that back. It's called recapture.

Section 1245 applies to depreciable personal property, like equipment, vehicles and furniture. Any gain is ordinary income up to the amount of depreciation you've taken. Only gain above that can be Section 1231 gain.

Nora's gain on the cameras is $2,160. She took $6,160 of depreciation, so all of the gain, the lesser of the two, is ordinary income.

If she sold them for $12,000, above original cost, her gain would be $8,160. The first $6,160 is ordinary recapture, and the $2,000 above original cost is Section 1231 gain.

Recapture only applies to gains. A loss on business property held over a year is a Section 1231 loss.

Quick check. Equipment cost fifty thousand dollars, thirty thousand of depreciation was taken, and it sells for thirty-five thousand. How much is ordinary income? Pause and pick.

It's A. Adjusted basis is $20,000, so the gain is $15,000. That's less than the $30,000 of depreciation, so it's all ordinary.

Microteach 11.4 · 1:35Real estate and Section 1231 netting

Depreciation on buildings is taxed at up to 25% on sale; net 1231 gains are long-term, subject to a five-year lookback.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 11. Free reading: Form 4797 instructions.

Read the script

Buildings follow different recapture rules. And then all Section 1231 gains and losses are netted together.

For individuals, buildings depreciated straight-line have no ordinary recapture. But the gain up to the depreciation taken, called unrecaptured section 1250 gain, is taxed at a maximum of twenty-five percent, not fifteen.

Erik sells an older rental building at an $80,000 gain. He's taken $60,000 of depreciation. That $60,000 is unrecaptured section 1250 gain, taxed at up to twenty-five percent. The other $20,000 is taxed at fifteen percent.

After recapture, add up all the year's Section 1231 gains and losses. A net gain is treated as long-term capital gain. A net loss is an ordinary loss.

There's a catch to stop people timing gains and losses. A net Section 1231 gain is ordinary income to the extent of net 1231 losses deducted in the previous five years.

Quick check. This year's net 1231 gain is ten thousand dollars. Three years ago there was a four thousand dollar net 1231 loss. How is the gain taxed? Pause and pick.

It's B. The lookback makes $4,000 ordinary, and the remaining $6,000 is long-term capital gain.

Microteach 11.5 · 1:35Like-kind exchanges

Exchanging real property for like-kind real property defers gain, except to the extent of boot.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 11. Free reading: Form 8824 instructions.

Read the script

Swap one investment property for another, and you can defer the gain entirely. That's Section 1031.

Since 2018, like-kind exchanges are only for real property. Both the property given up and the property received must be held for business or investment, not personal use. Using an intermediary, you have forty-five days to identify the replacement and a hundred and eighty days to close.

If you receive cash or other non-like-kind property, called boot, you recognize gain equal to the lesser of the boot or the realized gain.

Erik exchanges land with a basis of $100,000 for land worth $150,000 plus $20,000 cash. His realized gain is $70,000. He recognizes $20,000, the boot. The new land's basis is old basis plus gain recognized minus boot: $100,000, which preserves the $50,000 deferred gain.

A like-kind exchange defers gain; it doesn't forgive it. The unrecognized gain waits in the lower basis of the new property, unless the owner dies and the basis steps up.

Quick check. Which exchange can qualify under section ten thirty-one in 2026? Pause and pick.

It's B. Any investment or business real property is like-kind to any other. Trucks are personal property, a home is personal use, and shares don't qualify.

Microteach 11.6 · 1:42Involuntary conversions and installment sales

Reinvesting insurance or condemnation proceeds defers gain; installment sales spread gain over the years payments arrive.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 11. Free reading: IRS Pub. 537; Pub. 547.

Read the script

Two more ways to defer gain: when property is destroyed or taken, and when the buyer pays over time.

When property is destroyed, stolen or condemned, and insurance or compensation exceeds its basis, there's a gain. If you reinvest in similar property within two years, three for condemned real estate, you can defer it, except for proceeds you don't reinvest.

A hailstorm destroys Erik's rental garage. Insurance pays $40,000 and his basis was $25,000, so he realizes a $15,000 gain. He rebuilds for $36,000. He recognizes only the $4,000 he didn't reinvest.

In an installment sale, at least one payment comes after the year of sale. Gain is recognized as cash arrives: each payment of principal times the gross profit percentage.

Ruth sells land with a $40,000 basis for $100,000, paid $25,000 a year for four years. Her gross profit percentage is sixty percent, so she recognizes $15,000 of gain each year. Depreciation recapture, though, is always taxed in the year of sale.

Quick check. Insurance pays fifty thousand dollars for a destroyed machine with a thirty thousand dollar basis. Nora buys a replacement for fifty-five thousand. What gain is recognized? Pause and pick.

It's C. She reinvested all the proceeds, so the whole $20,000 gain is deferred.

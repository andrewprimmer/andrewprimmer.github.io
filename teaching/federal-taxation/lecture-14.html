Microteach 14.1 · 1:31Excluding gain on selling your home

Up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 joint) on a main home is excluded if owned and used two of the last five years.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 14. Free reading: IRS Pub. 523.

Read the script

For most families, their home is their biggest investment. And most of the gain on selling it is never taxed.

Section 121 excludes up to $250,000 of gain on the sale of your main home, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly.

You must have owned the home and lived in it as your main home for at least two of the five years before the sale, and not used the exclusion on another sale in the past two years. For the five hundred thousand dollar amount, either spouse can meet the ownership test, but both must meet the use test.

Suppose the Lindqvists sell their Marshall home for $420,000, paying $25,000 in commissions. They bought it in 2014 for $180,000 and added a $40,000 bedroom. Their $175,000 gain is entirely excluded.

The flip side: a loss on selling your home isn't deductible, because it's personal-use property. And the limits haven't been raised since 1997.

Quick check. A single owner sells her home of ten years at a three hundred ten thousand dollar gain. How much is taxable? Pause and pick.

It's B. She excludes $250,000, so $60,000 is taxable as a long-term capital gain.

Microteach 14.2 · 1:29Partial exclusions and nonqualified use

Moving early for work, health or unforeseen reasons earns a prorated exclusion; depreciation is never excluded.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 14. Free reading: IRS Pub. 523.

Read the script

What if you have to sell before two years are up? Or the home was a rental before you lived in it? The exclusion can shrink.

If you sell before meeting the two-year tests because of a job change, health, or unforeseen circumstances like divorce or losing your job, you get a reduced exclusion.

Erik and Kari sell after twelve months because Kari's job moves to Chicago. Their maximum exclusion is twelve twenty-fourths of $500,000: $250,000. Their $40,000 gain is fully excluded. Note that the fraction reduces the cap, not the gain.

If the home was a rental or vacation home before it became your main home, the gain is split. The share of ownership time that was nonqualified use, after 2008, isn't eligible for the exclusion.

And any gain up to depreciation you took, for a home office using the actual method or for rental periods, is taxable as unrecaptured section twelve fifty gain.

Quick check. A single owner sells after eighteen months because of a job transfer. What's her maximum exclusion? Pause and pick.

It's B. Eighteen of twenty-four months is seventy-five percent of $250,000: $187,500.

Microteach 14.3 · 1:26Mortgage interest and points

Interest on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt is deductible; points on a purchase are deductible when paid, on a refinance over the loan's life.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 14. Free reading: IRS Pub. 936.

Read the script

Mortgage interest is the classic homeowner deduction. Since 2018, it's narrower than it used to be.

Interest is deductible on acquisition debt: borrowing to buy, build or substantially improve your main home and one second home, up to $750,000 of total debt. Loans from before December sixteenth, 2017 keep the old one-million-dollar limit.

Home equity borrowing counts only if it's used to buy, build or improve the home. Interest on a home equity loan spent on a car or a vacation isn't deductible, though if it's traced to business use, it can be a business expense.

Points are prepaid interest. Points paid to buy your main home are deductible in the year paid. Points on a refinance are deducted evenly over the life of the loan.

If the Lindqvists refinance and pay $3,000 of points on a thirty-year loan, they deduct a hundred dollars a year. If they refinance again after five years, they deduct the remaining $2,500 then.

Quick check. Which interest is deductible as qualified residence interest? Pause and pick.

It's B. Acquisition debt under $750,000 qualifies. The others are personal uses.

Microteach 14.4 · 1:34Property taxes at closing

Real estate taxes are split between buyer and seller by days owned, whoever actually pays them.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 14. Free reading: IRS Pub. 530.

Read the script

When a home changes hands mid-year, who deducts the property tax? The days decide, not who writes the check.

Real estate taxes for the year are divided by days. The seller is treated as paying the tax up to the day before the sale, and the buyer from the sale date on, whoever actually pays the bill.

Erik sells on May first. The year's tax is $3,650, paid by the buyer in October. Erik owned the home a hundred and twenty days, so his share is $1,200, and the buyer's is $2,450.

At closing, Erik credits the buyer with his $1,200 share. Erik deducts $1,200 and the buyer $2,450. If the buyer pays taxes that belong to the seller without reimbursement, they add them to the cost basis instead.

Property taxes go into the same capped bucket as state income taxes: $40,400 for 2026, and they only help if you itemize.

Quick check. A home is sold on July first, after a hundred and eighty-two days. Annual taxes are seventy-three hundred dollars. What's the seller's deduction? Pause and pick.

It's B. A hundred and eighty-two over three sixty-five, times $7,300, is $3,640.

Microteach 14.5 · 1:29Renting out a home or vacation home

Rent for under 15 days is tax-free; heavy personal use limits rental deductions to rental income.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 14. Free reading: IRS Pub. 527.

Read the script

Rent out your home, a cabin or a room, and the tax result depends on counting days.

If you rent a home for fewer than fifteen days, the rent is tax-free and you deduct nothing extra. If personal use is more than fourteen days and more than ten percent of rental days, it's a residence with rental use, and rental deductions can't exceed rental income. With little personal use, it's a rental property under the passive loss rules.

The Lindqvists rent their Marshall house to visitors during Farmfest for ten days at two hundred dollars a night. The $2,000 is tax-free.

Erik rents his cabin on Lake Shetek for sixty days and uses it for thirty. With over fourteen days of personal use, rental deductions are limited to the $9,000 of rent. Two-thirds of costs are rental: interest and taxes first, then operating costs, then depreciation only up to what's left. Net rental income is zero, and $1,000 of depreciation carries forward.

The personal share of mortgage interest and property taxes can still be itemized, as second-home interest and state and local taxes.

Quick check. A cabin is rented a hundred days and used personally nine days. How is it treated? Pause and pick.

It's C. Personal use isn't more than the greater of fourteen days or ten percent of rental days, so it's a rental property.

Microteach 14.6 · 1:35Course recap: one return, start to finish

Every topic in the course fits a line of the tax formula.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 14. Free reading: Form 1040 and instructions.

Read the script

Fourteen lectures, one formula. Here's the Lindqvists' 2026 return, and where each part of the course fits.

Gross income comes from Lecture 5 and Nora's Schedule C from Lecture 9: $118,400. Half of her self-employment tax brings AGI to $116,281. The standard deduction from Lectures 4 and 6 and the QBI deduction from Lecture 9 give taxable income of $78,505.

Lecture 8 gives the tax, $8,925, the child tax credit of $4,400 and self-employment tax of $4,239. Withholding and estimates of $9,000 leave a $236 refund.

Around the return sit the rules for filing and disputes, the planning strategies of Lecture 3, investments and business property, and the rules for pay, retirement and the home.

Every question in this course is a version of four: what's income, what's deductible, at what rate, and what credits apply. Ask who is taxed, when, and on what kind of income, and you can work through any return.

One last quick check. Which part of the formula does the new senior deduction affect? Pause and pick.

It's C. It's taken after AGI, like the standard deduction, but whether or not you itemize. Good luck in the final.

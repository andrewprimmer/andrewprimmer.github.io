Microteach 2.1 · 1:28Who must file, and when

Most people must file when gross income reaches the standard deduction; the 2026 return is due April 15, 2027.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 2. Free reading: IRS Pub. 501; Form 4868.

Read the script

Not everyone has to file a federal tax return. Here's who does, when the return is due, and what an extension really gets you.

The general rule: you must file if your gross income reaches your standard deduction. For 2026 that's $16,100 for a single filer under sixty-five and $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly. Anyone with $400 or more of self-employment earnings must file too.

Even if you're below the threshold, file if tax was withheld from your pay or you qualify for a refundable credit. The money only comes back if you file.

For individuals the tax year is the calendar year. The 2026 return is due April fifteenth, 2027. Form 4868 gives an automatic six-month extension to October fifteenth.

But an extension to file is not an extension to pay. You must estimate what you owe and pay it by April fifteenth, or interest and penalties start to run.

Quick check. Nora's photography sideline made six hundred fifty dollars net, and she has no other income. Must she file? Pause and pick.

It's B. Self-employment earnings of $400 or more trigger a filing requirement, because she owes self-employment tax.

Microteach 2.2 · 1:21The statute of limitations

The IRS normally has three years to assess, six if income is understated by more than 25%, and forever for fraud.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 2. Free reading: IRS Pub. 556.

Read the script

How long can the IRS come back and ask for more tax? Not forever, usually. Here's the statute of limitations.

The statute of limitations is the period in which the IRS can assess additional tax. Once it closes, the year is final, even if the return was wrong.

The normal period is three years from the later of the due date or the date you filed. It's six years if you left out more than twenty-five percent of your gross income. And there's no limit at all for fraud or if you never filed.

An early return is treated as filed on the due date. Jake files on March first, 2027, but the clock starts on April fifteenth, so the three years run to April fifteenth, 2030.

So keep tax records for at least three years after filing. Keep records of what you paid for property, like a house or shares, until three years after you sell it.

Quick check. Ruth reported sixty thousand dollars of gross income but left out twenty thousand. How long does the IRS have? Pause and pick.

It's B. Twenty thousand is more than twenty-five percent of the $60,000 she reported, so the period is six years.

Microteach 2.3 · 1:24How the IRS picks returns to audit

Computer scoring and document matching pick most audits; most are handled by mail.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 2. Free reading: IRS Pub. 556.

Read the script

Fewer than one percent of individual returns are audited each year. So how does the IRS choose them?

Most selections start with the DIF score, a computer rating of how likely a return is to need changes. Information matching compares your return with W-2s and 1099s. Some audits follow from a related return, and a few are random, to keep the scoring model accurate.

Correspondence audits happen by mail and are the most common. Office audits ask you to bring records to an IRS office. Field audits send an agent to your home or business, usually for complex business returns.

Many taxpayers never meet an agent. A matching notice, the CP2000, simply proposes a change when a 1099 doesn't match your return. Answer it on time, with records.

The lesson: report every amount on your W-2s and 1099s. The IRS already has a copy.

Quick check. Sam forgot to report a nine hundred dollar 1099 from a summer job. What's the most likely result? Pause and pick.

It's B. Document matching catches the missing 1099, and the IRS proposes the extra tax by letter.

Microteach 2.4 · 1:17After the audit: appeals and courts

A taxpayer can appeal within the IRS, then go to the Tax Court without paying, or pay and sue for a refund.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 2. Free reading: IRS Pub. 5.

Read the script

The agent says you owe more, and you disagree. Here's the path from the audit to the courtroom.

After the audit you get a thirty-day letter proposing changes. You can ask for a hearing with the IRS Independent Office of Appeals. If you don't settle, a ninety-day letter, the statutory notice of deficiency, gives you ninety days to go to court.

There are three trial courts. The Tax Court is the only one where you don't pay first, which is why most cases go there. In a district court or the Court of Federal Claims, you pay the tax and sue for a refund.

If $50,000 or less is in dispute for a year, the Tax Court's small case procedure is quicker and informal, but the decision can't be appealed.

One more rule. Under the Golsen rule, the Tax Court follows the precedent of the circuit the taxpayer lives in. Marshall is in the Eighth Circuit.

Quick check. The Lindqvists dispute eight thousand dollars and can't afford to pay it first. Where should they go? Pause and pick.

It's C, the Tax Court. It hears cases before payment, and at $8,000 they can use the small case procedure.

Microteach 2.5 · 1:20Sources of tax law

Primary authority comes from Congress, Treasury and the IRS, and the courts; the Code sits at the top.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 2.

Read the script

When you research a tax question, which sources count, and which count most? Here's the hierarchy.

Primary authorities are the law itself. Statutory sources come from Congress, chiefly the Internal Revenue Code. Administrative sources come from Treasury and the IRS. Judicial sources are court decisions.

Among administrative sources, final regulations carry the most weight. Revenue rulings apply the law to a set of facts, and revenue procedures explain how to comply. A private letter ruling binds the IRS only for the taxpayer who asked.

Secondary authorities, like this course's textbook, tax services and journal articles, help you find and understand the law. They aren't the law.

Tax research follows five steps: get the facts, identify the issues, find the relevant authorities, analyze them, and document and communicate your conclusion, usually in a memo to the file.

Quick check. Which source carries the most weight? Pause and pick.

It's C. The Code is the statute, so it outranks regulations and rulings. A letter ruling for someone else isn't authority for you, and a textbook is secondary.

Microteach 2.6 · 1:32Penalties and professional standards

File on time even if you can't pay; preparers answer to Circular 230 and the AICPA's standards.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 2. Free reading: IRS Circular 230.

Read the script

Filing late, paying late, getting it wrong. Each has a price. And the people who prepare returns have rules of their own.

The failure-to-file penalty is five percent of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month the return is late. The failure-to-pay penalty is only half a percent a month. Each tops out at twenty-five percent.

So file on time even if you can't pay. Filing late costs ten times as much each month as paying late. And interest runs on unpaid tax either way.

Errors cost more. Negligence or a substantial understatement brings a twenty percent accuracy penalty. Civil fraud brings seventy-five percent, and the statute of limitations never closes.

Preparers have rules too. Circular 230 governs CPAs, attorneys and enrolled agents who practice before the IRS. CPAs also follow the AICPA's Statements on Standards for Tax Services. Every paid preparer needs a PTIN, and unreasonable positions bring preparer penalties.

Quick check. Leo's aunt files three months late and owes two thousand dollars. What's the failure-to-file penalty, ignoring the offset for late payment? Pause and pick.

It's C. Five percent a month for three months is fifteen percent of $2,000: $300.

Microteach 9.1 · 1:21Ordinary, necessary and reasonable

Business expenses are deductible if they are ordinary, necessary and reasonable in amount.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 9. Free reading: IRS Pub. 334; Schedule C instructions.

Read the script

Nora's photography business is a sole proprietorship. Its income and expenses go on Schedule C. What can she deduct?

Section 162 allows a deduction for expenses that are ordinary, meaning normal in that kind of business, and necessary, meaning helpful and appropriate. They must be reasonable in amount and paid in carrying on a trade or business.

Here's Nora's Schedule C. Receipts of $42,000, less supplies and software, advertising, vehicle costs, insurance, a home office and depreciation on her cameras, leave a net profit of $30,000. That profit carries to her Form 1040 and to Schedule S-E.

Reasonableness matters most for pay. When Nora paid Leo, the deduction depended on the wage being what she'd pay anyone for that work.

And costs that create assets lasting beyond the year, like a new camera, are capitalized and recovered through depreciation, which is Lecture 10.

Quick check. Which of Nora's costs is deductible on Schedule C? Pause and pick.

It's B. The cleaning kit is ordinary and necessary. Groceries and lessons are personal, and fines are never deductible.

Microteach 9.2 · 1:16Limits on business deductions

Meals are 50% deductible; entertainment, fines, political spending and personal costs are not.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 9. Free reading: IRS Pub. 463.

Read the script

Some costs pass the ordinary and necessary test but are still limited or banned. Here are the common ones.

Business meals with clients are fifty percent deductible. Entertainment, like concert tickets for a client, has been disallowed since 2018. Fines, penalties, political contributions and most lobbying are never deductible.

Personal living expenses aren't deductible, nor are costs of earning tax-exempt income. And business gifts are capped at $25 per recipient per year.

Nora takes a wedding client to an eighty-dollar lunch: forty dollars is deductible. She buys the same client two hundred dollars of concert tickets: nothing is deductible.

For meals, travel and gifts, keep records of who, what, when, where and the business purpose. Without them, the deduction can be lost on audit.

Quick check. Nora sends four clients holiday gifts costing sixty dollars each. How much can she deduct? Pause and pick.

It's C. Gifts are capped at $25 per person: four times $25 is $100.

Microteach 9.3 · 1:23Home office, vehicles and travel

Mixed-use costs are deductible only for the business share, under strict rules for home offices and commuting.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 9. Free reading: IRS Pub. 587; Pub. 463.

Read the script

Some costs are part business and part personal: a home office, a car, a trip. Only the business share is deductible.

A home office must be used regularly and exclusively for business, and must be your principal place of business or a place where you meet clients. A corner of the dining table doesn't count.

You can use the simplified method, five dollars a square foot up to three hundred square feet, or the actual method, the business percentage of mortgage interest, taxes, utilities and depreciation. Nora's three-hundred-square-foot studio gives her $1,500 under the simplified method.

Commuting from home to a regular workplace is personal. Driving between work locations is business. Because Nora's home is her principal place of business, her drives to shoots count.

Travel away from home overnight for business makes transport, lodging and half of meals deductible. If the trip is mainly personal, only the business portion of costs at the destination counts.

Quick check. Jake drives twenty miles from home to his office each day. Is that deductible? Pause and pick.

It's B. Driving from home to a regular place of work is commuting, a personal expense.

Microteach 9.4 · 1:19Hobby or business?

Hobby income is taxable but hobby expenses are not deductible; profit motive decides.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 9. Free reading: IRS Pub. 535.

Read the script

Leo sells a few drone videos. Ruth sells quilts. Business or hobby? The answer changes everything.

Both business and hobby income are taxable. But a business deducts its expenses and can use losses against other income. Since 2018, hobby expenses aren't deductible at all.

The regulations list factors that show a profit motive: running it like a business with good records, the time and effort you put in, your expertise, your history of profits and losses, and how much personal pleasure is involved.

There's also a safe harbor. If an activity shows a profit in three of five consecutive years, it's presumed to be a business.

Ruth sells $1,800 of quilts but spends $2,600 on fabric and booth fees. As a hobby, she reports $1,800 of income and deducts nothing. As a business, she'd have an $800 loss. Her lack of records and years of losses point to a hobby.

Quick check. Which fact best supports treating an activity as a business? Pause and pick.

It's C. Separate accounts and a business plan show it's run in a businesslike manner.

Microteach 9.5 · 1:24Accounting periods and methods

Most small businesses use the cash method; accrual businesses deduct when all events and economic performance have occurred.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 9. Free reading: IRS Pub. 538.

Read the script

When does a business count its income and expenses? That depends on its tax year and its accounting method.

Individuals and sole proprietors use the calendar year. Corporations can choose a fiscal year ending on the last day of any month.

Under the cash method, income counts when received and expenses when paid. Under the accrual method, income counts when earned, and expenses when all events have fixed the liability and economic performance has occurred. Small businesses below an inflation-indexed gross receipts test, currently over thirty million dollars, can use the cash method.

A cash-method business can deduct a prepayment, like insurance, if the benefit lasts no more than twelve months and doesn't extend past the end of next year.

Tax accrual isn't always GAAP accrual. For tax, prepaid income can usually be deferred only one year, and reserves for bad debts or warranties aren't deductible until actually incurred.

Quick check. On December first, 2026, Nora pays twelve hundred dollars for twelve months of insurance. What can she deduct in 2026? Pause and pick.

It's B. Under the twelve-month rule, the whole $1,200 is deductible when paid.

Microteach 9.6 · 1:49The QBI deduction

Owners of pass-through businesses deduct up to 20% of qualified business income.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 9. Free reading: Form 8995 instructions.

Read the script

Owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships and S corporations get a deduction worth up to twenty percent of their business income. It's now permanent.

The qualified business income deduction is twenty percent of qualified business income from a pass-through business. It's a deduction from AGI, but you get it whether or not you itemize.

Nora's qualified business income is her $30,000 profit less the deductible half of her self-employment tax: $27,881. Twenty percent is $5,576. The deduction can't exceed twenty percent of taxable income before the deduction, here $16,816, so she gets the full $5,576.

Below $201,750 of taxable income, or $403,500 on a joint return, the calculation is that simple. Above those thresholds, limits based on wages paid and property owned phase in, and owners of specified service businesses, like doctors and lawyers, gradually lose the deduction.

Why does it exist? When the 2017 law cut the corporate rate to twenty-one percent, Congress gave pass-through owners this deduction to narrow the gap. The 2025 law made it permanent.

Quick check. A single sole proprietor has qualified business income of fifty thousand dollars but taxable income before the deduction of only forty thousand. What's the deduction? Pause and pick.

It's B. Twenty percent of QBI is $10,000, but it's limited to twenty percent of taxable income, $8,000.

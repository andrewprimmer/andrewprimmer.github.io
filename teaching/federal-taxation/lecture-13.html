Microteach 13.1 · 1:25Defined benefit and defined contribution plans

DB plans promise a pension; DC plans promise only contributions; both defer tax until withdrawal.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 13. Free reading: IRS Pub. 575.

Read the script

Retirement plans are the biggest tax break most workers ever use. They come in two families.

A defined benefit plan promises a pension based on a formula, like years of service times final salary, and the employer carries the investment risk. A defined contribution plan, like a 401(k), promises only the contributions. What you retire with depends on how they're invested.

Qualified plans get a triple benefit. The employer deducts contributions now. The employee isn't taxed until money comes out. And the investment earnings grow tax-deferred in between.

Your own contributions are always yours. Employer contributions vest over time. For defined contribution plans, either a three-year cliff or graded vesting from year two to year six. Defined benefit plans can take longer.

In exchange for the tax benefits, qualified plans must cover employees broadly and not favor highly paid workers.

Quick check. Jake leaves his job after four years. His match vests on a six-year graded schedule. What share is his? Pause and pick.

It's C. Twenty percent a year starting in year two: twenty percent after two years, forty after three, sixty after four.

Microteach 13.2 · 1:40Traditional or Roth 401(k)?

Traditional saves tax now and taxes withdrawals; Roth taxes contributions now and withdrawals are tax-free.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 13. Free reading: IRS Pub. 560.

Read the script

Most 401(k) plans now offer two flavors: traditional and Roth. The choice comes down to one question: is your tax rate higher now or later?

For 2026 an employee can defer $24,500. Those fifty or older can add $8,000, and those aged sixty to sixty-three, $11,250. Employee and employer contributions together can't exceed $72,000. From 2026, catch-ups for anyone who earned over $150,000 in FICA wages last year must go in as Roth.

Traditional contributions are pre-tax: they lower your taxable wages now, and withdrawals are taxed later. Roth contributions are after-tax, but qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free.

Take $1,000 of pay that grows four times by retirement. In traditional, it grows to $4,000, and at a twelve percent rate in retirement you keep $3,520. In Roth, twenty-two percent tax now leaves $780, which grows to $3,120. If your rate will fall, traditional wins. If it will rise, Roth wins. If it's the same, they tie.

Whatever you choose, contribute at least enough to get the full employer match. It's an instant return that no investment can beat.

Quick check. Sam, twenty, pays no income tax now and expects a higher rate later. Which suits him? Pause and pick.

It's B, Roth. He pays nothing on the contribution now, and all the growth comes out tax-free.

Microteach 13.3 · 1:30Taking money out

Withdrawals before 59½ usually cost a 10% penalty; required minimum distributions start at 73 or 75.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 13. Free reading: IRS Pub. 590-B.

Read the script

Retirement plans give you tax breaks going in. Rules on getting money out keep them for retirement.

Withdrawals from traditional accounts are taxed as ordinary income. Before age fifty-nine and a half, there's usually an extra ten percent penalty as well.

There are exceptions: leaving your employer in or after the year you turn fifty-five, for that employer's plan; death or disability; a series of substantially equal payments; and for IRAs, up to $10,000 for a first home and qualified college costs.

If Jake withdrew $10,000 from his 401(k) at thirty-nine, he'd pay $1,200 of federal income tax, a $1,000 penalty and about $680 of Minnesota tax, keeping only $7,120.

At the other end, you can't defer forever. Required minimum distributions from traditional accounts begin at seventy-three, or seventy-five for those born in 1960 or later. Roth accounts have no required distributions during the owner's life.

Quick check. When is a withdrawal of Roth IRA earnings fully tax-free? Pause and pick.

It's B. A qualified distribution needs the account to be five years old and the owner to be fifty-nine and a half, disabled, or deceased.

Microteach 13.4 · 1:28Traditional and Roth IRAs

Anyone with earned income can put up to $7,500 into IRAs in 2026; deductibility and Roth eligibility depend on income.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 13. Free reading: IRS Pub. 590-A.

Read the script

Individual retirement accounts are open to anyone with earned income, with or without an employer plan.

For 2026 you can put up to $7,500 into traditional and Roth IRAs combined, plus $1,100 if you're fifty or older, limited to your earned income. You have until April fifteenth, 2027 to make 2026 contributions.

A traditional IRA contribution is deductible for AGI, unless you're covered by a plan at work and your income is above a phase-out range. Roth contributions are never deductible, and high earners can't contribute directly at all.

A spouse with little or no earnings can still contribute, based on the couple's combined earned income, on a joint return.

You can also convert traditional IRA money to a Roth at any income. The converted amount is taxed now, but there's no ten percent penalty, and future growth is tax-free. Conversions make sense in low-income years.

Quick check. Leo, fifteen, earned three thousand dollars editing photos. What's the most he can put in a Roth IRA? Pause and pick.

It's B. Contributions are limited to the lesser of $7,500 or earned income. There's no minimum age.

Microteach 13.5 · 1:43Retirement plans for the self-employed

Sole proprietors can use a SEP IRA or a solo 401(k), deducting contributions for AGI.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 13. Free reading: IRS Pub. 560.

Read the script

Nora has no employer plan. But self-employed people can set up plans of their own, often with bigger limits than an IRA.

A SEP IRA lets a sole proprietor contribute about twenty percent of net self-employment earnings. A solo 401(k) allows the same employer contribution plus an employee deferral of up to $24,500. Both are deductible for AGI.

For a sole proprietor, the twenty percent applies to net profit after half of self-employment tax. Nora's net earnings are $27,881, so she can put $5,576 into a SEP.

With a solo 401(k) she could also defer up to $24,500 as the employee, though total contributions can't exceed her net earnings. That's useful in a big year.

A SEP can even be set up and funded after year-end, up to the due date of the return including extensions, which makes it a useful last-minute deduction.

Quick check. A sole proprietor's net profit is a hundred thousand dollars, and self-employment tax is fourteen thousand one hundred thirty. What's the maximum SEP contribution? Pause and pick.

It's B. Net earnings are $100,000 less half the SE tax, $7,065: $92,935. Twenty percent is $18,587.

Microteach 13.6 · 1:35Nonqualified deferred compensation

Nonqualified plans defer executives' pay without contribution limits, but the employer's deduction is deferred too.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 13.

Read the script

Executives often want to save more than qualified plans allow. Nonqualified deferred compensation fills the gap, at a price.

A nonqualified plan has no contribution limits and can cover only selected executives. But the employer can't deduct the pay until the employee is taxed on it, years later. And the executive is an unsecured creditor: if the company fails, the money may be lost.

Section 409A sets strict rules. Deferral elections must generally be made before the year the work is done, and payments can only come on fixed events like separation from service or a set date. Breaking the rules makes the deferred pay taxable at once, plus a twenty percent penalty.

Kari defers a $50,000 bonus for five years, expecting her rate to fall from thirty-five to twenty-four percent. She saves $5,500 of tax, before the time value of money, but her employer waits five years for its deduction, and she bears the company's credit risk.

Nonqualified deferral is a trade between the two parties: the employee's tax deferral against the employer's delayed deduction. It works best when the employee's rate will fall and the employer's won't.

Quick check. When does the employer deduct nonqualified deferred compensation? Pause and pick.

It's B. The deduction is matched to when the employee is taxed.

Microteach 12.1 · 1:27Salary, bonuses and withholding

Employers deduct reasonable pay; employees are taxed when paid, with tax withheld under the W-4.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 12. Free reading: IRS Pub. 15-T; Form W-4.

Read the script

Compensation is a two-sided deal: the employee is taxed, and the employer deducts. Start with plain salary and bonuses.

The employee includes wages in income when received and pays their half of FICA. The employer deducts the wages, as long as they're reasonable, and pays and deducts its matching half of FICA.

Timing can differ. An accrual-method employer can deduct a year-end bonus in the year it's accrued if it's paid within two and a half months after year-end, while the cash-method employee isn't taxed until it's paid.

Withholding is set by Form W-4. Since 2020 it no longer uses allowances. Instead, it asks about multiple jobs, dependents, other income and extra deductions, so withholding comes closer to the actual tax.

One limit on the employer side: a public company can't deduct more than a million dollars a year of pay to each of its top executives.

Quick check. Jake's employer, on the accrual method, declares a five thousand dollar bonus on December twentieth and pays it February tenth. When does each side report it? Pause and pick.

It's B. The employer deducts it in 2026 under the two-and-a-half-month rule. Jake includes it in 2027, when he's paid.

Microteach 12.2 · 1:40No tax on tips and overtime

From 2025 to 2028 workers can deduct up to $25,000 of qualified tips and up to $12,500 of overtime premium.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 12. Free reading: Schedule 1-A (Form 1040).

Read the script

The 2025 law promised no tax on tips and overtime. In practice, they're two new deductions, with limits.

Workers in occupations that customarily receive tips can deduct up to $25,000 of qualified tips a year from 2025 through 2028. Tips must be voluntary, so automatic service charges don't count. The deduction phases out above $150,000 of modified AGI, or $300,000 joint.

Jess, twenty-eight, is a server at the restaurant where Sam works. She earns $18,000 of wages and reports $22,000 of tips. After her standard deduction and the tips deduction, her taxable income is only $1,900, and her income tax falls from about $2,620 to $190.

The overtime deduction covers only the premium part of overtime required by the Fair Labor Standards Act, the half in time-and-a-half. It's capped at $12,500, or $25,000 on a joint return, with the same phase-out.

Dan, a technician at a Marshall plant, earns thirty dollars an hour and works three hundred overtime hours at forty-five. Only the fifteen-dollar premium counts: $4,500 is deductible.

Quick check. Which is still taxed? Pause and pick.

It's B. Both deductions cut income tax only. Tips and overtime are still wages for Social Security and Medicare.

Microteach 12.3 · 1:32Employee or independent contractor?

Control decides worker status, which decides who pays payroll taxes and who withholds.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 12. Free reading: IRS Pub. 15-A.

Read the script

When Nora hires help, is the worker her employee or an independent contractor? The answer decides who pays payroll taxes.

The IRS looks at control. Behavioral control: does the business direct how the work is done? Financial control: who provides equipment and bears the risk of loss? And the relationship: are there benefits, and is it ongoing?

An employee splits FICA with the employer, and the employer withholds income tax and files a W-2. A contractor pays all of their self-employment tax and makes estimated payments. From 2026, a business must file a 1099-NEC for a contractor paid $2,000 or more, up from $600.

Calling an employee a contractor to save payroll tax is risky. The business can owe the back taxes it should have withheld and paid, plus penalties and interest.

Nora's second shooter brings her own cameras, works for other photographers and decides how to shoot: a contractor. Her studio assistant works set hours on tasks Nora assigns: an employee.

Quick check. Nora pays a freelance editor fifteen hundred dollars in 2026. Must she file a ten ninety-nine N E C? Pause and pick.

It's B. From 2026 the threshold is $2,000. The editor must still report the income.

Microteach 12.4 · 1:37Stock options

Nonqualified options create ordinary income at exercise; ISOs can turn the whole gain into long-term capital gain.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 12. Free reading: IRS Pub. 525.

Read the script

Stock options let employees buy company shares at a fixed price. How they're taxed depends on the type.

When you exercise an option, the bargain element is the share's value minus the price you pay. Kari, Erik's wife, holds options on a thousand shares at twenty dollars, and exercises when the shares are worth fifty: a $30,000 bargain element.

With nonqualified options, Kari has $30,000 of ordinary income at exercise, subject to payroll tax, and her employer deducts the same amount. Selling later at seventy gives a $20,000 capital gain. With incentive stock options, there's no regular tax at exercise, and the whole $50,000 is capital gain on sale. But the employer gets no deduction.

To get ISO treatment, Kari must hold the shares two years from the grant and one year from exercise. Selling sooner is a disqualifying disposition, taxed like a nonqualified option.

One catch: the ISO bargain element counts for the alternative minimum tax in the year of exercise, which can bring a surprise AMT bill.

Quick check. Kari exercises nonqualified options on five hundred shares, at ten dollars, when they're worth twenty-five. What income does she have? Pause and pick.

It's C. The bargain element is fifteen dollars times five hundred: $7,500 of ordinary income.

Microteach 12.5 · 1:18Restricted stock and the 83(b) election

Restricted stock is taxed at vesting unless the employee makes an 83(b) election to be taxed at grant.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 12. Free reading: IRS Pub. 525.

Read the script

Restricted stock is real shares, but you lose them if you leave before they vest. When are they taxed? Usually at vesting, unless you choose otherwise.

Under Section 83, restricted stock is taxed as ordinary income when it vests, at its value on that date. The employer deducts the same amount.

Kari is granted a thousand restricted shares worth ten dollars, vesting in three years, when they're worth forty. Without an election, she has $40,000 of ordinary income at vesting. With an eighty-three B election within thirty days of the grant, she has only $10,000 of ordinary income now, and all later growth is capital gain.

The election must be filed within thirty days of the grant. Miss it, and it's gone.

The risk: if Kari leaves before the shares vest and forfeits them, she gets no deduction for the $10,000 she already paid tax on. The election is a bet that the shares will rise and that she'll stay.

Quick check. When is an eighty-three B election most attractive? Pause and pick.

It's B. A low value at grant means little tax now, and expected growth becomes capital gain.

Microteach 12.6 · 1:25Employee expenses and accountable plans

Unreimbursed employee expenses aren't deductible, so reimbursement through an accountable plan is the tax-efficient route.

Reading: McGraw Hill's Taxation of Individuals, Chapter 12. Free reading: IRS Pub. 463.

Read the script

Jake pays for a conference out of his own pocket. Can he deduct it? Not anymore. Here's why reimbursement matters.

Unreimbursed employee business expenses used to be a miscellaneous itemized deduction. The 2017 law suspended it, and the 2025 law made that permanent. Employees generally can't deduct work costs they pay themselves.

The answer is an accountable plan. If the expense has a business connection, the employee substantiates it promptly, and any excess advance is returned, reimbursements are tax-free to the employee and deductible by the employer.

If Jake's employer reimburses his $2,000 conference under an accountable plan, it's tax-free to him. If it pays a flat allowance with no receipts, that's wages. If Jake pays and isn't reimbursed, he gets no deduction at all.

So an employee with regular work costs is better off with an accountable reimbursement than an equal raise. The raise is taxed; the reimbursement isn't.

Quick check. Jake buys three hundred dollars of work boots his job requires, and isn't reimbursed. What can he deduct? Pause and pick.

It's C, nothing. Unreimbursed employee expenses aren't deductible.

Management Accounting with Claude for Excel

One fictional car maker, a full year of its data, and seven exercises that use AI in Excel to apply the core techniques of management accounting, from variance analysis to reading the year-end accounts.

Halden Motors Ltd is a made-up UK vehicle manufacturer, assembling cars in Birmingham and battery packs in Coventry. It makes four models: the Sprite electric city car, the Ridge hybrid SUV, the Torque GT electric performance saloon and the Haul E-Van. The workbook holds its 2025 budget, standard costs, monthly actuals, overheads, dealer sales, capital projects, KPIs and sales ledger.

Things went wrong during the year, as they do in real businesses. Battery-cell prices spiked in the summer, a pay award arrived in April, a steel quality problem raised scrap in March and a strike hit October. The exercises ask you to find those events in the numbers.

Halden Motors and every dealer, supplier and person in the data are fictional. The figures are synthetic but internally consistent, so totals reconcile across sheets.

How to work through an exercise

  1. If the technique is new to you, watch the short basics video at the top of the exercise.
  2. Open the dataset in Excel and start Claude for Excel from the ribbon.
  3. Copy the exercise prompt below and paste it into Claude.
  4. Check what Claude builds. Click into its formulas, trace the cell references and test them with a figure you can work out by hand.
  5. Explain the result in business terms using the discussion point. Only then compare your answer with the worked solution.

Each worked solution is a single sheet. It copies the inputs it needs, builds every calculation as a live formula, and opens with the key findings. Blue figures are inputs from the dataset, black are formulas, and yellow cells are assumptions you can change. The key findings are written text and will not update when you change an input.

Claude can make mistakes, as can any spreadsheet. Treat its output the way you would treat a junior colleague's work: check it before you rely on it.

Exercise oneStandard costing & variance analysis

Sheets used: Std_Cost_Card, Actual_Costs, Sales_Production

The basics in two minutes

Two short videos on the technique, with a simple worked example in each. Watch them before you try the exercise.

Flexing the budget and material variancesPart 1 · 1:40 · no sound

Read the script

Variance analysis, part one. A standard cost is what one unit should cost. A variance is the gap between what it should have cost and what it did cost.

A workshop makes chairs. The standard for one chair is four kilos of wood at five pounds a kilo, and two hours of labour at twelve pounds an hour.

The budget was a thousand chairs. The workshop actually made eleven hundred.

Never compare actual cost with the original budget. Flex it: ask what eleven hundred chairs should have cost.

For wood: eleven hundred chairs at four kilos is forty-four hundred kilos. At five pounds, that should cost twenty-two thousand.

It actually used forty-six hundred kilos at five pounds fifty: twenty-five thousand three hundred. That is three thousand three hundred adverse.

Pause the video. How much of that three thousand three hundred is down to the price paid, and how much to the quantity used?

Price variance: the difference in price, times the actual quantity. Fifty pence times forty-six hundred kilos is two thousand three hundred adverse.

Usage variance: the extra quantity, valued at standard price. Two hundred kilos at five pounds is one thousand adverse.

Check: two thousand three hundred plus one thousand is three thousand three hundred.

Who owns each one? Price belongs to purchasing: market prices, supplier choice, the quality bought. Usage belongs to production: scrap, machine faults, skill.

And they interact. Cheap wood can give a favourable price variance and an adverse usage variance. Next: labour and variable overhead.

Labour and variable overhead variancesPart 2 · 1:40 · no sound

Read the script

Variance analysis, part two. Same workshop, same eleven hundred chairs. Now the people and the overheads.

Labour should take two hours a chair: twenty-two hundred hours at twelve pounds, twenty-six thousand four hundred.

The workshop paid for twenty-four hundred hours at twelve pounds forty. A hundred and fifty of those hours were idle during a breakdown, so twenty-two fifty were worked. Total: three thousand three hundred and sixty adverse.

Rate variance: forty pence times the twenty-four hundred hours paid. Nine hundred and sixty adverse.

Idle time: a hundred and fifty idle hours at the standard rate. Eighteen hundred adverse.

Efficiency: hours worked against standard hours. Fifty hours at twelve pounds, six hundred adverse.

Check: nine sixty, plus eighteen hundred, plus six hundred, is three thousand three hundred and sixty.

Variable overhead follows the hours worked. The standard is three pounds an hour. The actual cost was seven thousand two hundred.

Spending variance: actual cost, less hours worked at the standard rate. Four hundred and fifty adverse.

Efficiency variance: the same fifty extra hours, at three pounds. One hundred and fifty adverse.

Notice the pattern. Every cost variance is one of two shapes. A price type: the difference in rate, times the actual quantity. Or a quantity type: the difference in quantity, times the standard rate.

Then ask why. A pay award? A breakdown? New staff? The number only starts the conversation.

The exercise

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Build a flexed budget for each model and month, then calculate material price & usage, labour rate, idle time & efficiency, and variable overhead spending & efficiency variances. Summarise the five largest variances and their likely causes.

Discuss: Link variances to operational events (cell price spike, pay award, robots, strike).

Show the headline answer

Total cost variance £6.17m adverse; the battery-cell price variance (£4.04m adverse) is the largest single item.

Exercise twoSales variances

Sheets used: Products, Std_Cost_Card, Sales_Production

The basics in two minutes

Why selling more units can still disappoint: price and volume variances, then volume split into mix and quantity. Watch it before you try the exercise.

Sales price, volume, mix and quantity1:39 · no sound

Read the script

Sales variances. Selling more is not enough. What you sell, and at what price, matters too.

A bike shop sells two models. The budget is six hundred Standard bikes at a hundred pounds, earning forty pounds contribution each, and four hundred Deluxe at two hundred pounds, earning ninety.

It actually sold seven hundred and seventy Standard at ninety-five pounds, and three hundred and thirty Deluxe at full price. Eleven hundred bikes in all.

Price variance: the price difference times the units actually sold. Five pounds off seven hundred and seventy bikes is three thousand eight hundred and fifty adverse.

Volume variance: the change in units, times standard contribution. A hundred and seventy more Standard is six thousand eight hundred favourable. Seventy fewer Deluxe is six thousand three hundred adverse. Net: five hundred favourable.

Pause the video. A hundred extra bikes, but only five hundred pounds of extra contribution. Why so little?

Split it in two. Quantity: the extra units, at the budget average contribution. The budget earns sixty thousand on a thousand bikes, so sixty pounds each. A hundred extra is six thousand favourable.

Mix: at the budget mix, eleven hundred bikes would be six sixty Standard and four forty Deluxe. The shop sold a hundred and ten more of the low earner and a hundred and ten fewer of the high earner. Five thousand five hundred adverse.

Check: six thousand favourable, less five thousand five hundred adverse, is five hundred favourable.

What it tells you. Price is about discounting and competition. Quantity is about market size and share. Mix is about which products customers chose. Each one needs a different conversation.

The exercise

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Calculate sales price, sales volume (contribution basis), sales mix and quantity variances by model for FY2025.

Discuss: Distinguish market-driven vs management-driven variances.

Show the headline answer

Sales price variance £11.97m adverse; volume variance £3.03m favourable (quantity £8.40m F, mix £5.37m A).

Exercise threeAbsorption vs marginal costing

Sheets used: Std_Cost_Card, Assumptions, Sales_Production, Fixed_Costs

The basics in two minutes

Two ways to treat fixed production overhead, why they give different profits, and how stock explains the gap. Watch it before you try the exercise.

Absorption vs marginal costing1:35 · no sound

Read the script

Absorption and marginal costing. One factory, two profit figures. The only difference is what happens to fixed production overhead.

Under marginal costing, stock is valued at variable cost only. Fixed overhead is charged in full to the period.

Under absorption costing, each unit absorbs a share of fixed overhead, and that share sits in stock until the unit is sold.

A factory sells one product at fifteen pounds. Variable cost is six pounds. Fixed overhead is forty thousand a month, across ten thousand units: four pounds each.

This month it made ten thousand and sold eight thousand. Two thousand units go into stock.

Pause the video and work out the profit under each method.

Marginal: sales of a hundred and twenty thousand, less variable cost of forty-eight thousand, gives contribution of seventy-two thousand. Take off all forty thousand of fixed overhead. Profit: thirty-two thousand.

Absorption: the same sales, less eight thousand units at the full cost of ten pounds. Profit: forty thousand.

The gap is eight thousand. That is two thousand units in stock, each carrying four pounds of fixed overhead into next month.

The rule. When stock rises, absorption profit is higher. When stock falls, marginal profit is higher. With no change in stock, they agree.

Why it matters. Under absorption, a manager can lift profit just by producing for stock. Marginal costing suits decisions. Absorption is required for published accounts.

The exercise

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Calculate the fixed overhead absorption rate per labour hour, prepare quarterly profit statements under absorption and marginal costing, and reconcile the difference through inventory movements.

Discuss: Explain why profits differ and the implications for managerial incentives.

Show the headline answer

Absorption profit £26.70m against marginal profit £27.94m, after £6.17m of adverse variable cost variances. The £1.24m gap is the fixed overhead in the inventory change.

Exercise fourActivity-Based Costing

Sheets used: ABC_Cost_Pools, Std_Cost_Card, Products

The basics in two minutes

Why a single labour-hour rate misprices products, and how ABC assigns overhead by the activities each product causes. Watch it before you try the exercise.

Activity-based costing1:44 · no sound

Read the script

Activity-based costing. Who really causes the overhead? ABC shares it out by the activities each product uses, not by labour hours.

A factory makes two products. Nine thousand Basic and one thousand Custom. Each takes one labour hour. Overhead is two hundred thousand.

The traditional method divides overhead by labour hours. Twenty pounds on every unit, Basic or Custom.

But Custom needs far more set-ups and inspections.

ABC has four steps. Group overhead into activity cost pools. Pick a cost driver for each pool. Divide pool cost by driver volume to get a rate. Then charge each product for the drivers it uses.

Two pools. Set-ups cost a hundred and twenty thousand, for forty set-ups. Inspection costs eighty thousand, for eight hundred inspections.

So a set-up costs three thousand pounds, and an inspection costs a hundred.

Pause the video. Basic uses ten set-ups and two hundred inspections. Custom uses thirty and six hundred. What is the overhead per unit?

Basic: thirty thousand plus twenty thousand is fifty thousand. Across nine thousand units, five pounds fifty-six each.

Custom: ninety thousand plus sixty thousand is a hundred and fifty thousand. Across one thousand units, a hundred and fifty pounds each.

Check: fifty plus a hundred and fifty is the full two hundred thousand.

Under the traditional rate both carried twenty pounds. Under ABC, Basic carries under six, and Custom carries a hundred and fifty.

That is cross-subsidy. The high-volume product was paying for the complex one. Total overhead is unchanged. ABC only changes who carries it.

The exercise

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Compute cost driver rates and a full ABC cost per vehicle for each model. Compare with the traditional labour-hour absorption cost and comment on Torque GT's profitability.

Discuss: Cross-subsidisation between high- and low-volume products.

Show the headline answer

Torque GT overhead rises from £7,053 to £12,932 a car under ABC, and its margin falls from 26.6% to 17.1%.

Exercise fiveOverhead apportionment

Sheets used: Departments

The basics in two minutes

The four steps that take overhead from the invoice to a rate per hour, with a three-department example. Watch it before you try the exercise.

Allocate, apportion, reapportion, absorb1:44 · no sound

Read the script

Overhead apportionment: getting overhead from the invoice to a rate per hour. Four steps. Allocate, apportion, reapportion, absorb.

Allocate: a cost that belongs to one department goes straight to it. Apportion: a shared cost is split on a fair basis.

Rent is split on floor area. The canteen on headcount. Machine insurance on machine value.

Rent is sixty thousand. Machining has half the floor, so thirty thousand. Assembly eighteen thousand. Maintenance twelve thousand.

Add the costs already allocated. Machining now holds a hundred thousand, Assembly sixty thousand, and Maintenance forty thousand.

Step three. Maintenance makes nothing. It is a service department, so its forty thousand moves to the departments it serves.

Pause the video. Maintenance spends seventy-five percent of its hours in Machining and twenty-five percent in Assembly. What are the new totals?

Machining takes thirty thousand, making a hundred and thirty. Assembly takes ten thousand, making seventy. The total is still two hundred thousand.

Step four: absorb. Machining divides by thirteen thousand machine hours: ten pounds an hour. Assembly divides by fourteen thousand labour hours: five pounds an hour.

Use the hours that drive each department. Machines in Machining, people in Assembly.

One complication: service departments often serve each other. Step-down clears them one at a time and ignores work flowing back. The reciprocal method recognises both directions, using repeated rounds or equations.

The answers are usually close. Reciprocal is the more accurate.

The exercise

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Apportion shared overheads using the suggested bases, then reapportion service centres using (a) step-down and (b) the reciprocal method. Compute a machine-hour rate for Body Shop and labour-hour rates for Final Assembly.

Discuss: Sensitivity of product costs to apportionment method.

Show the headline answer

Step-down and reciprocal rates are close: Body Shop about £289 per machine hour.

Exercise sixFinancial statement analysis

Sheets used: FS_Income_Statement, FS_Balance_Sheet, FS_Cash_Flow, FS_Notes

The basics in two minutes

Four questions to ask of any set of accounts, with the key ratio for each and why profit is not the same as cash. Watch it before you try the exercise.

Reading the year-end accounts1:47 · no sound

Read the script

Financial statement analysis. Four questions to ask of any set of accounts: is the company profitable, efficient, liquid, and safely financed?

A small company. Revenue of one million, cost of sales of seven hundred thousand, operating profit of a hundred thousand, and interest of twenty thousand.

On the balance sheet: inventory a hundred and forty, receivables a hundred, cash forty. Current liabilities are a hundred and forty, including seventy of trade payables. Borrowings are three hundred and equity five hundred.

Question one: is it profitable? Operating margin is operating profit over revenue. Ten percent.

Return on capital employed is operating profit over equity plus borrowings. A hundred over eight hundred: twelve and a half percent. Compare it with the cost of that capital.

Question two: is it efficient? Inventory sits for seventy-three days. Customers take thirty-six and a half days to pay. And the company takes thirty-six and a half days to pay its suppliers.

The cash conversion cycle is inventory days plus receivable days, less payable days. Seventy-three days between paying out and getting paid.

Question three: is it liquid? Current assets over current liabilities. Two hundred and eighty over a hundred and forty: two.

Question four: is it safely financed? Gearing is borrowings over borrowings plus equity: thirty-seven and a half percent. Interest cover is operating profit over interest: five times.

Pause the video. Next year profit is the same, but receivables double to two hundred thousand. What happens to cash?

A hundred thousand more is tied up in customers. Same profit, less cash. That is why you read the cash flow statement too.

And remember, one ratio is just a number. Compare it with last year, with the sector, and with the bank's covenants.

The exercise

The dataset includes Halden Motors' mock year-end financial statements for FY2025, with FY2024 comparatives, in thousands of pounds. Operating profit agrees to the absorption costing profit from exercise three, finished vehicle stock to the same exercise, and trade receivables to the sales ledger in exercise seven.

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Analyse Halden Motors' FY2025 year-end financial statements on the four FS_ sheets against FY2024. Calculate profitability, working capital, liquidity, gearing and cash flow ratios for both years, compare them with the sector benchmarks and bank covenants in FS_Notes, and summarise the five most important changes and their likely causes.

Discuss: Revenue grew but cash fell: reconcile profit to cash. Which findings from the costing and credit control exercises explain the movements? How close is the company to breaching a covenant?

Show the headline answer

Revenue is up 8.6% but operating profit is down 17.6%: operating margin falls from 4.8% to 3.6% and ROCE from 12.3% to 9.1%. Receivable days rise from 31 to 39, cash falls £15.2m and net debt rises £37.2m. Interest cover drops from 4.3x to 2.7x against a 2.5x covenant.

Exercise sevenAged debtor analysis & credit control

Sheets used: Debtors_Ledger, Customers

The basics in two minutes

Ageing bands, debtor days and a bad-debt provision, worked through on a £100,000 sales ledger. Watch it before you try the exercise.

Reading an aged debtor report1:51 · no sound

Read the script

Aged debtors. Who owes us, and for how long? A sale is not cash until the customer pays. The aged debtor report shows how late they are.

Every unpaid invoice goes into a band, by the number of days past its due date.

Here is a ledger of a hundred thousand pounds. Sixty percent is current, not yet due. Twenty percent is up to thirty days late. Ten percent is thirty-one to sixty. Six percent is sixty-one to ninety, and four percent is over ninety days late.

Age from the due date, not the invoice date. Otherwise every customer on sixty-day terms looks late.

Debtor days: debtors, divided by credit sales, times three hundred and sixty-five. Pause the video. Debtors are a hundred thousand and credit sales for the year are seven hundred and thirty thousand. How many days does it take to collect?

A hundred thousand over seven hundred and thirty thousand, times three sixty-five, is fifty days. If terms are thirty days, customers are taking twenty days too long.

The older the debt, the less likely it is to be paid. So provide a higher percentage on the older bands.

One percent of current is six hundred. Two percent of the next band is four hundred. Ten percent is a thousand. Twenty-five percent is fifteen hundred. Fifty percent of the oldest is two thousand. Total provision: five thousand five hundred.

The rates are a judgement. Base them on past collection experience.

What to look for. Customers over their credit limit. Disputed invoices, which are a query, not a slow payer. Unallocated cash and credit notes not yet matched. And one large old balance hiding in the total.

Then act. Chase the oldest and largest first, and stop supplying before the loss grows.

The exercise

Prompt for Claude for Excel

Using the report date of 31-Dec-2025, calculate the outstanding balance on every open item and age it by days past due into Current, 1–30, 31–60, 61–90 and 90+ days. Build an aged debtor report by customer with totals, % of ledger in each band, and flags for customers over their credit limit. Then calculate debtor days and suggest a bad-debt provision.

Discuss: Ageing from invoice date vs due date; treatment of credit notes, disputed items and unallocated cash; customer in administration.

Show the headline answer

£82.8m ledger, 39 debtor days, six accounts over their credit limit and an £11.05m bad-debt provision.

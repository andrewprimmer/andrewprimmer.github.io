One railway, five tax authorities

How the profits of an indivisible undertaking were divided between five revenue administrations before international tax law existed, and why nobody ever took the division to court.

Andrew Primmer · Aston Business School

In the balance sheet notes to his 1945 report on the ownership of Rhodesia Railways, Sir Harold Howitt recorded a fact in a single clause and moved on. A basis for allocating the company's capital expenditure across the territories its lines ran through had been agreed with the tax authorities. He then gave the result.

57%

32%

9%

2%
United Kingdom
£1,705

Howitt meant it as a technical aside on the way to a larger argument about nationalisation. But the clause describes something for which the tax law of the period made no provision at all. Five revenue authorities, none of them bound to any other by a double taxation convention, had settled between them how the capital base of a single indivisible undertaking should be divided. They had done it by agreement with the taxpayer. No statute prescribed the split. No case law on allocation existed to fall back on. Howitt conceded in the next sentence that the agreed division did not entirely follow the physical situation of the assets.

My current research reconstructs how that settlement was built, and what happened when part of it was tested in court.

A problem with no legal answer

Rhodesia Railways operated a physically continuous railway across Southern Rhodesia, Northern Rhodesia, the Bechuanaland Protectorate and the Union of South Africa, while being incorporated, domiciled and financed in London. It was assessed to income tax in all five places.

A train carrying copper south from the Copperbelt crossed several fiscal borders in one journey. No transaction occurred when it did. There was no sale between a Northern Rhodesian railway and a Southern Rhodesian one, because no such separate businesses existed. There was one railway. And yet five administrations each needed to know how much of its profit was theirs.

The answer they arrived at was administrative rather than legislative. Operating revenue was apportioned by railway mileage. Expenditure and local revenues were attributed to the territory where they were actually incurred. Where assets were genuinely shared, vehicle-mile statistics were used instead.

What makes the hybrid interesting is where the line between its two halves fell. Formulary allocation was used precisely where no administration could independently verify anything — the revenue generated by a journey spanning four territories cannot be observed. Actual allocation was used where the stronger administrations could check the answer, as with wages, workshop expenditure and local purchases. The composition of the method tracked the boundary of joint verifiability. It was not an arbitrary compromise; it was a map of who could audit what.

The company's ledgers became fiscal infrastructure

This left the revenue authorities dependent on the firm they were taxing in a way that goes beyond the usual observation that taxpayers file their own returns.

Rhodesia Railways held the only accounting system capable of describing the undertaking as a whole. Its ledgers recorded traffic, mileage, renewals and territorial costs across the entire network. No single administration could reconstruct the economic activity of a railway spanning the region, and none was in a position to propose an alternative basis of division, because none could see the whole.

The taxpayer was not merely being measured by the state. The taxpayer supplied the categories through which measurement was possible.

Accounting systems are usually treated as objects that revenue authorities inspect. Here they were something more fundamental. They formed part of the administrative infrastructure of taxation itself.

Then the railway went to the Privy Council

One reading of a quiet administrative record is that the colonial fiscal system had no effective forum for dispute. That reading is wrong, and the evidence against it is emphatic.

In 1933 Rhodesia Railways took the Collector of Income Tax of the Bechuanaland Protectorate to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, and won.

The dispute concerned a programme of renewals on worn and dangerous track between Vryburg and Bulawayo. The company charged £252,174 to revenue, roughly six times its ordinary maintenance charge for the year, and returned an overall loss of £97,445. The Collector wrote back the entire renewals figure, converting that loss into an assessed profit of £154,729 and a tax demand of £17,188.

The scale is worth registering. Against an eighteen-year average of some £24,000 a year in Bechuanaland railway tax, a single disputed assessment of over £17,000 was most of a normal year's collection from the territory's largest taxpayer. This was not a marginal adjustment. The Collector overruled the company's objection and successfully defended his assessment through two tiers before losing in London.

That record is hard to reconcile with a picture of a small administration cowed by its dominant taxpayer. What it shows is not incapacity but a particular shape of capacity. Bechuanaland could identify a large and legally arguable point and sustain it domestically. It could not prevail against a metropolitan company with London solicitors before an appellate forum applying doctrine developed in and for well-resourced revenue systems. Aggression at assessment and weakness on appeal are not contradictory. They are two faces of the same structural position.

An accounting category became the legal test

The evidential structure of the case matters as much as its outcome.

The distinction on which everything turned was between ordinary maintenance and the periodic renewal of worn-out track. That distinction did not come from the statute. It came from railway accounting practice, and it was put before the Board by the company's chief accountant as a description of how the company's own management accounts worked, for operational reasons having nothing to do with tax. The Judicial Committee adopted it, and in adopting it converted an internal ledger category into the operative legal test for a quarter of a million pounds of deduction.

The same witness supplied the comparative evidence, telling the court that renewals were allowed as a deduction in the Union, in Rhodesia and in the United Kingdom. No revenue department produced that comparison. None was in a position to. The only party with a synoptic view of how the company was taxed across the system was the company, and when the court needed that view it came from the witness box.

So why was allocation never adjudicated?

Here is the puzzle. If the company would litigate a quarter of a million pounds to London, and the smallest administration would provoke it, why was the allocation convention — plainly conventional, plainly consequential, and admitted by Howitt not to follow the physical facts — never tested?

Three conditions must hold for a question to be adjudicable, and allocation failed at least one for every possible party.

Standing. No administration could sue another. The Judicial Committee's jurisdiction ran vertically, from each territory's courts up to London, never horizontally between territories. Southern Rhodesia's extraterritorial charge injured Bechuanaland's base, but Bechuanaland had no cause of action and no tribunal before which to assert one.

Legal ground. There was nothing to attack the allocation under. Each statute charged profits arising within the territory and left "arising" undefined. A convention agreed in correspondence is not an enacted formula whose vires can be tested. Quantification disputes, by contrast, arose under detailed statutory language, which is exactly what gave the 1933 case its foothold.

Incentive. Southern Rhodesia charged profits arising in weaker neighbouring jurisdictions, with credit for tax paid there. Profit shifted out of Southern Rhodesia was therefore picked up again at the Southern Rhodesian rate. The credit mechanism largely neutralised any gain from arguing for a more favourable division, so the company's incentive ran towards enlarging deductions rather than relocating profit.

The silence was not consent. It was the product of a jurisdictional structure that gave no administration standing, a legal form that gave no ground of challenge, and a credit mechanism that removed the taxpayer's incentive.

There is a further twist. The allocation convention itself cost the dominant territory about three percentage points of the profit base. Its unilateral extraterritorial charge then recovered fourteen. The formula was not where the distribution happened, which is worth remembering whenever a debate about fairness fixes on the apportionment method alone.

There was never one tax base

It is intuitive to imagine governments computing a single corporate profit and then dividing it. That is not what occurred.

Allocation was common; quantification was not. The agreed schedule produced territorial inputs which were then fed into five different bodies of deduction law. The same permanent-way expenditure was deductible in the Union, in Rhodesia and in the United Kingdom, and disallowed in the Protectorate until the Board reversed. There was no single base divided five ways. There were several bases, built from common accounting information and then transformed by divergent legal rules.

This matters for how we think about relief. A foreign tax credit is calibrated to differences in rates. It cannot reach a disagreement about whether £100 of expenditure is deductible at all. The arbitrariness sat in the base, where no relief mechanism of that kind could touch it.

Conventions travel

The last finding is the one I did not expect. When Howitt came to consider public acquisition of the railway in 1945, he needed a way to divide financial responsibility between the governments concerned. One of the bases he reached for was the allocation of capital expenditure that had already acquired authority through the tax system.

A convention devised to answer an administrative question about income tax was being promoted into a proposed basis for apportioning ownership of the railway itself. Once a number is settled and repeated, it stops looking like a convention and starts looking like a fact, and it begins answering questions it was never built for.

Why this is not only history

The technology has changed; the structure has not. Wherever an integrated cross-border undertaking meets several administrations of unequal capacity and no binding allocation rule, the base will be divided by convention, and the conventions will be supplied by the taxpayer, because only the taxpayer can supply them. Three points follow for anyone working on cross-border infrastructure, transmission networks, submarine cable, or the digital services that pose the allocation problem in its purest modern form.

First, conventional allocation is not a failure state waiting to be replaced by better rules. It is what happens when a base is genuinely indivisible. The design question is which conventions, and settled by whom.

Second, the capacity to interrogate a taxpayer's accounting conventions is not the same capacity as computing a liability from them, and it matters more. An administration can be numerate, aggressive and well-advised, as Bechuanaland's was, and still lose on the ground where the conventions are actually tested.

Third, an appellate hierarchy is not a coordination mechanism. Reviewing each administration in isolation corrects errors within jurisdictions and does nothing about inconsistency between them. A system can possess an apex court, use it vigorously, and still have no means of reconciling the divergent bases its members compute.

Designing for the disputes that are brought, without asking which disputes cannot be brought at all, reproduces exactly the silence this case was for eighty years mistaken as consent.
